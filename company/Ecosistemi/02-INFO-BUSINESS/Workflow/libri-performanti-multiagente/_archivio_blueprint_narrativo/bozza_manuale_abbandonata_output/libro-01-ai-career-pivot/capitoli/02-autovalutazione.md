# Chapter 2: Before You Pivot

## Self-Assessment with AI as Your Thinking Partner

Most career-change advice starts with "figure out what you want to do." That's correct and almost useless as stated, because if you already knew clearly what you wanted to do, you'd likely be doing it. This chapter treats self-assessment as a process, not a moment of insight — a structured way of using AI to surface patterns in your own experience that are hard to see from the inside, because you're too close to them.

### Why Self-Assessment Is Harder Than It Sounds

Three things make honest self-assessment difficult without help:

**You're inside your own frame of reference.** The skills you use every day feel ordinary to you precisely because you use them every day. The thing you find easy is often the thing someone else would pay well for, and you routinely undervalue it because it doesn't feel like a "skill" — it feels like just doing your job.

**Career language is field-specific.** What you call "stakeholder alignment" in operations might be called "cross-functional communication" in tech, or "client management" in consulting. If you don't know the vocabulary of your target field, you can't see that you already have the underlying capability — you just don't recognize it under its new name.

**Self-assessment done alone tends to loop.** Left to your own thoughts, most people either overrate the parts of their job they enjoy or underrate the parts they've stopped noticing. A structured back-and-forth with a tool that asks follow-up questions and reflects patterns back to you breaks the loop in a way that journaling alone often doesn't.

AI tools are well-suited to all three problems: they've been trained on enough career and industry language to translate between fields, they can hold a long structured conversation without getting tired or bored, and — critically — they have no stake in your answer. They won't nudge you toward the career your family wants for you or away from the one that scares them. That neutrality is a genuine asset here.

### The Structured Self-Assessment Conversation

Rather than a single prompt, this works best as a conversation across several passes. Below is a sequence you can run in one sitting or spread across a few days — spreading it out often produces better results, because you'll notice things about your own answers between sessions.

**Pass 1: The Unfiltered Inventory**

Start broad, before trying to organize anything.

> *Prompt:* "I want to do a career self-assessment before considering a career change. I'm going to describe my current and past roles in detail. Don't try to organize or categorize anything yet — just ask me clarifying questions to help me be more specific and complete. Here's my background: [describe your work history, including responsibilities, projects, and anything you're proud of or found interesting, even if it seems minor]."

The instruction to ask clarifying questions rather than immediately summarize is deliberate. Most people, when asked to describe their work, give a compressed, resume-style answer that strips out the texture — the actual problem-solving, the moments something went sideways and you fixed it, the parts you found genuinely engaging. Clarifying questions pull that texture back out.

**Pass 2: Pattern Extraction**

Once you've given a full picture, ask the tool to find patterns rather than list skills.

> *Prompt:* "Based on everything I've told you, identify patterns — not a list of skills, but recurring themes in what kind of work energizes me, what kind of problems I gravitate toward solving, and what kind of environments I seem to do well in. Where do you see contradictions or tension between what I say I want and what my actual experience suggests I'm drawn to?"

That last question — asking explicitly for contradictions — is often the most useful part of this exercise. People frequently say they want more strategic work while describing years of evidence that they thrive on hands-on execution, or vice versa. A good AI assistant, prompted correctly, will surface that tension instead of politely ignoring it.

**Pass 3: Reframing for New Fields**

This is where translation happens.

> *Prompt:* "I'm considering a move into [target field or a short list of fields]. Based on the patterns we identified, translate my experience into the language and priorities of that field. What would someone hiring in that field recognize immediately from my background? What would they need explained or reframed? Be specific and honest — including where the translation is a stretch."

The phrase "including where the translation is a stretch" matters. Left unprompted, AI tools tend toward encouragement, which feels good and helps nobody. You want honest friction here, because the gaps this pass surfaces become your task list for Chapters 4 and 5.

### A Framework for Organizing What You Learn

After the conversation passes above, you'll have a lot of raw material. Organize it into four categories, which will be referenced again in Chapter 3:

1. **Core transferable skills** — capabilities that apply almost regardless of field (structured problem-solving, written communication, project coordination, data literacy).
2. **Domain knowledge** — specific to your current field, some of which transfers directly to adjacent fields and some of which doesn't transfer at all.
3. **Demonstrated interests** — patterns in what kind of work you sought out, volunteered for, or did well beyond what was required, even if it wasn't your formal job.
4. **Gaps** — things the target field visibly requires that your current profile doesn't yet show evidence of.

Write these four categories down in a single document. You'll return to it constantly through the rest of the book — it's the reference point for the resume rewrite in Chapter 6, the skill-mapping in Chapter 3, and the credibility-building work in Chapter 5.

### The Limits of This Exercise

A structured AI conversation will surface patterns you hadn't articulated, but it cannot tell you what you should want. Two people with nearly identical skill profiles can reasonably choose very different next careers based on values, risk tolerance, and what kind of life they want to build around the work — and none of that is visible to an AI system unless you tell it directly, and even then, weighing it is your call, not the model's.

It's also worth naming a failure mode directly: it's easy to use these conversations to build a case for whatever career you'd already decided on before you started, cherry-picking the parts of the AI's response that confirm it. The value of Pass 3's honesty prompt, and of asking explicitly for contradictions in Pass 2, is to work against that tendency. If every response you're getting feels flattering and none of it feels uncomfortable, you're probably not asking the right questions yet.

One practical safeguard: after you've done this exercise, share your four-category summary with one person who knows you well and one person who works in or near your target field, if you can find one. AI-assisted self-assessment is a strong starting point, not a replacement for a human gut check — this is one of the places in the process where a real conversation with a real person catches things a model simply cannot.

**Chapter 2 Action Steps**

1. Run the three-pass conversation above with ChatGPT or Claude, using your real work history. Do not skip the clarifying-questions step by front-loading a summary — let the tool pull detail out of you.
2. Build your four-category document: core transferable skills, domain knowledge, demonstrated interests, gaps. Keep it as a living document; you'll edit it through the rest of the book.
3. Identify one person who knows you well and, if possible, one person in or near your target field. Plan to share your summary with both before making any major decisions based on it.
