# A Woman Who Does Not Do That Any More

They came to Marged Pryce's door on the Wednesday evening, which told her before anyone opened their mouth that the parish council had spent an entire day working up to it.

There were three of them on her step. Councillor Beaton, who had put on a coat for the occasion. Reverend Attwood, who looked as though he would rather have been almost anywhere. And Gil Tanner from the garage, who had not been sent and had clearly come anyway, and who was the only one of the three Marged had any patience for.

"Marged," said Beaton.

"No," said Marged.

"You haven't heard what we've come about."

"I've lived here sixty one years, Alan. I've watched three of you stand on this step in a coat you don't wear to the shop. I know exactly what you've come about, and the answer is no, and you can tell the council I was very pleasant about it."

Reverend Attwood made a small noise that might have been a laugh trying to escape from under a professional obligation.

Beaton drew himself up. He was a decent enough man who had never in his life been told no by a woman on her own doorstep and had not developed a strategy for it.

"There's a crack across the green," he said. "It's grown. It was three quarters of an inch yesterday morning and it's better than two now, and the surveyor can't come until the eleventh, and there is a thing my mother used to say about this village and about your family that I did not believe when she said it and am no longer entirely certain about."

"Your mother," said Marged, "was a gossip and a snob and she wouldn't have crossed the road to say good morning to my Owen."

"She wasn't wrong about the well."

Marged Pryce stood in her doorway in a cardigan with a tissue up the sleeve and let ten full seconds go by.

"Goodnight, Alan," she said.

She closed the door on him, gently, which she considered a considerable act of restraint, and she stood in her own hallway with her hand still on the latch and her heart going far too hard for a woman of sixty one who had just declined to attend a meeting.

Through the frosted glass she watched the three shapes confer, and separate, and go. Gil's was the last to leave. He stood there a moment longer, and lifted a hand toward the glass, knowing perfectly well she was still standing behind it, and then went too.

---

The trouble was that she had already felt it.

She had felt it on Tuesday morning, before Gil ever walked over the thing, before Beaton drove down, before anybody in Lost Ember had a word for what had happened to the green. She had been standing at her own back step with the kettle going, looking at nothing, and something had gone through her from the soles of her feet to the crown of her head, quick and cold and entirely familiar, and she had put down the milk bottle she was holding rather than drop it.

Eleven years. Eleven years of not doing it, of building the habit of not doing it the way you build a wall, one small refusal cemented on top of another every single day, until you can walk past your own kitchen table without your hands wanting anything.

And then a crack in the ground three hundred yards away had opened, and her whole body had turned toward it like a plant toward a window.

It had happened on a Thursday in October, eleven years ago, in the field above the Ridley place, and the village had a version of it that Marged had never once corrected.

The village version went like this: Owen Pryce, who had never been the stronger of the two of them and everybody knew it, had taken on a working beyond himself out of pride, and his wife had been there and had not been able to stop him, and afterwards she had put it all down and never picked it up again, poor woman, and wasn't it a shame, and hadn't she been marvellous about it.

Every part of that was nearly true, which was what made it so serviceable.

Marged had let it stand at the funeral because at the funeral she had not been capable of standing up, let alone of correcting sixty people, and she had let it stand in the week after because the week after was a fog, and by the month after it had set like concrete and there was no longer a shape of hole in the story that a correction would fit into.

She made herself a cup of tea she did not drink. She washed up a plate that was already clean. She sat in Owen's chair, which she had not moved and had not stopped calling Owen's chair, and she picked up the crossword and got two clues and put it down again.

At ten past eleven she gave up and went to bed, and at half past twelve she was still lying on her back in the dark with her hands flat on the covers, refusing.

Because it was assembling.

That was the word for it and there had never been another one. A working did not arrive fully formed. It gathered, quietly, in the space behind the ribs, the way a sentence gathers before you speak it: a shape, an intention, a set of small readinesses in the hands and the breath. Marged had spent eleven years catching that gathering at the very first flicker and putting it out, every time, thousands of times, until it had mostly stopped starting.

Tonight it started, and it kept starting, and it did not care in the slightest that she was refusing.

She lay in the dark and felt a binding assemble itself behind her sternum, unbidden and unwelcome and beautifully competent, the way an old dancer's feet find a step. She felt her own hands go warm on the covers. She felt, with a clarity that frightened her far more than the crack in the green ever could, exactly how she would go about it: down to the well, the lid off, four points and the beck water and the long slow line that Owen used to call the spine.

*Stop,* she told herself. *You are a woman who does not do that any more.*

She got up at one in the morning and went downstairs and stood in her kitchen with the light off.

The truth, and she was old enough and tired enough to look straight at it in the dark, was that she had never been frightened of magic. Not once. Not the day she learned it at her grandmother's table, not the day it went wrong, not any day since. What she was frightened of was how much she still wanted it, and what she had proved she was willing to do while wanting it, and the fact that eleven years of refusing had not diminished either of those things by a single ounce.

The village thought she had given it up out of grief. The village had decided that at the funeral, and had been kind to her about it ever since, and she had let them.

Marged filled the kettle again and did not switch it on.

---

She went at half past five, before anyone was up, because she had never in her life been able to stand not knowing something she could go and find out.

The green in the grey light was empty and wet. The crack ran from the church wall to the road, straight as a ruled line, and it was wider than two inches now whatever Beaton's tape measure had said the day before, and Marged did not need to crouch down and hold her hand over it, because she could feel the warmth coming off it from six feet away.

She stood at the edge of it for a long moment.

Then she walked the length of it, slowly, from the wall to the road and back again, reading it the way her grandmother had taught her to read a thing on the ground, and at the halfway point she stopped, because the crack passed three feet east of Ember Well and at that point it bent.

Not much. A hand's width. But a line that had run dead straight for forty feet bent, once, going around the well, the way water goes around a stone.

*It isn't coming from the well,* Marged thought, with a cold clarity that arrived all at once. *It's going toward it.*

Something moved at the edge of her vision, and she turned, and there was a girl standing by the bench watching her.

The Quill girl. Seventeen, dark eyed, in a coat too big for her, standing at half past five in the morning in the wet with her hands jammed in her pockets as though she had been there some time.

They looked at each other across the green.

"You feel it and all," the girl said. It was not a question.

Marged Pryce, who had spent eleven years building a wall out of small daily refusals, heard herself say, before she could stop it: "How long have you been feeling it?"

And the girl said, "Eight years."
