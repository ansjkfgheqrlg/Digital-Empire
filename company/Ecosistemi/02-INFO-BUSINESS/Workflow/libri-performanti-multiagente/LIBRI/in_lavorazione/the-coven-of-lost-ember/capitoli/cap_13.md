# The Seam

They took the book back to Ada's, and Marged read the eleven words in the margin about forty times, and then went home without saying goodbye to anybody, which she regretted on the way and did not go back to fix.

*It will not knit where a thread is held back.*

She sat in her kitchen with the tin box still unopened on the table in front of her and the light off, and she took the sentence apart the way her grandmother had taught her to take a line apart, word by word, distrusting all of them.

*Knit.* Not bind, not seal, not hold. Knit was a mending word. You knitted bone. You knitted a wound. It was the word for a thing that closed itself, given the right conditions, and the tending was not a lid being screwed down, it was a dressing being put on so that the closing could happen. That fitted. That fitted everything Nessa had been saying for a week, and everything Marged had felt in the four minutes on the green, and it meant that the four of them had never been the thing doing the work at all. They were the dressing. The ground did the knitting.

*Thread.* Not person. Not witch. Not woman. The thread was the working, the long line she had laid herself twice now, and it ran through all four of them, and it was *one* thing and not four.

*Held back.* Not broken. Not weak. Not absent. Held back.

Marged sat in the dark with her hands flat on the table.

She had been sixty one years in this valley and eleven of them alone in this kitchen, and she had known within about ninety seconds of reading the sentence exactly what it meant and exactly what it was going to cost her, and she had gone home rather than say so, and she had been sitting here for two hours failing to be a person who could say so.

Because it did not mean *try harder.* It did not mean *practise.* It did not mean, God help them all, that the fault was Nessa's for putting her hands down or Bel's for being untaught.

It meant: the thread runs through four women, and one of them, or two, or all four, is holding a piece of herself out of it, and the working can feel that, and it will not knit over a lie.

---

She went to Ada's at ten past nine the next morning and all three of them were already there, which told her they had all worked it out too and had all spent the night in the same condition.

"Right," said Marged, before she had her coat off. "Say it, one of you, so that it isn't me."

Bel put down her cup.

"It's confession," she said. "That's what the joining is. It's not trust in the sense of I have decided to rely on you. It's trust in the sense of I have given you something you could destroy me with."

"Yes."

"And the working can tell the difference."

"The working," said Marged, "*is* the difference. That's what I couldn't see for eleven years, and I want to say that plainly in front of all of you, because you've each of you been very careful with me and it's not been useful." She sat down heavily. "A joining is not four people agreeing. It's four people opening. And what I have got in me, I have spent eleven years making absolutely certain that nobody in this valley ever sees, and that is not a preference, it's the entire architecture of my life."

Nobody spoke.

"So that's mine on the table," Marged said. "Not the thing itself. The shape of the thing. There is something, it is eleven years old, and it is the reason I stopped, and it is not what this village thinks it is."

Nessa, from the corner, said: "That's not the same as saying it."

"No," said Marged. "It is not. And you'll notice I haven't."

---

"There's a thing I want to say before anybody else speaks," said Nessa, "and it's not a confession, it's a question, and I want somebody to answer it honestly instead of managing me."

"Go on," said Ada.

"How does it know?" Nessa had her arms folded. "Everyone keeps saying the working can feel it. Feel it how? It's a hole in the ground and a line of words. How does a hole in the ground know that Marged hasn't told anybody something from eleven years ago?"

Marged opened her mouth and found she did not have a clean answer, which was itself instructive.

"It doesn't know," said Wren.

They all turned.

"That's the wrong picture and it's the one everybody starts with, including me at fourteen." Wren pulled her chair round. "There's nothing down there reading our minds. A joining is four people making one shape out of four. To do that, each of you has to put in what you actually are, not what you present. And a person who is holding something back does not put in what she actually is: she puts in a version, with a piece missing, and the piece missing is exactly the shape of the thing she is not saying."

"So it's not that it knows," said Nessa slowly. "It's that we come out the wrong shape."

"You come out the wrong shape, and four wrong shapes do not close on each other, and the thread finds the gap and unpicks along it. Every single time. In the same place, because it's the same gap." Wren spread her hands. "It's not a moral test. Nobody's being judged. It's a joinery problem."

Nessa sat back.

"That's the first time in a fortnight," she said, "that anybody's explained any of this to me like I'm a person who could understand it."

"Ask better questions and you get better answers," said Ada Fenwick. "That one was better than anything the four of us managed in a week."

It went badly after that, which Marged had expected.

Bel wanted a structure. Bel wanted them to go in an order, agreed in advance, with a rule about what happened afterward, and Marged watched a woman who had spent twenty one years in an institution try to build a procedure for the one thing that could not have one.

"There has to be some protection," Bel said. "You are asking four people who have known each other for eleven days to hand over the worst thing about themselves, and three of them have a legal interest in the fourth."

"Two of them," said Wren. "You've released me under caution."

"Which is not the same as the file being closed, and you know it isn't."

"Then close it."

"I don't have the authority to close it, I have the authority to be *ignored* by the people who do, which as of Monday is apparently absolute."

"That's not the point," said Nessa.

They both turned.

"You're doing the thing," Nessa said. "You're arguing about the rules of it because arguing about the rules is easier than doing it. My last school did this every time somebody had to say something real. There'd be twenty minutes about who's going to speak first and in what order and what happens after, and then the bell would go."

Marged, watching, thought: *she is seventeen and she is the only one in this room who has spent her whole life having to say true things to hostile adults, and it has made her the best of us at this.*

"All right," Bel said, after a moment. "Then I'll go."

Everybody looked at her.

"Not now," Bel said. "Tonight. At Ada's, with the four of us and Ada, because Ada writes things down and I would like it written down." She was very pale. "And I want to say in advance, so that none of you has to be tactful afterward, that what I am going to say will make it obvious that I should not have been given this case, and that I knew that, and that I took it anyway."

"Bel," said Wren.

"Don't be kind to me," said Bel Carrow, "I'm finding it very difficult," and went out into the lane to stand in the rain, which Marged understood entirely and did not follow her for.

---

Marged went home at noon.

The tin box was still on the kitchen table where she had put it on Tuesday. She stood in the doorway of her own kitchen with her coat on and looked at it for a long time, and then she crossed the room and put both hands flat on the lid, the way you put your hands on the shoulders of somebody you are about to tell something to.

Owen's mother's box. Owen's mother's before that.

*It will not knit where a thread is held back.*

"Two days," Marged said out loud, to an empty kitchen, and was appalled to hear how much her voice sounded like Wren Halloway's had, three days ago, asking for exactly the same thing.
