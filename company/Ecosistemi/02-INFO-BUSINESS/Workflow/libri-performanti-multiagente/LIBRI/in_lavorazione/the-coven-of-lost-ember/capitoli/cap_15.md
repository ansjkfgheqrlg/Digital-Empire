# The Sock at the Bottom of the Bag

Bel searched the room on the Friday morning, and she was honest with herself about it while she was doing it, which did not make it better and possibly made it worse.

There was no operational justification. She had released Wren under caution on Monday. The warrant was, in every sense that mattered, dead: her own organisation had told her the priority had changed and had then stopped answering her at all. There was no supervisor to authorise a search, no premises warrant, nothing.

She did it because Sheila had gone to the cash and carry, and because the key was on a hook behind the bar, and because Bel Carrow had spent twenty one years learning that a woman who will not answer a direct question four times is a woman whose room you search.

She did it because on Thursday night she had put her own worst thing on a kitchen table in front of three people, and Wren Halloway had watched her do it, and had said nothing.

She was in the room eleven minutes.

The canvas bag was under the bed, which was not hiding, it was simply where a bag goes. It held four shirts, a jumper, a wash bag, a phone charger with the cable taped at the join, two hundred and forty pounds in twenties in an envelope, and a paperback with a train ticket in it as a bookmark. Nothing else. No papers. No documents. It was the luggage of a person who had reduced her life to what she could carry at a run, and Bel, going through it, felt exactly as unpleasant as she deserved to.

At the bottom, under the flat cardboard base that the bag came with, there was a grey wool sock.

Bel had done this for twenty one years. She had found things in socks perhaps thirty times: money, phones, a wrap of something, once a set of car keys belonging to a man who had reported them stolen. She picked it up expecting weight and got it, more than she expected, dense and low, the weight of a thing much smaller than the sock suggested.

She unrolled it onto the bed.

It was a stone about the size of a large hen's egg, black, matte, perfectly smooth on four faces and rough on the fifth as though it had been broken from something. It was warm.

Not sun warm. Not body warm from the bag. It was warm in the specific way of a thing generating its own heat, and as Bel picked it up the floorboards under her boots gave a single long creak of the kind old buildings give when the temperature changes, and downstairs in the empty bar a glass rang once against another glass.

Bel stood in a rented room above a pub with a stone in her hand and her heart going like a hammer.

*Value substantial,* she thought. *Two years. No inventory.*

*This.*

---

She did it wrong, afterward. She knew that even at the time, and she never once tried to pretend otherwise, and it remained, for the rest of her life, the thing she would have gone back and changed.

The correct thing, the thing twenty one years should have produced, was to put the stone in her pocket and find Wren alone and ask her one question in a room with a door shut.

What Bel actually did was walk out of the Ram with the stone in her fist and find that Wren was on the green with Marged and Nessa, all three of them standing at the near end of the crack, and cross forty feet of wet grass with her arm already coming up.

"What is this?"

Wren turned.

Bel would remember the next second and a half for a very long time. She would remember that Wren looked at her face first, and then at her hand, and that the order of that was the whole story: she was checking Bel before she was checking the stone, because she had known for four days that this was coming and the only unresolved question had been how bad it would be.

"Bel," Wren said. "Put it down."

"Answer me."

"Put it *down*, on the grass, and step away from it."

"I have been carrying your file for two years," Bel said, and her voice was not steady any more, "and I have asked you what you took five separate times, and last night I put my sister on a table in front of you, and you sat there, and you had this under your bed."

"Bel."

"What is it?"

And Wren Halloway said, "It's the Ashmoor hearthstone, and you are holding it eleven feet from an open seal."

---

The ground answered.

That was the phrase all four of them used afterward, independently, when they came to describe it, and it was not a metaphor and nobody who was on the green that morning ever treated it as one.

It was a sound. It came up through the turf from directly below, low enough that Bel felt it in her sternum before she heard it, and it lasted perhaps three seconds, and it was not a rumble and it was not a crack and it did not resemble subsidence in any way whatever.

It had a shape. It rose at the end.

Nessa Quill made a noise that Bel had never heard a human being make, and went down on one knee in the grass with both hands over her ears, which did nothing at all, because the sound was not coming in through her ears.

The crack, along its entire length from the church wall to the road, opened by an inch and a half in three seconds.

"Put it down," Wren said again, and this time her voice was almost gentle. "Bel. On the grass. Slowly. It's answering you because you're holding it, that's all, it isn't doing anything else."

Bel crouched, very carefully, and set the stone on the grass, and stepped back three paces.

The sound stopped.

The four of them stood in a rough square around a black stone lying on a village green in the rain, and nobody moved for a long moment.

Behind them, up at the top of the lane, a car had stopped at the barrier. Bel registered it without turning: a red estate, engine running, and somebody in the driver's seat who had wound the window down and was looking at the four of them and at the ground.

Then the window went up and the car reversed away up the pass, badly, in three goes.

"That was Sharon Harrison," said Nessa, from the grass. Her voice was thick. "That's her car. She does the Thursday shop."

"Right," said Bel.

"She'll have heard it. It went right through my feet, she'll have heard it from up there with the engine on."

"Then by seven o'clock this evening," said Marged, "about a hundred people in this valley will have been told that the ground made a noise, and by tomorrow morning it will be four hundred, and I would like everybody here to understand that this is not necessarily bad."

"It's a disaster," said Bel.

"It is the opposite of a disaster." Marged had not taken her eyes off the stone. "For a fortnight the four of us have been the only people who could see this, which has meant that everything we do has had to be explained away afterwards. A valley that has heard it is a valley that will get out of the way when we tell it to."

Bel found she had nothing to say to that, largely because Marged was right and she had not got there herself.

"How long have you known it does that," Marged said.

"Since the second night I was here." Wren's face was white and entirely calm, which Bel recognised, because she had seen it on people who had rehearsed a moment so many times that the moment itself came as a relief. "It doesn't do it in Northumberland. It doesn't do it in a town. It does it here, because there's a failing seal under this green, and the closer it gets the louder they get with each other."

"And you brought it into this village," said Marged.

"Yes."

"Eleven days before the ground came open."

"Thirteen days," said Wren. "But yes."

Nessa was still on her knees in the grass. Bel went to her, and got an arm round her, and the girl was shaking so hard it was difficult to hold on to her.

"It's all right," Bel said, uselessly.

"It's not," Nessa said, into her shoulder. "It's not, it's not, because she *knew*, she's known the whole time, and I sat in a drain with her on Tuesday and she let me tell her everything and she knew."

And Bel Carrow, holding a seventeen year old girl on a wet village green with the crack a foot wide behind her and her own worst thing lying on a kitchen table two hundred yards away where she had put it the night before, found that the thing she felt was not anger at all.

It was recognition.

*She held one back,* Bel thought. *So did I, for two years. So did all of us. That's the whole of it. That's the seam.*
