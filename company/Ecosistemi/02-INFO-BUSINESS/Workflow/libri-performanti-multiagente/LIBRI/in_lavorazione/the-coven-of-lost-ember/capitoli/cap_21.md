# Ruth Sedgewick Comes to Lost Ember

Marged Pryce had expected, if she had ever imagined this moment at all, somebody frightening.

The woman on Ada Fenwick's step was about sixty, in a green wool coat that had been expensive fifteen years ago and had been looked after, with grey hair cut well and a canvas bag over one shoulder. She looked like a retired headmistress. She looked like the sort of woman who would stop and help you with a gate.

"Mrs. Fenwick," she said. "I'm sorry to come without ringing. My name is Ruth Sedgewick and I've driven from Northumberland, and I believe you have something of ours."

"You'd better come in," said Ada.

"Ada," said Marged.

"They're standing in my garden in front of the whole lane, Marged, and Bill Crozier is already at his window." Ada stood back from the door. "Just the one of you. The two young men can wait by the car and I'd be obliged if they didn't lean on my wall."

Ruth Sedgewick's mouth moved very slightly, and she said something over her shoulder, and came in alone.

---

She stopped in the kitchen doorway and looked at the four of them and the hearthstone on the tea towel, and something in her face eased, and that was the moment Marged stopped seeing a headmistress.

"Wren," Ruth said. "You look well."

"Don't."

"I mean it. You look considerably better than you did in the September before last, and I've thought about that a good deal." She set her bag down by the door, unhurried, and did not sit until Ada indicated a chair. "Thank you. I'll be brief, because I imagine you all have questions and I'd rather answer them than have you construct me."

"Then answer this one," said Bel. "Did you leave that stone out for her to take?"

"Yes," said Ruth Sedgewick.

The kitchen went very quiet.

"I want to be careful here," Ruth said, "because there is a version of this conversation where I am a monster and it will be satisfying for everybody and it will not be accurate, and an inaccurate account is no use to any of you." She folded her hands. "I left it out. I unlocked the long room. I told two people not to be in that corridor at four in the morning. Wren took it, exactly as we hoped, and left, exactly as we hoped, and I signed the request to the Assize eleven days later knowing perfectly well what the Assize would do with it."

"Which was nothing," said Bel.

"Which was to appoint one officer with a private reason to be thorough, and to require of her a report on the subject's location at regular intervals." Ruth looked at Bel directly for the first time. "You were extremely good, Warden Carrow. Forty three reports. Not one of them late."

Marged watched Bel's hands close on the edge of the table.

---

"Why," said Wren.

"Because the seals are failing," said Ruth Sedgewick, "across the whole of the north, and nobody knows which ones, and there has been no survey since nineteen fifty one."

She said it flatly, as a fact, and the flatness of it was worse than drama would have been.

"There were four hundred and six covens in this country in nineteen hundred," she said. "There are thirty one. Every one that stopped left behind whatever it was holding, and there is no register of what any of them were holding, because a coven's seal was a coven's business and nobody wrote it down anywhere central, because who would you have written it to." She turned her cup a quarter turn. "A hearthstone warms near a failing seal. It is the only instrument that exists. There are perhaps nine of them left in England and Ashmoor has one, and it cannot be carried by an Ashmoor witch, because the moment an Ashmoor witch walks into a village and starts warming up in the churchyard, the coven that still exists there wants to know why, and the Assize wants to know why, and it becomes a jurisdictional matter that takes four years."

"So you needed somebody who wasn't yours," said Marged.

"We needed somebody who was ours and had stopped being ours," said Ruth. "In a hurry, publicly, in a way nobody would question. And in the June before last a very good young witch stood up in a meeting in front of eleven people and asked whether anybody had ever gone back to Hartsett, and I sat there and I thought, *there she is.*"

Wren made a small sound.

"Nine weeks," said Ruth Sedgewick. "I took your students, I took your rotas, I told you that you looked tired, and I did every bit of it deliberately, and I did it in front of people who thought they were watching a woman come apart. And then I left a door unlocked." She did not look away. "It is the worst thing I have ever done to another person and I did it on purpose over two months, and I would like that said in a room with witnesses, because for two years I have only said it to myself."

---

"How many," said Marged.

Ruth Sedgewick looked up.

"How many villages. In two years. You said she walked eleven counties, so you have a number, and I would like it said in front of the woman who walked them."

"Forty one," said Ruth.

Nobody spoke.

"Forty one places where the stone went warm," she said. "Of those, nine were warm enough to be called failing, and of the nine, three have since had what the county records call ground movement. Hexham in April. A field wall near Kirkoswald in January. And here."

"And what did Ashmoor do," said Marged, "about the other six?"

Ruth Sedgewick did not answer immediately, and Marged watched her decide to give the true answer rather than the survivable one, which was, Marged thought, the only thing about the woman she would ever be able to respect.

"Nothing," said Ruth. "We produced a map. That was the commission. There was no second part."

"There are six villages in the north of England," said Marged slowly, "sitting on seals that you know are going, and nobody in any of them has been told."

"Yes."

"Does the list exist on paper?"

"It is in my bag by your door," said Ruth Sedgewick, "and I brought it for the same reason I brought the letters, and I would be grateful if somebody other than me had it by this evening."

"You could have asked her," said Nessa.

"Yes."

"You could have just *asked her.* She'd have gone. She'd have gone anywhere, she was asking the question, she *wanted* somebody to go back to Hartsett."

"Yes," said Ruth Sedgewick again. "I know. I have known that for about nineteen months."

"Then why didn't you?"

"Because a woman who volunteers can change her mind," said Ruth, "and a woman who is fleeing cannot, and I decided that the second was safer for the survey." She put her hands flat. "That is the answer. It is not a good answer. It is the true one."

Marged found that she had to look at the table for a moment.

"And the Assize," said Bel. Her voice had gone very quiet, which Marged had learned in a fortnight to find alarming. "You said you signed the request knowing what they'd do with it. That means you knew they'd give it to somebody like me and let it run for years without ever intending to close it."

"Yes."

"Why would they do that? Why would an enforcement body run a two year case it has no intention of finishing?"

"Warden Carrow," said Ruth Sedgewick, "who do you think asked us for the survey?"

---

Bel Carrow stood up so fast that her chair went over.

Nobody picked it up. It lay on Ada Fenwick's kitchen floor for the next eleven minutes.

"Say that again."

"The Assize commissioned it," said Ruth. "In the spring of two thousand and twenty three, informally, through a man called Hollis, and the arrangement was that Ashmoor would produce a map of the failing seals in the north and the Assize would not look too closely at how the map was made." She met Bel's eyes and did not flinch. "I have the correspondence. Two letters and a note of a telephone call, in my bag, by the door. I brought them because I have spent nineteen months trying to decide what I am, and I have concluded that I am the person who did this and also the only person who can prove who asked for it."

"Hartsett," said Bel.

"Hartsett," Ruth agreed, "was not part of the survey. Hartsett was before. Hartsett is the reason there is a survey."

"My sister died at Hartsett."

"I know," said Ruth Sedgewick. "That is also why the Assize gave you that file. A grieving sister is not merely persistent, Warden. She is *credible.* If anybody had ever asked what the Assize was doing about the missing property of a Northumberland coven, there would have been an officer with an exemplary record and an obvious personal motive, and no one would have looked any further."

Bel Carrow stood in the middle of Ada Fenwick's kitchen with a chair on the floor behind her.

Then she said, in a voice with absolutely nothing in it: "Ruth Sedgewick, I'm arresting you on suspicion of conspiracy to endanger life, and of perverting a lawful process, and of a third thing I will name properly when I have had ten minutes to look it up."

"You have no authority," said Ruth. "You were told to secure the site and await reassignment, and you have been out of contact for eleven days."

"I know," said Bel.

"I'm not objecting," said Ruth Sedgewick. "I'm telling you so that you know what it will cost you, and so that nobody can say afterwards that I let you do it without warning."

She put her wrists together on the table, which was not necessary and which nobody had asked for, and Marged Pryce looked at that and understood that it was not surrender.

It was a receipt.
