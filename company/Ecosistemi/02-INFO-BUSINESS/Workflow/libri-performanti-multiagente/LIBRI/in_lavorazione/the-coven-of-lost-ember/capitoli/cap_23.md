# The Binding

They worked it at ten past one in the afternoon, in daylight, in front of about sixty people.

That was not a decision either. Bel had sent two of Ashmoor's young men round the doors to clear the buildings on the green side, and what a village does when you knock on its door and tell it to come out is come out and then stand at a distance and watch. By one o'clock there were sixty of Lost Ember's four hundred and six standing along the churchyard wall and up the lane behind the barrier, in coats, in the middle of a Thursday, saying almost nothing.

Wren had expected to mind. She found she did not mind at all.

"They should see it," Ada said, from her chair. She had refused to be moved further back. "Twenty six women did this three times a year for thirty two years and nobody ever watched, and I have always thought that was half the reason it stopped."

---

The ground had changed, as Ruth had said it would.

Marged walked it first, slowly, with the eighteen thirty nine book open in both hands and Nessa beside her holding the copy, and it took her eleven minutes to set the four points, and none of them were where they had been.

The near point was no longer near the well. It was six feet the other side of it, on ground that had risen very slightly, and Marged stood there twice and moved twice before she was satisfied.

"Here," she said at last. "And I want to say, so it's said in front of everybody, that I am setting these by feel and not by measurement, and that the last time I did that a man went through a wall."

"Set them," said Bel.

"I'm setting them."

Wren took the far point, which put her back to the church and the crack directly in front of her at its widest. Nessa and Bel took the two together on the eastern side.

"Hands," said Marged. "Down, six inches, and Nessa."

"I know."

"I'm going to say it anyway because I'd like it heard by sixty people. Nessa, whatever happens, your hands do not touch the ground."

"My hands do not touch the ground," said Nessa Quill, clearly, to the whole green.

---

Marged began.

She read the opening from the older book and it was different from the copy, rougher, less tidy, and about a third longer. The places first: the beck, the pass, the four fields, the church. Then the names, all twenty six of them, read slowly off the black ledger, ending as it always ended.

*Annie Ridley, who kept it alone and poorly, and was not to blame.*

And then, in the eighteen thirty nine book and in no copy since, four more lines that Marged read out for the first time in a hundred and fourteen years, and that were not an incantation at all but a set of instructions to the four people standing in the circle. Wren heard them and understood, in the way you understand something you have known for twenty years without having words for it, exactly what a coven had originally been.

*Say what you have not said.*
*Do not say it to the ground, say it to the three.*
*The ground is listening and will know.*
*Then lay the thread.*

"Right," said Marged Pryce. "In turn. And quickly, because I can feel it going."

---

They said them again, out loud, on a village green, in front of sixty people.

Ada Fenwick, from a kitchen chair: four months, and it is September, and I gave you the tidy book because I have spent fifty one years writing things down instead of saying them.

Marged: I laid the line wrong and Owen had eleven minutes and he spent them protecting me and I let this village believe it was him.

Nessa, in a voice that carried further than she expected: I knew four days early and I said nothing because for four days I mattered. It talks to me most nights. And there have been nights when I wanted it to come up, because it is the only thing that has ever wanted me.

Somebody on the churchyard wall made a noise. Sharon Harrison, Wren thought, and did not look.

Bel: I requested this posting. I hoped for another Hartsett. And you sat at a table three nights ago and let me say it and you knew, and I have not forgiven you, and I am standing here anyway.

And Wren Halloway, at the far point, with her back to a church that was dropping courses:

"I was at Hartsett. I walked it with a stone for nine days and I told them where the seal was thinnest and two days later eleven people died. I don't know if I caused it. I have never known. And I stayed in this village for a fortnight knowing the ground was following me, and I told myself I stayed because I wasn't sure which way it went, and that was true, and it was also because for the first time in fifteen years somebody had asked me to stay."

Nobody said anything.

"Lay it," said Ada.

---

Marged laid the thread.

It caught in four seconds, which was quicker than either previous attempt, and Wren felt it come up through the soles of her boots and take hold of her the way it had at Ashmoor a hundred times and had never once done here.

And it held.

That was the whole of it, and she was never afterward able to make it sound like anything. It held. There was no seam. She could feel the other three the way you feel people in a dark room, and there was nothing between them: no drag from the west, no half step off from the north, no small held-back weight from her own point. The shape closed on itself and stayed closed and began, without any of them doing anything further, to pull.

Under the green, the crack began to draw in.

It did it slowly, the way a wound knits, from both ends toward the middle. Wren watched the near end come off the road and the far end come away from the church wall, and heard sixty people make a single collective noise she would remember for the rest of her life, and felt Nessa Quill on her left going like a struck bell and holding, holding, hands six inches off the grass.

It took nineteen minutes.

At about the eleventh, when the gap was down to a hand's width, the thing underneath came up.

---

Wren had no word for it then and did not acquire one later, and neither did anybody else on that green, which is exactly why it mattered.

It was not a shape. It did not have edges. What came up out of the closing seam over the last eight minutes was something between a sound and a change in the light, an enormous slow pressure that rose out of the ground and stood over the green and did not move, and every person present felt it look at them, and not one of them was afraid.

That was the part the county could never do anything with afterward.

Sixty people. Sixty statements, taken over eleven days, and no two of them agreed on what it looked like, and all sixty of them agreed on that.

Nessa was crying and laughing at once and saying something that nobody could hear.

And then it went back down, without hurry, the way a thing goes back to sleep, and the last hand's width of the crack closed over it with a sound like a door in another room, and the green was grass.

---

Wren stayed on her feet for about four seconds after it closed, and then found that she was sitting on the grass without any memory of having decided to.

The thread had let go of all four of them at once, cleanly, the way a working does when it has finished rather than when it has broken, and the difference between those two sensations was something she had been taught at fifteen and had not felt since. She sat on a village green with her hands open on the grass and understood that she was crying and that it was not distress.

Nessa was on her knees six feet away with both palms flat on the turf, which she had not been told she could do and which nobody was going to say a word about now.

"It's quiet," Nessa said.

"Is that bad?" said Bel, immediately, because Bel had been listening in Ada's kitchen when Wren explained what quiet meant.

"No." Nessa's face came up, wet and completely open. "No, it's not that quiet. It's the other one. It's like." She laughed, and it came out wrecked. "It's like somebody's stopped shouting through a wall because you finally opened the door and now they're just sitting in the room."

Somewhere behind them along the churchyard wall, sixty people were making a noise that was not cheering and was not quite anything else.

Marged Pryce sat down where she was standing.

Nobody spoke for a long moment. Somewhere behind them, in the crowd along the wall, a woman was crying and somebody else was saying *did you see it, did you see it* over and over.

Ada Fenwick, in a kitchen chair in the middle of a village green, opened the black ledger on her knees, and unscrewed the cap of a fountain pen, and wrote in a hand that had gone very shaky:

*4.ix. M. Pryce, B. Carrow, W. Halloway, N. Quill. Kept.*

She looked at it for a moment.

Then she added, underneath, in the same hand, the only annotation anybody had made in that column in a hundred and fifty one years:

*and seen, by sixty.*
