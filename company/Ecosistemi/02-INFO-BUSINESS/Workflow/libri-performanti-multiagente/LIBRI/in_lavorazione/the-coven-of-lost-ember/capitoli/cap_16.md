# What Wren Took

They took it to Ada's, because by then everything went to Ada's, and because none of them could think of anywhere else in Lost Ember where four women could stand round a table with a stone on it at eleven in the morning and not be interrupted.

Ada put a tea towel down first. Nessa noticed that and never forgot it: a seventy nine year old woman folding a tea towel in half and setting it on her own kitchen table before anybody would put the thing down, the way you would for a hot pan.

The hearthstone sat on the towel. It looked like nothing. That was the part Nessa could not get past. It looked exactly like a stone you would pick up on the beck and put in your pocket and forget about.

"Right," said Marged. "From the beginning, and don't leave anything out to be kind."

So Wren told them.

---

She had been six when they took her to Ashmoor. Her mother had been at Ashmoor and had died there, of something ordinary, and there had been nobody else, and the coven had absorbed her the way a coven absorbs a child, which is to say completely and without ceremony and with a great deal of practical kindness that nobody ever called love.

She had been taught from nine. She had been good at it from eleven. She had been given the hearthstone to hold, the first time, at fourteen, in a room with six other people, and had been told that this was an honour, and had believed it, and had been right to: it was.

"A hearthstone isn't a weapon," she said. "I need you to understand that before I say the rest, because otherwise you'll build the wrong thing in your head and everything after will look worse than it is. It's an anchor. Every coven that holds a seal keeps one. It's where the working ties off. Ours had been Ashmoor's for two hundred years and it had been in that house the entire time and it had never once left the building."

"Until you took it," said Bel.

"Until I took it."

"Why?"

And Wren Halloway said, "Because it was the only thing I had that they wanted," and something in the kitchen changed shape.

---

She laid it out flat, without arranging it, which Nessa recognised because it was how Bel had done it two nights ago and how Nessa herself had done it a hundred times in rooms with adults holding forms.

In the June before last she had asked a question at Ashmoor, in a meeting, in front of eleven people. It had been a small question about a working she had done in the spring, and it had been the wrong question, and she had known it was the wrong question within about four seconds of the room's reaction.

Nobody had shouted at her. That was not how Ashmoor worked.

What happened instead was that over the following nine weeks she was moved off everything. Not dramatically. Her name simply stopped appearing on rotas. Her students were reassigned, one at a time, each with a reason that made sense on its own. She was told she looked tired. She was asked, twice, in a caring voice, whether she had considered that she might be under strain.

"By the September I couldn't tell any more whether I was right about the thing I'd asked or whether I was ill," Wren said. "That's what it does. That's the entire mechanism. Nobody has to lie to you. They just keep being concerned about you until you can't find the floor."

"And then you left."

"And then I took the stone off the shelf in the long room at four in the morning on the eleventh of October, and I walked out of the front door, and nobody stopped me." She looked at it on the tea towel. "I took it because if I'd left with nothing they'd have let me go and I'd have spent the rest of my life wondering if I'd imagined all of it. I took it so that they'd have to come after me. I wanted to be worth coming after."

Nessa felt that land in her chest with a physical weight.

"And they came," said Marged.

"They filed a warrant with the Assize inside a week and then they never came at all," said Wren. "That's the part I've had two years to think about, and I've thought about very little else, and I could not make it fit. Ashmoor doesn't lose things. Ashmoor has recovered property from three counties in my lifetime and it has never once used the Assize to do it, because using the Assize means letting somebody outside look at your books."

Bel had gone very still at the end of the table.

"And instead," Wren said, "they gave a file with no inventory in it to one officer with a personal reason to be dogged, and let her follow me across eleven counties for two years filing a report every eleven days."

---

"Say the rest," said Marged.

Wren did not answer immediately.

"The crack follows me," she said. "I've known since the second night. I got here on the ninth, the ground came open on the twenty third. It isn't the first time. In April there was a subsidence in a village outside Hexham eight days after I passed through, in the county record as a landslip, nobody hurt. In January it was a field wall in Cumbria. I told myself both times it was coincidence, because the alternative was unbearable, and then I stood on that green two weeks ago and felt it answer the thing in my bag and I stopped being able to tell myself anything at all."

"You stayed," said Nessa. "You knew and you stayed."

"I stayed because I couldn't work out whether leaving would take it with me or leave it here." Wren's voice cracked for the first time. "That's the truth. Not courage. I genuinely do not know which way it goes, and I still don't, and every single day I've been here I've been sitting with the possibility that the most useful thing I could do for four hundred people is walk out of the valley and never be traced."

"And that's what you're going to do," said Marged.

It was not a question.

"Marged," said Bel.

"No." Marged Pryce stood up from the table. She was not shouting. Nessa would remember that too. "I have listened to all of it and I believe every word and I am not going to pretend that changes the arithmetic. There is a stone on that towel that makes the ground under this village talk out loud, and it came here in her bag, and there are four hundred people in this valley including a nine year old girl whose father is in Kendal with a plate in his skull because of what we did on that green."

"That was me," said Nessa. "That was my hands, not hers."

"It was all of us, and it was mostly me, and that is a separate conversation." Marged did not look away from Wren. "I am asking you to take it out of Lost Ember. Today. That is the whole of what I'm asking, and I'd like it on the record that I'm asking it without any anger at all, because you have not lied to us since about eleven o'clock this morning and I don't think you lied much before."

---

Wren went at two in the morning.

Nessa knew because Nessa was awake, and because Nessa had spent eight years learning to hear a door in a building where she was not supposed to be listening, and because at four minutes past two she heard the side door of the Ram and got out of bed and went to the window in the Harrisons' box room and saw her on the lane with the canvas bag and her coat collar up.

She was down the stairs and out of the back door in under a minute, in a coat over pyjamas, and caught her at the bottom of the churchyard wall.

"Don't."

Wren stopped.

"Don't go, I'm asking you, I've never asked anybody for anything." Nessa's voice was doing something she could not control. "Everyone leaves. That's the whole of my life, that's literally the entire structure of it, and every single one of them had a reason and every single one of the reasons was good."

"Nessa."

"You sat in a drain with me." She was crying now and furious about it. "You went down there and you cried and you were the first one, you were the only one in eight years, and now you're doing it at two in the morning so you don't have to see my face."

Wren Halloway stood in the dark at the bottom of the churchyard wall with a bag on her shoulder, and for a long moment she did not say anything at all.

"I told you," she said finally, "in the culvert, that in three days you'd find out I'd been lying to all of you. Do you remember what I said I wanted?"

"That there'd be one thing on the other side of the scale."

"Yes." Wren's hand came up, briefly, and touched the side of Nessa's head, once, the way you would touch something to check it was really there. "It's still true. What I told you down there is still true, and it's yours, and it doesn't go anywhere when I do."

Then she went down the lane past the barrier and the diversion sign, and Nessa Quill stood in the churchyard in her pyjamas and watched her go, and behind her, under the green, the ground made a sound like something turning over in its sleep.
