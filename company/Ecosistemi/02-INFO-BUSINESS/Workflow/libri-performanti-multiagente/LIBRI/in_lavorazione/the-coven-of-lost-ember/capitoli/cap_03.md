# The Warden

Bel Carrow came over the top of the pass at four in the afternoon with the file on the passenger seat and two years of her life in it.

The valley opened below her the way these valleys did, all at once, a long green trough between two shoulders of fell with a village at the bottom of it like something dropped. Slate roofs. A church tower. A road in and, according to the map, no road out that a sensible person would use in October.

She pulled into the lay by at the top and sat with the engine off for a while, which was a habit she had picked up somewhere around the eighth village and had never managed to lose. You looked at a place before you went into it. You got its shape in your head while it was still a thing on a hillside and not a set of people with faces.

Lost Ember, population four hundred and six at the last count. One pub, one shop, one garage. No Assize presence within forty miles. No registered practitioner since 1961.

And a woman in the room above the pub who had been running for two years and one month.

Bel opened the file, not because she needed to, but because opening it was part of the ritual by now. HALLOWAY, WREN ELIZABETH. Twenty eight. Last confirmed sighting in a market town in Northumberland in June. The photograph was four years old and had been taken for something else entirely, a coven registration document, and in it Wren Halloway was looking slightly off camera with an expression of somebody about to be told off for something minor.

Under the photograph, in the box marked GROUNDS, someone at Ashmoor had typed: *Unauthorised removal of coven property. Value substantial. Coven requests recovery.*

Two years of Bel's life for a theft charge nobody had ever properly specified.

She had asked, once, in the second month, what exactly had been taken, and had been told by Ruth Sedgewick in a voice like a closing door that it was coven business. She had asked her own superior, Hollis, and Hollis had said that the Assize did not require an inventory to act on a member coven's request, and had said it in the tone that meant the conversation was finished and had been finished for some time.

Bel had gone on chasing her anyway, for two years, through eleven counties.

She was honest enough with herself, most days, about why.

---

Iris had been thirty one. She had been three years into a posting at a village called Hartsett, in a valley not unlike this one, doing the unglamorous rural work that the Assize gave to officers it had decided were reliable rather than promising. On the eleventh of October 2011 the lane above the village had subsided and taken four buildings and eleven people with it, and Iris Carrow had been in the second of them.

The Assize had called it a ground collapse. There had been an inquiry, of a sort. Bel had read the report eleven times, and had noticed, on about the fourth reading, that the report described the subsidence as *irregular in form* and then never once returned to the point.

She had asked to see the site survey. She had been told there had not been one.

She had asked why an Assize officer had been in Hartsett at all, given there was no registered coven within thirty miles, and had been told that officers were posted where officers were needed.

And then she had been given the Halloway file, in the month after the inquiry closed, and had been told it would be good for her to have something in front of her, and she had taken it, and had been grateful, and had spent two years discovering that a file you are given to keep you busy is a very difficult thing to put down.

The village had the particular hush of a place where something had happened and had then stopped happening. She parked by the church, got out, and stood a moment. It was the last of the light. Somebody was burning garden waste on the far side of the beck and the smell of it hung in the cold.

There were people on the green.

Not many, four or five, standing around in that loose way people stand around a hole. Bel walked over with her hands in her coat pockets, unhurried, doing the thing she did, which was to arrive somewhere as a woman in a coat rather than as a warden, because a warrant card changed a village's face in under a second and you never got the first face back.

She looked down at the crack.

She had been to Hartsett. She had been to Hartsett eleven days after it happened and had stood on what was left of the lane and had looked at the shape of it in the ground, and she had never afterward been able to unsee that shape.

This was the same shape.

It was smaller. It was very much earlier. But it was the same, a straight line where straight lines do not occur in earth, running from a stone wall to a road, warm to within a foot of it, and Bel Carrow stood on the green at Lost Ember with the smell of a bonfire in her nose and felt the whole architecture of the last two years shift about four degrees under her.

"You want to be careful how close you stand," said a voice.

Bel looked up.

The woman was on the far side of the crack, maybe fifteen feet away, in a thin coat and a wool hat, with a canvas bag over her shoulder. She had come out of nowhere the way people did in villages, from between two cottages, and she was standing there looking at Bel with an expression Bel recognised because she had seen it in an interview room a hundred times.

She had also seen it in a four year old photograph roughly once a week for two years.

Neither of them moved.

Bel became aware of a number of things in a very short space of time. That Wren Halloway was standing eleven feet from her with a packed bag on her shoulder. That the road out of the valley went past Bel's car, and that Bel had left the keys in her coat pocket, which was correct. That there were four members of the public within thirty feet of them. And that Wren Halloway was not looking at Bel's face, or at Bel's hands, or at the road.

She was looking at the crack.

"How long has it been open?" Wren said.

That was not the question of a woman about to run. Bel heard it, and something in her adjusted.

"Two days," Bel said. "By the village's count. It was three quarters of an inch on Tuesday morning."

"It's two and a half now."

"You've measured it."

"I've watched it." Wren's jaw worked. She shifted the bag on her shoulder, and Bel watched her do it, and understood suddenly that the bag was not packed for the bus. It was packed for a woman who had been sleeping with her bag packed for two years, which was a thing Bel knew about, because she had spent those same two years sleeping in hotels beside a case file.

"You know what I am," Bel said.

"I've known since you got out of the car." Wren still had not looked away from the ground. "You're Assize. You've got the coat and you've got the walk and you looked at the church tower before you looked at any of the people. There's only one file open on me, so you're Carrow."

"I am."

"Right." A long breath. "Then I'd like to ask you something before you do the part where you say the words at me, and I'd like you to think about it honestly, because I've got no reason to lie to you and neither of us has time."

Bel said nothing, which Wren correctly took for permission.

"Have you seen one of these before?"

The green was very quiet. Somewhere behind them a car went past on the top road.

Bel Carrow had rehearsed the arrest of Wren Halloway perhaps four hundred times. She had rehearsed it in the car, in hotel rooms, in the shower, in the long empty stretches of the A66. In not one single version of it had she stood on a village green in the last of the light and answered a question.

"Yes," she said. "Once."

"Where?"

"A village called Hartsett," said Bel. "In two thousand and eleven."

And she watched the colour go out of Wren Halloway's face in a way that no photograph in any file had ever prepared her for, and she thought, with a slow cold turning of the stomach, *she knows that name, and she should not know that name, and I have been chasing the wrong thing for two years.*

Neither of them ran.

That was the part Bel came back to afterward, the thing she would tell the others in Ada Fenwick's kitchen much later when it was her turn to be honest. There was a crack in the ground between them, and a warrant in her pocket, and a woman with a packed bag on the other side, and neither of them ran, because both of them had just understood that the other one could see it.
