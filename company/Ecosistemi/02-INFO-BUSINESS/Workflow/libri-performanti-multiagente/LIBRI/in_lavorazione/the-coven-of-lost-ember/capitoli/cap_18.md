# The Older Book

Bel got there at twenty past six, which was eleven minutes after Ada telephoned, and she got there before Marged because Marged had stopped to put clothes on and Bel had not.

Wren Halloway was at the kitchen table with a mug she had not touched, and Bel stood in the doorway of Ada Fenwick's kitchen and looked at her, and every single thing she had rehearsed on the way down the lane went out of her head.

"You came back," she said.

"Twelve miles," said Wren. "The stone went cold."

"What?"

"Sit down," said Ada, "you as well, and neither of you say another word until Marged's here, because I am not having this told twice in my own kitchen."

---

Marged came at half past with Nessa, who had been collected on the way and who had not been asked whether she wanted to be, and who came into the kitchen and saw Wren and stopped dead in the doorway with an expression that Bel found she had to look away from.

"You left," Nessa said.

"I got twelve miles."

"You left at *two in the morning.*"

"Yes," said Wren. "And I was wrong, and I'm going to tell you why in about a minute, and then you can decide what you think of me, but you'll be deciding it with the actual facts, which is more than I had at two."

Ada put the pot on the table. "Now," she said.

And Wren told them about the stone going cold.

---

Bel listened to all of it with her hands flat on the table, which was a thing she had started doing at some point in the last fortnight and had not noticed.

An anchor does not act. It is warm when it is near a seal. Which means it had not been making cracks across eleven counties. It had been **finding** them.

"It's a detector," Marged said slowly.

"It's a detector," Wren agreed. "That's all it ever was. And they gave it to me, and they let me carry it, and they had a report on my location every eleven days from an officer who thought she was tracking a thief."

The kitchen was quiet.

"They were surveying," said Bel. Her own voice sounded distant to her. "Eleven counties. Two years. They were using you and me to build a map of every failing seal in the north of England."

"Yes."

"Why."

"That," said Wren, "is the part I don't have. I've got what happens. I haven't got what it's for."

---

It was Ada Fenwick who got up.

She did it without saying anything, and went out of the kitchen, and they heard her go slowly up the stairs, and be some time, and come slowly down again, and when she came back into the kitchen she was carrying something wrapped in a pillowcase and she was walking differently.

"Ada," said Marged.

"Sit down, I'm perfectly all right." She was not. Bel watched her set the bundle on the table and have to put one hand on the back of a chair. "I've had three of these on this table in my life and I've put two of them away again, and I told you that a fortnight ago and I want it understood that I wasn't being dramatic. I was being a coward, and I'd like to stop."

She unwrapped it.

It was a book, and it was in pieces. The spine had gone entirely; what was left was a block of pages held between two boards by a strip of linen tape that somebody had put on a very long time ago and that had itself gone brittle. The paper was the colour of weak tea.

"Eighteen thirty nine," Ada said. "It's the one the marbled book was copied from in nineteen eleven, when they capped the well and decided everything should be written up properly. The copy is the one everybody's read for a hundred and fourteen years. This is the one it came from."

"You've had this the whole time," said Bel.

"I've had it since nineteen seventy four and I've read it perhaps six times and I have never once shown it to anybody, and I am going to tell you why in a moment because it matters." Ada opened it, very carefully, with two fingers under the block. "But first you're going to read a line."

She turned it round.

The hand was different from the copperplate in the marbled book: older, browner, harder to read, the long s still in use in places. Ada put her finger under a line about a third of the way down a page and Bel leaned over and read it, and then read it again.

*the Tendinge at Lost Ember. to be wroughte by fower who have tolde eache other true.*

Bel sat back.

"Read it out," said Marged, who could not see from where she was.

"To be worked by four," Bel said, "who have told each other true."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

"The copy says *joined in trust*," said Wren.

"The copy says joined in trust," Ada agreed, "and I have thought about that particular piece of tidying up for fifty one years. Some Victorian in eighteen ninety or nineteen ten sat down with this book and a clean sheet and he came to that line, and he did not like it. It's not decent, is it. *Told each other true.* It sounds like a confession box. So he wrote *joined in trust*, which sounds like a wedding, and everybody has read that instead ever since."

"And the working can't tell the difference between a wedding and a confession," said Marged.

"The working," said Ada, "cannot be lied to. That's what the whole page is about, if you read the rest of it, and I'll spare you the spelling. It says the tending fails where a thing is *kepte backe*. It says it twice. It says that the four should be chosen for what they are willing to say and not for what they can do."

Bel put her hand over her mouth.

"Two attempts," she said. "Nine seconds and four minutes eleven. And we spent a fortnight thinking we were bad at it."

---

"Why didn't you show us this," said Nessa.

It came from the corner, flat and hard, and Ada Fenwick turned to face it properly, which Bel thought took some doing.

"Because of what it asks," Ada said. "Not of you four. Of me."

She sat down.

"The tending book has twenty six names in it and the last one is Annie Ridley, nineteen forty four, *kept, alone, poorly*. And there's a page after that in this one" she touched the block of pages "which is not in the copy at all, because the man doing the copying in nineteen eleven left it out, and I've always assumed he left it out for the same reason he changed the line about telling true."

"What's on it?"

"A list of everyone who was asked and said no." Ada's hands were flat on the table now, the same posture Bel had adopted without knowing where she got it. "It goes back to seventeen ninety. Sixty one names. And in nineteen seventy four, when my mother gave me this book and told me what it was, she showed me that page and said: *you can't do it, Ada, you've not got a scrap of it in you, so your job is the book. But when it comes open, and it will, you go and find the four, and you don't wait to be certain, because the ones on that list all waited to be certain.*"

She stopped, and the kitchen was very quiet.

"The ground came open on the twenty third of August," Ada said. "You four were in this room on the twenty eighth. I had this book upstairs the entire time and I gave you the copy, because the copy is tidier and because I have spent fifty one years being the woman who writes things down and not the woman who says them."

"Ada," said Marged.

"I'm not finished, and I'd rather do it in one go." Ada Fenwick took her glasses off. "I've something wrong with me. I've known since the May. It's the sort where they tell you a number of months and the number was four, and it's September."

Nobody moved.

"So that's mine," said Ada, "and it's on the table, and I've just noticed that I've done the thing the book asks and I did it without meaning to, which is probably how it works for most people." She put her glasses back on. "Now. Which of you is going next."
