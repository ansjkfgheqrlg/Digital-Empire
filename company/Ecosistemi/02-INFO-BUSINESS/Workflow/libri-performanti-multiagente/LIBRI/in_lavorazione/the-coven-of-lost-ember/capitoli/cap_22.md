# Wren Says It

The green went at twenty past eleven.

They heard it from Ada's kitchen: not a crash, a long low sound like a hull settling, and then the small hard noise of slate coming off a roof somewhere down the lane, and then Bill Crozier shouting for his wife.

Bel got to the door first. The crack had opened to the width of a man's shoulders along its whole length and the north wall of the church had dropped a course, and the barrier at the bottom of the lane was standing at an angle it had not been standing at an hour ago.

"Everybody out of the buildings on the green side," she said to Ruth Sedgewick's two young men, who were standing by the car looking at the ground with the expression of people who had understood something. "Now. You two, go and knock on doors. Crozier's, the Harrisons', the Ram. Nobody in a building with a wall facing that."

They went without arguing, which told her a good deal about what they had just felt.

She turned back and Wren was standing in the doorway with the cold hearthstone in her hand.

"Now," Wren said. "It has to be now, because in about an hour there won't be a green to stand on."

---

They did it in the lane, which was the wrong place for it and the only one available: Ada on a kitchen chair somebody had carried out, Marged and Nessa on the wall, Ruth Sedgewick standing to one side with her hands still together, and Bel in the middle of the road.

"Say it," said Bel.

"I was at Hartsett," said Wren.

The road was very quiet.

"In two thousand and eleven. In October. I was twenty two." Wren's voice was flat and clear and she did not look away from Bel for one second of the next ninety. "There were four of us from Ashmoor. Ruth wasn't there. It was called an assessment. We were told a seal in the valley above the village had been unheld since the forties and that Ashmoor had been asked to survey it and report."

"By whom?"

"We weren't told. I was twenty two, Bel, nobody tells you that at twenty two, you get told the county and the date."

"Go on."

"We were there nine days. I carried the stone." Wren's hand closed on it. "That was my job. I walked the lane and the fields and the churchyard twice a day with a stone in a bag and I said where it was warm and where it wasn't, and somebody wrote it down, and on the eighth day I said it was warmest at the top of the lane above the village and they wrote that down too. And on the ninth day we left."

"And two days later the lane went," said Bel.

"Two days later the lane went. Eleven people." Wren's jaw was rigid. "And I found out in a meeting, six weeks afterward, when Ruth stood up and said the ground in that valley had always been poor."

"Did you do anything to it? Did you work anything at Hartsett?"

"No."

"Did anybody?"

"I don't know," said Wren. "That's the true answer and it's the one I've had for fifteen years. I was one of four and I only ever saw my own part. I walked with a stone and I said warm or cold. I don't know what the other three did in the evenings. I have never known. And for three years I told myself that meant I hadn't done anything, and then I stopped being able to tell myself that."

"Why?"

"Because a survey doesn't need nine days," said Wren. "I've thought about it for fifteen years and that's what I keep coming back to. You can walk a village that size with a hearthstone in an afternoon. I did this one in two hours. We were there nine days, and I have no account of what happened in seven of them, and I was the one who told them exactly where the seal was thinnest."

---

Bel Carrow stood in the middle of the lane.

Marged, watching from the wall, would say afterward that she had never in her life seen a person so still while so much was happening to them.

"You told them where it was thinnest," Bel said.

"Yes."

"And then it went, at the thinnest point, two days after you left."

"Yes."

"And you have carried that for fifteen years and you did not tell me." Bel's voice did not rise at all. "You sat at Ada Fenwick's table three nights ago and you watched me put my sister on it. You watched me say that I had wanted another village to come open. And you knew."

"Yes," said Wren Halloway. "I knew, and I said nothing, and there is no version of that I can make defensible, and I'm not going to try."

Bel closed her eyes.

The green made the sound again, further off, under the church.

---

Afterward Marged would try to work out how long it actually took, and would decide it could not have been more than five or six seconds, and would never quite believe that.

What she could see, from the wall, was a woman standing in a lane with her eyes shut and both hands open at her sides, and a church behind her dropping stone, and sixty seconds' worth of village noise going on somewhere out of frame: a door, a dog, somebody calling a child in.

What Bel was doing in those seconds, and told Marged much later on a different evening, was this.

She was not deciding whether to forgive Wren Halloway. That was not available and she knew it was not available and she did not try. What she was doing was holding two things at once and finding out whether a person could.

The first was Iris at thirty one, three years into a posting in a valley like this one, in the second building.

The second was a girl of twenty two walking a lane twice a day with a bag, saying warm and cold, because that was the job and because at twenty two you have been taught since nine that when you are told to carry something you carry it.

And what she found, in the fifth or sixth second, was that she could hold both, and that she had in fact been holding both since Ruth Sedgewick sat down in Ada Fenwick's kitchen and said *who do you think asked us for the survey.* She had simply not been willing to look at the second one, because looking at it meant putting her sister's death down somewhere she could not pick it up again and carry it at somebody.

Bel opened her eyes.

"Right," she said. "Two things, and then we work."

"Bel," said Wren.

"Be quiet, I'm doing this once." Bel Carrow's hands were shaking and her voice was not. "First. You did not kill my sister. Four people were sent to that village by somebody who wanted a seal opened, and you were the twenty two year old they gave the bag to, and if you had refused they would have handed it to the next one down the corridor and Iris would still be dead. I have spent five days learning what my own organisation does to people who ask questions and I am not going to sit here and pretend you were the one with the choice."

"I told them where it was thinnest."

"You told them what a stone in your hand was telling you, and you told the truth, which is what you had been trained since the age of nine to do." Bel's mouth twisted. "The people who took that number and used it are the ones who did it. One of them is standing by that wall with her hands together and the other one is in an office in Carlisle and I am going to spend the rest of my career on him."

She stopped, and breathed, and the second thing came out much harder.

"Second. What you did to me at that table three nights ago was cruel, and I am not going to say it wasn't, and I am not going to be talked out of it in six weeks when everybody's being kind." Her eyes were streaming and her voice stayed level. "You let me open my chest in front of you and you sat there. And I am telling you that in front of four witnesses because the book says the thing has to be *said*, not resolved, and I would rather stand in that circle with this said out loud and unfinished than pretend I've forgiven something I haven't got to yet."

"That's fair," said Wren.

"It is not fair, it's just true, which is what we're apparently doing." Bel wiped her face roughly with the heel of her hand. "Marged. Ada. Nessa. Everybody's said one?"

"Everybody's said one," said Ada Fenwick from her kitchen chair in the middle of the lane.

"Then we do it now, on the green, while there's a green." Bel bent and picked up her coat from the wall. "Ruth Sedgewick, you're going to sit in that car with your hands where I can see them and you are not going to say one word to any of us, and when this is finished you and I are going to have a very long afternoon."

"Warden," said Ruth. "The four points on the eighteen thirty nine diagram are set *as the ground gives*. Your ground has changed since the last attempt. Don't use the old marks."

Bel looked at her for a moment.

"Thank you," she said, with enormous coldness, and went to get the book.
