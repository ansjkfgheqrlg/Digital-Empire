# A Coven Again

Ada Fenwick died on the nineteenth of October, six weeks and a day after the green closed, in her own bed, having spent the last four of those weeks being extremely tiresome about the parish records.

She had left instructions. Of course she had left instructions. They were in an envelope in the front board of the eighteen thirty nine book, behind Marged's folded sheet of paper, and they ran to two sides and included a note about which hymn absolutely not to use and why.

The church was full. Nessa had not expected that, and then had felt stupid for not expecting it: the woman had kept the records of four hundred people for fifty one years, and there was almost nobody in that valley whose birth or marriage or father's death had not passed through her handwriting.

Reverend Attwood read the instructions out, most of them, including the bit about the hymn, and the church laughed, which Nessa understood was the entire reason Ada had written it that way.

---

Afterwards, in the hall, over the ham, Marged Pryce stood up on a chair.

"Ada left one more thing," she said, "and she was very clear that it wasn't to be done at the graveside, because she said graveside decisions never stick."

She had the box under one arm: the hinged wooden box with the three books in it, the black ledger and the marbled copy and the eighteen thirty nine block held together with linen tape.

"The parish records of Lost Ember have been kept by a Fenwick since eighteen ninety one," Marged said. "There isn't another Fenwick. Ada picked somebody, and she picked her in July, before any of this started, and she wrote it down and had me witness it, so nobody's to say it was decided in the excitement."

Nessa, who had been standing at the back by the urn as she had stood at the back of every gathering in this village for seventeen years, understood about half a second before it happened and had nowhere at all to put her face.

"Nessa Quill," said Marged Pryce, to a hall of two hundred people. "Come and take the box."

---

She was eighteen by then. Sharon Harrison had done a cake in September and eleven people had come, which was ten more than the year before.

The county had come and gone. There had been sixty statements and an inspector from Kendal and a structural engineer who spent three days on the green and wrote eleven pages that concluded, in effect, that the ground had moved and then had stopped moving. Nobody had been able to do anything with sixty people describing the same thing in sixty different ways, and after a while the file had gone somewhere and stopped.

The other file had not.

Bel Carrow had resigned from the Assize on the twelfth of September, in writing, in four sentences, having first sent copies of Ruth Sedgewick's two letters and the note of the telephone call to three places that were not the Assize. What happened to Warden Hollis took fourteen months and was still going on, and Bel described it, on the rare occasions anybody asked, as *slow*.

She had not gone back to Northumberland. She had taken the flat over the shop, which had been empty since 2019, and had got a job doing something dull with the National Park that she was substantially overqualified for, and when Sharon Harrison asked her at the funeral how long she was stopping, Bel said "I've bought a bed" in a tone that closed the subject.

Ruth Sedgewick was in Carlisle, cooperating, comprehensively and without being asked twice. Nessa had not decided what she thought about that and had stopped feeling she had to.

---

Gil Tanner came to the funeral on a stick.

He had been out of Kendal since the end of September. He had a plate in his skull and a headache that came most afternoons and a nine year old daughter called Ruby who had been told a version of events that Nessa privately thought was closer to the truth than the one the adults were using.

He came over to the four of them by the tea urn, slowly, and stood there.

"I've been meaning to say something for six weeks and I've made a hash of it four times," he said, "so I'm going to do it badly now and get it over with."

"Gil," said Marged.

"No. You lot put me in a wall." He said it flatly. "That's true and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, and there was a fortnight in Kendal where I was very angry about it." He shifted his weight on the stick. "And then on the fourth of September I was on that wall with everybody else and I watched what came up out of that green, and I've had six weeks to think about what was under my house."

Nobody said anything.

"So." He put out his hand, and Nessa, who was nearest, took it. "Ruby wants to meet the one who sits against the door. I've told her that's what you are. I hope that's all right."

---

The four of them had one difficult evening in the middle of October, and Nessa thought afterwards that it was probably the most important one.

It was at Marged's, and it started because Bel asked, in the flat way she asked things, what exactly they were now.

"We're four people who did a thing in September," said Wren.

"That's what we were in September. What are we in March?"

"Are we obliged to be anything?"

"Yes," said Marged, from the range. "That's the entire content of the black ledger and I'd have thought a fortnight of reading it would have got that across. Twenty six women, three times a year, for thirty two years. Nobody ever asked them what they were. They were the ones whose turn it was."

"That's a rota, not a relationship," said Bel.

"It is a rota," said Marged, "and I want to be honest with the three of you about something, because it is apparently what we do now. I have spent eleven years being the woman this village does not ask questions of, and the last six weeks have been the least lonely of my adult life, and I am frightened that if we call it a friendship it will turn out not to be one, and if we call it a rota it will hold."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Then we call it a rota," said Nessa.

"Nessa."

"No, she's right, and I'd rather have the rota." Nessa had her hands round a mug. "I've had four families call themselves a family at me. Not one of them wrote anything down. Ada wrote everything down and she picked me in July and put it in an envelope, and that's why I believe it." She looked up. "Put us in the book. Three dates a year. If it turns into the other thing it can turn into it on its own."

Bel Carrow, who had resigned from a twenty one year career eleven days earlier and had told nobody how frightened she was about it, said: "Yes. That."

They lifted the lid on the first Sunday of November.

It was Ada's date, one of the three: the tenth of March, the twenty second of June, the fifth of November. Close enough.

It took all four of them and a crowbar and eleven minutes, because the lid had not been off since 1911 and had a hundred and fourteen years of moss and grit in the seam, and when it came up the smell that came out of Ember Well was cold stone and water and, faintly, underneath, something warm.

They looked in.

Nessa would never tell anybody what she saw down there, and neither would the other three, and in this at least the four of them were in complete and permanent agreement without ever having discussed it.

What she did do, afterwards, was sit on the wall with the black ledger open on her knee and a fountain pen that had been Ada's, and write, in a hand that was nothing like Ada's and that would take about forty years to get anywhere near as good:

*5.xi. M. Pryce, B. Carrow, W. Halloway, N. Quill. Kept.*

She blew on it, which Ada had told her not to do.

"That's it?" said Wren, looking over her shoulder. "That's all you write?"

"That's all anybody's ever written." Nessa closed the book. "Twenty six women, a hundred and fifty one years. Nobody once wrote *did it well.* You just write kept."

Wren Halloway sat down on the wall beside her.

She had stayed. There had been a fortnight in September when it was genuinely not certain, and there had been one very bad night in October when the county inspector was still asking questions and Wren had had a bag by the door again, and Bel had knocked at half eleven and sat on the end of the bed and neither of them had said anything about forgiveness, which was still not finished and might take years, and both of them had understood that showing up at half eleven was a different thing and was available now.

"March, then," Wren said.

"March," said Nessa.

Below them the green lay flat and ordinary in the November light, grass and a bench and a capped well with the moss knocked off the rim, and there was nothing whatever to see, and there had not been for four hundred and six people since the fourth of September.

Marged called up from the path that the tea was going cold, in the voice of a woman who had made tea for four people in her kitchen twice a week since the summer and had stopped pretending she did not like it.

"Coming," said Nessa Quill, and put the box under her arm, and went.
