# Bel's File

She brought the actual file, which none of them had expected.

It was a grey cardboard wallet, worn soft at the corners and thickened with two years of additions, and Bel Carrow put it on Ada Fenwick's kitchen table at eight o'clock on the Thursday evening and sat down in front of it with her hands flat on either side, and Wren thought: *she has carried that in a car boot across eleven counties looking for me, and now it is on a table between us, and I do not think she has ever once opened it in front of another human being.*

Ada had the parish notebook out and a pen. Nobody had asked her to. Nobody suggested she put it away.

"I'll do it in order," Bel said, "because if I don't I'll arrange it, and if I arrange it it'll come out better than it was."

She opened the wallet.

"My sister was Iris Carrow. She was three years older than me. She joined the Assize in ninety eight, two years before I did, and if you had asked anybody at that time which of us would still be in it at fifty they would have said her, and they would have been wrong for reasons I've never fully understood." A photograph came out and went face up on the table: two women on a hillside, squinting, one of them unmistakably a younger Bel. "She was posted to a village called Hartsett in two thousand and eight. Rural liaison. It's the work they give you when they think you're steady and not brilliant, and she was both, and it annoyed her enormously."

"How long was she there?" Marged asked.

"Three years, one month." Bel did not look up. "On the eleventh of October two thousand and eleven the lane above the village subsided. Four buildings. Eleven people. She was in the second one."

Nobody said anything. Ada's pen moved.

"The Assize called it a ground collapse. There was an inquiry. I have read the report of that inquiry eleven times and I could recite you most of it." Another paper out of the wallet, photocopied, a page with a paragraph ringed in biro. "On the fourth reading I noticed this. *The subsidence was irregular in form.* Four words. And then the report never returns to it. Not once, in thirty one pages."

"Irregular in form," said Wren.

"It means it was straight," said Bel. "Everybody in this room knows what it means. It took me two years to let myself know it."

She laid out the rest of it methodically, the way she must have laid out a hundred cases for a hundred hearings. She had asked for the site survey. There had not been one. She had asked why an Assize officer had been posted for three years to a village with no registered coven within thirty miles, and had been told officers went where they were needed. She had asked, twice more, in writing, and had received, twice more, a form of words.

And then, in November of 2023, she had been given the Halloway file.

"I want to be exact about this part," Bel said, "because it's the part that matters and it's the part I have lied to myself about." She put both hands flat again. "They did not force it on me. Hollis called me in and said there was an open coven matter that needed somebody who'd stick at it, and he said it would be *good for me to have something in front of me*, and I understood that to be kindness. I have understood it to be kindness for two years."

"It wasn't," said Nessa.

"No." Bel's mouth did something complicated. "It was a leash and a compass, and I was both ends of it. I filed forty three reports. They were acknowledged within two working days, every time, which is faster than the Assize acknowledges anything, and I never once asked myself why an organisation that could not process a grade three equipment requisition in under six weeks was so extremely prompt about my movements."

"You were being kept close," Marged said.

"I was being *read.* And she" Bel nodded at Wren without looking at her "was being followed, and I was the thing doing the following, and I worked that out on Monday afternoon sitting in a car in the rain, and I have not slept properly since."

She stopped.

"There's one more thing," she said. "And this is the one I would have gone to my grave with."

Wren found that she had stopped breathing.

"I requested this posting." Bel said it flatly, into the middle of the table. "In August. I saw a duty circular about ground movement at a village in this valley, unregistered coven history, and I put in for a two week rural assessment, and I attached the Halloway file to the request to make it look like enforcement work, because a two week assessment on its own would have been refused."

"You came here looking for another Hartsett," said Marged.

"I came here *hoping* for one." Bel's voice cracked, once, and she took a moment. "That's the sentence. I have been hoping, for two years, that it would happen somewhere else. Not to eleven people. Not to four hundred. I never once let myself put a number on it, because the moment you put a number on it you have to look at what you're hoping for. But I wanted another one, because another one would prove the first one was not an accident, and I have wanted that more than I have wanted almost anything, and then on Tuesday afternoon I stood on that green and I got it."

The kitchen was completely silent except for Ada's pen, which had stopped.

"And Gil Tanner is in Kendal with a plate in his head," Bel said, "and I did not put him there, but I have to say out loud in front of the three of you that when I first saw that crack, before I thought about the village or the girl or any of it, for about two seconds, what I felt was *relief*, and I would like somebody to write that down."

Ada Fenwick wrote it down.

---

Nobody spoke for a while after that. It was Nessa who moved first, which surprised none of them by then; she got up and went round the table and put a hand on Bel's shoulder from behind, briefly, awkwardly, the way somebody does who has not had many opportunities to practise, and then went and stood by the window.

"Right," said Marged eventually. "That was properly done."

"It was appalling."

"It was properly done," Marged repeated, "and I've listened to a great many people say difficult things in this valley and most of them arrange it first. You didn't." She pulled her cardigan straight. "And now everybody in this room knows something about you that could finish you, and that is precisely the point of the exercise, and I would like it noted that I am *dreading* my turn."

"Marged," said Bel. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet, I haven't done it."

But it was Wren, standing with her back against Ada's dresser, who was doing the arithmetic none of them could see. Because Bel had said *irregular in form*, and Bel had said *eleven people*, and Bel had said *I wanted another one*, and Wren Halloway had been standing four hundred yards from that lane in Hartsett on the eleventh of October 2011 with a hearthstone in a canvas bag, twenty two years old, doing exactly what she had been told.
