# The Tuesday It Started

Nessa Quill woke at three in the morning with her jaw locked and the taste of pennies flooding the back of her mouth, and lay very still in the dark of the box room at the Harrisons', counting the ceiling cracks she could not see, waiting for it to pass.

It did not pass. That was the first thing that was different.

She had been having the tooth nights since she was nine. That was what she called them, privately, because calling them anything else would have meant admitting they were not about teeth. They came two or three times a year, always in autumn, always between two and four in the morning: a pressure in the roots of her back molars, a sourness on her tongue, and under both of those a low sensation that she had never once managed to describe to herself properly, though the closest she had ever got was *something turning over in its sleep, a long way down.*

They lasted twenty minutes. She would lie still, and breathe, and after twenty minutes it would ease and she would sleep and wake up ordinary.

At half past four she was still awake, and her hands had started to hum.

She got up. The Harrisons' house was quiet in the particular way of a foster placement at four in the morning, which is to say that Nessa knew exactly which two floorboards on the landing would announce her and stepped over both without thinking about it, the way she had learned to in four houses before this one. Sharon Harrison was a decent woman who had never once raised her voice, and Nessa had every intention of not being the reason she started.

In the kitchen she ran the cold tap over her wrists until the humming dulled, which was a trick she had worked out at eleven and had never told a doctor about, because at eleven she had already understood what happened to children who told doctors things like that.

Through the window over the sink she could see a slice of the village green, silver under the one working street lamp, and the black bulk of the church tower behind it, and the fells rising past that into a sky with no stars in it at all.

The green looked wrong.

She stood at the sink for a long time trying to work out how. It was too dark to see anything properly. The grass was grass. The bench was where the bench had always been. The well sat at the far edge with its round stone lid and its ring of moss, exactly as it had sat there her entire life and, according to Mrs. Fenwick, for a good deal longer than that.

And still, looking at it made the roots of her teeth ache, and the humming came back into her hands so strongly that she gripped the edge of the sink with both of them and made herself breathe out slowly through her nose until it stopped.

*Something turning over in its sleep.*

Except now, at four thirty in the morning on the last Tuesday in September, it did not feel asleep at all.

---

By nine the whole village knew about the crack, because Gil Tanner had walked over it on his way to open the garage and had nearly put his foot in it.

It ran the length of the green, roughly north to south, from the low wall by the church path to the edge of the road. At its widest it was about the breadth of a man's thumb. At its narrowest it was a black hairline in the turf that you could have missed entirely if you were not looking. What made it strange, and what half the village stood around discussing over the course of the morning, was that it did not wander the way a crack in dry ground wanders. It went straight. It went straight the way a drawn line goes straight, from the church wall to the road, passing three feet to the east of Ember Well.

"Drought," said Councillor Beaton, who had driven down specially. "Ground's been shrinking since June. You'll see this all over the county."

"We had two inches of rain a fortnight ago," said Gil.

"Subsoil," said Councillor Beaton, with the confidence of a man who had heard the word recently.

Somebody photographed it with a phone. Somebody else fetched a tape measure, and the number they got, three quarters of an inch at the widest point, was written down in the parish notebook by Ada Fenwick, who had been keeping the parish notebook since before Councillor Beaton was born and who did not say anything at all while she wrote it.

Nessa watched the whole thing from the wall outside the shop with a bacon roll going cold in her hand.

She was good at watching. It was the skill you developed when you were the girl who had been left at a church door, in a village where four hundred people all knew that about you and had known it for seventeen years, and where every single one of them was perfectly kind to your face and nobody ever quite forgot. She knew how to stand at the edge of a group without joining it. She knew how to hear things people did not think she was close enough to hear.

She heard Ada Fenwick say, quietly, to nobody: "Well. There it is, then."

---

She went back at dusk, when everyone had lost interest.

The crack was wider. Not dramatically. But it had been three quarters of an inch that morning and it was more than an inch now, and Nessa knew that because she had brought Sharon's tape measure in her coat pocket and had come specifically to check, and had felt ridiculous the whole walk over, and did not feel ridiculous any more.

She crouched at the edge of it with the measure in her hand and looked in.

There was nothing to see. It was a crack in the ground, a couple of inches deep as far as she could tell, packed earth and root and a bit of gravel. It looked like exactly what Councillor Beaton had said it was.

Warm air came out of it.

Nessa held her palm flat over the gap and felt it, unmistakable, a slow steady exhalation of air noticeably warmer than the September evening around her, rising out of the ground into her hand. She held it there for a full minute to be sure. Then she moved along the crack four feet and held her hand over it again and felt the same thing, and again at the far end near the road, and it was warm along its entire length.

The ground under Lost Ember was breathing out.

Her hands were humming so hard now that her fingers had gone half numb, and the ache in her jaw had come back, and she understood in some part of herself that had no words in it that she was not frightened, and that this was the worst thing about the whole situation. She was seventeen years old and she was crouched over a hole in the earth that was breathing, and what she felt, mostly, underneath everything, was a kind of terrible relief.

*It's real,* she thought. *After eight years. There it is, and I'm not mad, and it's real.*

Behind her, from the direction of the pub, a door banged.

Nessa was on her feet and moving before she had decided to be, because being caught crouched over the crack with a tape measure was exactly the kind of thing that got a girl talked about in a village this size. She got as far as the bench.

The woman coming out of the Ram's side door had a canvas bag over one shoulder and was walking fast with her head down, and she stopped dead in the middle of the path when she got level with the crack, so suddenly that her bag swung forward and hit her hip.

She stood there. She did not look down at it the way the village had looked down at it all morning, with the faintly pleased curiosity people have about small local disasters. She looked at it the way you look at a dog you have already been bitten by.

Then she crouched, exactly as Nessa had done, and held her hand flat over the gap.

Nessa watched the woman's shoulders go rigid.

She was young, or youngish, maybe twenty five or thirty, with dark hair shoved up under a wool hat and a coat too thin for the valley in September. Nessa had seen her twice in the shop and once at the bus stop in the eleven days since she had turned up. Nobody knew anything about her except that she had taken the room above the Ram and paid for a fortnight in cash, which in Lost Ember was itself a full biography.

The woman stayed crouched for perhaps ten seconds. Then she stood, and turned, and looked straight at Nessa across forty feet of dark green, with an expression Nessa could not read at all.

Neither of them said anything.

Then the woman walked away very quickly toward the pub, and Nessa heard the side door bang shut behind her, and a light came on in the upstairs window, and stayed on.

Nessa stood by the bench in the cold with the tape measure in her fist.

Upstairs at the Ram, she thought, that woman is packing.

She did not know how she knew it. She knew it the same way she had known, four days ago, lying awake with her jaw aching, that something was coming: completely, without evidence, and with a certainty that had never once in eight years turned out to be wrong.
