# Twelve Miles of Road

The first bus out of the valley was the six forty from the top of the pass, and Wren had four hours to fill, and she filled them by walking, because walking was what she had instead of a plan.

The road climbed out of Lost Ember in three long bends and then ran along the shoulder of the fell for six miles with nothing on either side but drystone wall and dark. She had done this eleven times in two years, in eleven different valleys, and there was a rhythm to it that she had come to rely on more than she liked to admit: the first mile was always the worst, the second was grief, and by the third the thing in her chest went quiet and she became, for a few hours, simply a person walking on a road.

The third mile did not do it this time.

She noticed at about four o'clock, on the long flat stretch above the reservoir, that she was still going over the kitchen at Ada's, and Marged saying *I'm asking without any anger at all*, which had been true and had been so much worse than anger, and Nessa in her pyjamas at the churchyard wall.

She put her hand in her coat pocket, which she had been doing every few minutes since the village, the way you check for a wallet.

The stone was cold.

---

She stopped in the middle of the road.

It had never been cold. In two years it had never once been cold. It had been warm in a canvas bag in a room above a pub in Northumberland and warm in the bottom of a rucksack on a bus to Carlisle and warm through two layers of wool in a bothy in February when Wren herself had been so cold she could not do up a zip.

She took it out. It sat in her palm in the dark, matte and black and perfectly inert, and it was the temperature of the air.

Wren Halloway stood on an empty road eleven miles above Lost Ember at four in the morning and worked it out, and it took her about ninety seconds, and every one of those seconds was worse than the one before.

*It isn't the stone.*

The stone had never been doing anything. A hearthstone is an anchor. An anchor does not act. It sits where it is put and gives a working somewhere to tie off, and it had been warm for two years because for two years it had been near something, and it had stopped being warm at, she checked her watch, somewhere in the last twenty minutes, which was roughly twelve miles.

*It's warm when it's near a seal.*

And a village outside Hexham had come open eight days after she passed through it. And a field wall in Cumbria in January. And she had carried this thing in a bag across eleven counties for two years, filing a location report every eleven days through a woman who did not know she was filing it.

Wren sat down on the wall at the side of the road because her legs had stopped being reliable.

---

She made herself do it properly, out loud, in the dark, because she had learned at Ashmoor that a thing you only think stays flexible and a thing you say stays said.

"They knew I'd take it."

She had asked a question in June in front of eleven people. Nine weeks of being moved off rotas. Being told she looked tired. The stone left on a shelf in the long room, in a house where nothing was ever left anywhere, and the long room unlocked at four in the morning on the eleventh of October in a building where every internal door was locked at eleven.

She had not stolen it. She had been given a door and a reason, and she had walked through it carrying exactly what they wanted carried, and she had spent two years believing she was the one who had done something.

"And they didn't come after me because they didn't need to."

Because the Assize had a file with no inventory in it, and one officer, and a report every eleven days. Ashmoor never had to follow her at all. Ashmoor got a map.

Wren put her head in her hands on a drystone wall above a reservoir.

*Eleventh of October.*

She had taken the stone off the shelf on the eleventh of October two years ago.

And Bel Carrow's sister had died at Hartsett on the eleventh of October, three years before that, in a lane that went in a straight line and took eleven people.

---

She was moving again before she had finished the thought, back down the road, at a pace that turned into a run somewhere on the second bend.

Because there was one more piece and it was the one she had been not-looking-at for two years the way you do not look at a thing in a room you have decided not to enter.

In 2011 Wren Halloway had been twenty two years old, and she had been at Hartsett.

She had been sent. There had been four of them, and it had been described as an assessment, and she had carried a bag from Ashmoor to a village she had never heard of and had sat in a car for six hours doing what she was told, and eleven days later there had been a subsidence and eleven people had died and Ruth Sedgewick had said, in a meeting, that it was a tragedy and that the ground in that valley had always been poor.

Wren had been twenty two. She had believed her. She had believed her for three years, and then she had stopped believing her and had not known what to do with that, and then in June she had asked a question in front of eleven people.

*That's the question I asked.*

She stopped again, four miles above the village, with her hands on her knees and her breath going white.

She had asked whether anybody had ever gone back to Hartsett.

That was it. That was the whole question, the one that had cost her nine weeks of being told she looked tired. She had asked whether anybody had ever gone back and looked at the ground.

And nine weeks later a two hundred year old anchor stone had been left out on a shelf in an unlocked room, in the middle of the night, in a house where nothing was ever left anywhere.

---

She stopped once more, at the cattle grid a mile above the village, and did the last piece of arithmetic, the one she had been running from since the reservoir.

If the stone was a detector and not a cause, then everything she had believed about herself for two years was wrong in the direction of mercy: she had not cracked Hexham, or the wall in Cumbria, or Lost Ember. She had walked into three places that were already failing and the stone had gone warm.

And if that was true, then the crack under the green had not started when she arrived on the ninth of August.

It had started in 1944, when Annie Ridley wrote *kept, alone, poorly* and then died in the February, and four women were not there on the third Sunday, and nobody went.

Eighty one years of nobody going. And a stone in a bag had simply been the first thing in eight decades that noticed.

Wren stood at the cattle grid in the dark and found that this did not make her feel better in the slightest, and worked out why, and it was this: she had spent two years believing she was dangerous, and being dangerous is at least a kind of importance. What she actually was, on the evidence, was a woman who had been used as an instrument and had then walked out of two villages that were quietly falling in.

*I could have said something at Hexham,* she thought. *I could have knocked on one door.*

She had not, because she had been running, and because a woman who is running does not knock on doors.

The light was coming up grey behind the fell when she came down the last bend and saw the roofs, and the church tower, and the barrier across the lane with its diversion sign, and the green.

She could see the crack from half a mile away.

It ran the length of the green and it was glowing, not dully now but properly, a low steady orange that lit the underside of the mist lying over the grass, and she could see it from the road at first light, which meant anybody in Lost Ember who happened to look out of a window could see it too.

Wren came down into the village at ten to six in the morning with the cold stone in her pocket and went straight past the Ram and past the church and up the path to Ada Fenwick's door, and knocked, and stood there shaking.

Ada opened it in a dressing gown, immediately, as though she had been sitting in the hall.

"I wondered," Ada said, "whether you'd get as far as the reservoir or all the way to the top."

Wren opened her mouth and found that nothing came out.

"Twelve miles, was it." Ada stood back from the door. "Come in. I'll put the kettle on and then I'm telephoning the other three, and while it boils you can tell me the thing you've worked out, because you've the face of somebody who's worked something out and has decided it makes her the worst person in England."

"Ada."

"Sit down first."

Wren sat down at the kitchen table where the hearthstone had been on a folded tea towel nineteen hours earlier, and put her hands flat on the wood, and said:

"They sent me. Both times. And I need to tell all four of you at once, and one of you is going to have to decide something in about a second and a half, and it isn't fair, and I'm going to do it anyway because there isn't time to be fair."
