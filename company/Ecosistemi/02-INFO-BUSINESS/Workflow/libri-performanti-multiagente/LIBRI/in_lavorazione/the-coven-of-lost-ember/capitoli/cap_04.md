# What Ada Knows

The back room of Ada Fenwick's cottage had one window, four walls of shelving, and a hundred and fifty one years of Lost Ember in it, and Wren Halloway stood in the doorway of it at nine o'clock on the Thursday morning and felt the whole thing press against her like weather.

"Mind the step down," Ada said, going ahead of her. "Everybody trips on it once and then never again. I've thought about mending it and decided it does a useful job."

Wren minded the step. Behind her, Bel Carrow did not, and swore under her breath, and Ada said "There you are" with enormous satisfaction.

It should not have been possible to be here. Wren had run the calculation eleven times in the last fifteen hours, lying awake in the room above the Ram with her bag by the door and a warden of the Assize sleeping in a cottage two hundred yards away, and it came out the same every time: she should have been on the six forty bus. She had money. She had the stone. She had eleven weeks of practice at exactly this.

She had not gone, and the reason she had not gone was standing at the end of the room with her hands in her coat pockets pretending to read the spines of the parish registers, and the reason was one word.

*Hartsett.*

"Now then," said Ada, and lowered herself into the chair at the little table with the care of a woman who had learned not to trust her own hips. "You'll want the tending book. Not the registers, everybody always wants the registers and they're no use to anybody but genealogists and the police. You want the tending book."

"Mrs. Fenwick," Bel said, "before we go any further, I should tell you that I'm here in an official capacity, and that this woman is the subject of an active Assize warrant."

"I know exactly who you both are," said Ada. "You're the warden who arrived yesterday and didn't arrest anybody, and she's the girl from the Ram who's been walking the green at night for a week and a half. Sit down, both of you, I'm seventy nine and I'm not shouting up at people in my own back room."

They sat down.

Wren, watching, thought: *she is not surprised, and she should be surprised, and she has been waiting for somebody to knock on this door for a very long time.*

---

The tending book was not a book. It was three books, or the remains of three, bound at different times by different hands and stored in a hinged wooden box that Ada set on the table and opened with a small deliberate ceremony.

"This one's the copy," she said, laying out the newest, a black clothbound ledger with a spine gone soft. "Nineteen eleven, when they capped the well and the parish decided everything ought to be written up properly. That's what the council would look at if they ever thought to look at anything, which they don't."

She turned it around so they could read.

It was a list. Columns for date, names, and a third column headed simply KEPT. The dates ran three times a year, spring, midsummer and the first Sunday of November, and beside each date were four names.

*10.iii.1912. M. Fenwick, J. Ridley, A. Ridley, E. Sowerby. Kept.*

Page after page of it. The same handful of surnames turning over, mothers replaced by daughters, a Sowerby giving way to a Tanner in 1927, four names and four names and four names, three times a year, for thirty two years.

And then, on a page near the back, in a hand that had gone shaky:

*5.xi.1944. A. Ridley. Kept, alone, poorly.*

And under it, nothing. The rest of the page was blank. The rest of the book was blank.

Wren put her finger on the last entry and did not immediately trust her own voice.

"What happened in nineteen forty four?"

"Annie Ridley's sister died in the March, her cousin was killed at Anzio and the fourth was Elsie Tanner who took her children to her mother's in Carlisle and never came back," Ada said. "So Annie did it on her own, which you're not supposed to do, and she wrote that she'd done it poorly because she was an honest woman. And then Annie died in the February of forty five, and there was nobody left who'd been taught it, and the war was on and everybody had a great deal else to think about." She smoothed the page with two fingers. "And that was that. Nobody decided to stop. That's the part I've never been able to make anyone understand. There was no meeting. Nobody voted. Four women simply weren't there any more, and the third Sunday came round, and nobody went."

"Eighty one years," said Bel.

"Eighty one years," Ada agreed.

"There's a thing I've never understood about it," Ada said, "and I'll say it now while we're all being honest, which I notice we suddenly are. Everybody assumes the coven was the important part. The names, the four of them, the standing in a circle. And I've had this book fifty one years and I don't think that's it at all."

"What is it, then?"

"The date." Ada tapped the column with one finger. "Look at them. Tenth of March. Twenty second of June. Fifth of November. Every year, same three, give or take a Sunday. Thirty two years." She sat back. "It wasn't a spell. It was a rota. It's the same as the church flowers or the gritting or who does the field gate when it comes off its hinge. Somebody's turn, three times a year, written in a book so that nobody can say afterward that they didn't know it was theirs."

"And when the rota stopped," said Bel.

"When the rota stopped, nothing happened," said Ada. "That's the whole difficulty. For eighty one years absolutely nothing happened, and every year of nothing made it that little bit more obvious to everybody that there had never been anything to worry about in the first place."

Wren sat back.

She had been raised at Ashmoor from the age of six. She had been taught, badly and by people who did not much like her, that a seal was a piece of coven property, a thing you held title to, and that the holding of it was the entire justification for the coven's standing. She had never once in twenty two years been taught what happened to a seal when the coven simply stopped existing, because at Ashmoor the answer was unthinkable. Ashmoor did not stop existing. Ashmoor took other people's things and grew.

"What is it," she said. "Under the well."

Ada looked at her for a long moment with pale, entirely undeceived eyes.

"You'd know better than I would, pet," she said. "I've never had a scrap of it in me. I'm the one who writes it down. That's all my family ever were, the ones who could see enough to keep the book." She reached past the black ledger and drew out the second volume, older, its cover marbled and worn to the board at one corner. "But this is the one you want, and I'll tell you now, I've had it out on this table three times in my life and put it away again twice."

"Why?"

"Because both times the person I'd got it out for said *what a lot of nonsense*," said Ada, "and I decided I'd rather keep it than argue about it."

She opened the marbled book to a page held with a ribbon, and turned it round.

There was a diagram.

Wren looked at it and felt the room tilt very slightly, because she knew it. Not the drawing, which was crude, ink gone brown, done by somebody with more care than skill. She knew the *shape*, the way you know a face you have not seen since childhood. Four points around a centre. The long line through it that Ashmoor called the spine and that this book, in its faded copperplate, called the *thread*.

Under the diagram, in the same hand:

*The Well at Lost Ember. Tending as it hath been done. To be worked by four joined, and by no fewer, and never by one.*

"Four," said Bel.

"Four," said Ada.

"There aren't four," said Bel. "There's a girl of seventeen who nobody's ever taught anything, a widow who hasn't touched it since her husband died, a fugitive from a coven in Northumberland, and me, and I'm not even sure I count."

Ada Fenwick folded her hands on the table.

"Then that's a great pity," she said pleasantly, "because there were four names on that page three times a year for thirty two years, and there has been nobody at all for eighty one, and on Tuesday morning the ground came open. You are all very welcome to go on telling me what you are not. It won't make any odds to the well."

Nobody said anything.

Outside, faintly, a car went past on the top road, and the sound of it seemed to come from a very long way off.

"How wide is it this morning?" Ada asked, without looking up.

Wren, who had measured it at half past six with a folding rule she had bought in the shop and had told nobody about, said: "Three and a quarter inches."
