# What Nessa Hears

"Don't say anything for a bit," Nessa said. "That's the only rule. You can ask after. Just not while."

"All right."

"People always start talking. They think it's polite."

"I said all right."

Nessa gave her a look, decided to accept it, and went down the bank into the beck.

It was Tuesday afternoon and the light was already going, which it did earlier every day now, and the beck ran along the bottom of the green behind a line of alders and then went under the road and under the green itself through a stone culvert that had been built at some point when people still built culverts out of stone. The mouth of it was about four feet high. There was a rusted iron grille across it that had been broken in one corner since before Nessa was born, and the gap was big enough for a person who did not mind getting wet to the knee.

Nessa did not mind. She had been coming here since she was eleven.

Wren came down the bank after her without complaint, which Nessa noted. Sharon would have said *is it safe*. Her Year 10 form tutor would have said *does anyone know we're here*. The last social worker but one, who had actually been all right, would have taken one look at the culvert and said *absolutely not* and then bought her chips.

Wren just looked at the grille, and at the water, and rolled her jeans up.

Inside it smelled of wet stone and iron and, underneath that, very faintly, of something warm.

They went in maybe fifteen feet, to where the stonework changed. Nessa put her hand on the join to show her: on one side the blocks were regular and cut, all the same size, Victorian; on the other they were bigger and rougher and did not match, and the mortar was different, and the whole passage narrowed by about a foot.

"They built the culvert into something that was already here," Wren said.

"You're talking."

"Sorry."

Nessa went another six feet, ducking, and then stopped, and crouched down with her back against the older stone, and did not say anything else.

She waited.

After a moment, behind her, she heard Wren crouch down too.

---

It took about four minutes, which was quicker than usual. It was always quicker here.

It started the way it always started, in the roots of her back teeth, and then in her hands, and then it stopped being anywhere in particular and became the other thing, the thing she had never been able to explain to a doctor or a foster carer or the woman at CAMHS who had been very nice and had written *auditory phenomena* on a form.

It was not a voice. That was the part nobody ever got. Nessa had spent eight years listening to adults arrange their faces carefully and ask her *what does the voice say, love,* and it was not a voice, there were no words in it, it had never once told her to do anything.

It was more like being in the next room to a conversation. You cannot hear the words through the wall. You can hear the rhythm of it, and you can hear when it goes up at the end, and you know absolutely, with total certainty, whether the person next door is angry or frightened or telling a long story they have told before.

That was what came up through the stone.

She sat with her back against it and let it come, and it came the way it always did, patient and enormous and slow, and it was not angry. Nessa had been trying to say that for eight years and had never once found anybody who would let her finish the sentence. It was not angry. It had never been angry.

It went on. It went on and on, the way weather goes on, the way a river goes on, and it had been going on before there was a village and before there was a culvert and before anybody had thought to put a lid on a hole and write a date on it.

And then, over the top of it, the other thing, the thing that had only started on Tuesday, which was the reason she had brought Wren down here.

A change in the rhythm.

Nessa opened her eyes.

Wren was crouched three feet away with her hand flat on the old stone and her face absolutely white, and she was crying, without any noise at all, and did not appear to have noticed.

---

On the way back out through the grille Nessa stopped and put her hand on the old stonework where the two masonries met.

"There's one more thing and then I'll shut up," she said. "It changed on Friday."

"Changed how?"

"The rhythm." Nessa struggled with it, the way she always struggled with it, because there were no words that had ever been made for this. "It's like. You know when somebody's telling a long story they've told a hundred times, and you can hear from the other room that they're on the bit they always do? It's been that my whole life. The same shape, over and over, patient, going nowhere."

"And on Friday?"

"On Friday it started repeating." Nessa's hand was flat on the stone. "Same six or seven seconds, again and again and again, for four days. And then on Tuesday morning the ground came open and it stopped repeating and went back to the long thing."

Wren had gone very still behind her.

"Nessa. When did it start repeating, exactly?"

"Friday, about three in the morning. That's when I woke up with my jaw."

"And you're certain it stopped when the crack opened."

"It stopped when the crack opened," said Nessa. "I'm certain because I noticed and I was relieved and then I felt sick about being relieved. Why? What is it?"

Wren did not answer for a moment.

"At Ashmoor," she said, "when a seal starts to go, the thing under it does one of two things. Either it goes quiet, which is bad, because quiet means it has already found the way out and is not wasting effort. Or it repeats. And repeating means it is trying the same place over and over because that is the only place that gives."

"So it was pushing."

"It was knocking," said Wren, "on the same spot, for four days, and you were the only person in England who could hear it, and there was nobody to tell."

They sat on the bank afterward with their feet in the grass and the light going.

"You can talk now," Nessa said.

Wren did not, for a while.

"How long?" she said eventually.

"Eight years. Since I was nine."

"Every day?"

"No. It comes and goes. Two or three times a year, mostly autumn, and then it stopped being twice a year about eighteen months ago and now it's most weeks." Nessa picked at the grass. "That's the bit I've never told anybody, that it's got more. Because the second you say a thing's got worse, that's when the appointments start."

Wren wiped her face with the back of her wrist, roughly, as though annoyed with it.

"It's not angry," she said.

Nessa's chest went tight so fast it hurt.

"Say that again."

"It isn't angry." Wren turned to look at her. "It's not raging and it's not trying to get out and it's not hunting anybody. It's *talking*. It's been talking the entire time, and everything I was ever taught about seals said it would be like holding a door against a shoulder, and that is not what that is."

"No," Nessa said. Her voice had gone strange. "No, it isn't."

"How many people have you told that to?"

"All of them. Every single one. For eight years." Nessa was gripping the grass with both hands now. "And every one of them did the face. There's a face they do. It's the face where they've stopped listening to what you're saying and started listening to *how* you're saying it, because they've decided the how is the useful information and the what is the symptom."

"And I didn't do the face."

"You cried," said Nessa, and it came out furious, and she had absolutely no idea why she was furious. "You went down there and you sat on the floor of a drain and you *cried*, and nobody has ever, not once, in eight years."

Wren said nothing for a moment.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and I'm not going to tell the other two yet, and I want you to understand that's not because I trust you more. It's because you already know it and nobody's ever confirmed it, and I've been where you are, and it's not survivable for as long as people think it is."

"Go on."

"There's four kinds of people at a place like Ashmoor." Wren pulled her knees up. "There's the ones who can work it. That's most. There's the ones who can work it well, that's a handful. There's the ones who can teach it. And then, once every couple of generations, there's one who doesn't work it at all and just *hears* the ground."

Nessa did not breathe.

"They don't have a nice word for it," Wren said. "I want to be honest with you about that, because I'd rather you had it from me than found out later. At Ashmoor they call that one the ear, and they don't say it kindly, and they do not let her go anywhere on her own."

"Why not?"

"Because she's the only one who can tell them whether a seal is holding," said Wren, "and that makes her the most valuable object in the building."

The beck went on running under the road. Somewhere up on the green a car door shut.

"That's what I am," Nessa said.

"That's what you are."

"And you're telling me because."

Wren looked at her for a long moment, and there was something in her face that Nessa was too young, that autumn, to read properly, and that she understood completely about eleven days later.

"Because in three days," Wren said, "you're going to find out that I've been lying to all of you since I got here. And when that happens I want there to be one thing on the other side of the scale that I told you first, and true, and before I had to."
