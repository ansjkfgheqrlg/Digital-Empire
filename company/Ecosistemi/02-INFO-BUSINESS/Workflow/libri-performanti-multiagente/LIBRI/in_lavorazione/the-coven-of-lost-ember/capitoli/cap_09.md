# Nobody Is Coming

The Assize came back to her at eleven forty on the Monday morning, thirty one hours after the transport request, in the form of a telephone call from a man Bel had met twice.

"Warden Carrow."

"Sir."

"Your request of yesterday morning." A pause with paper in it. "Halloway, W. E. You've got her in custody."

"I have her secured in a locked room at licensed premises in the village. It's not a custody suite and I've logged it as what it is." Bel was standing in the lane above the church with the phone to her ear because it was the only place in Lost Ember with two bars. "I've asked for transport four times now. Three of those were automated acknowledgements and one was a delivery failure."

"Yes." Another pause. "There isn't going to be transport."

Bel looked down the valley at the church, and the green beyond it with a strip of blue and white tape across the middle of it now, and the council barrier at the bottom of the lane.

"Say that again, sir, please."

"The Assize is not sending a unit to Lost Ember." Warden Hollis had a pleasant voice. He had always had a pleasant voice. "There's a geological incident in progress, the county's got the site, and it is not appropriate for us to be inserting an enforcement action into a live civil emergency. You'll release her under caution pending, and you'll secure the site, and you'll await reassignment. That's the instruction."

"Release her."

"Under caution."

"Sir, I have chased this woman for two years and one month. In that time I have submitted forty three reports. On four occasions I was told to drop other work to pursue her." Bel heard her own voice, and heard that it was perfectly steady, and was distantly impressed. "And now she is thirty yards from me behind a door with a key in my pocket, and the instruction is release her."

"The instruction is that the priority has changed."

"Then may I ask what it's changed to?"

"You may not," said Hollis, without any edge at all, which was somehow worse than if there had been one. "Secure the site. Await reassignment. And Bel." The first time he had used her name in the entire call. "The village is a county matter now. It isn't yours. Don't make it yours."

The line went.

---

She sat in the car for an hour.

That was not a decision either. She got into the driver's seat because it was raining and she needed to be somewhere that was not a lane, and then the hour went by, and she spent it going through the file in her lap page by page the way she had gone through it perhaps two hundred times, except that this time she was reading it backward.

Not the contents. The shape.

*Unauthorised removal of coven property. Value substantial. Coven requests recovery.* No inventory. She had asked twice and been refused twice, and had accepted that, because coven business was coven business and the Assize had always been very clear about the line.

Forty three reports. Every one of them acknowledged within two working days, which, she noticed now, laying them out in date order on the passenger seat, was a faster turnaround than the Assize managed for anything else she had ever filed in twenty one years.

Four occasions on which she had been told to drop other work. She looked those up. October of last year, she had been pulled off a licensing dispute in Alston. February, off a records audit. April, off an unregistered practitioner in Consett who had been selling something to teenagers and who, as far as Bel could establish from the case number, had never subsequently been dealt with by anybody at all.

And then a village opens up under her feet, and the same organisation that had moved her across three counties to chase this woman for two years now could not be persuaded to send a van.

Bel sat in the rain with the file open on her knees and permitted herself, for the first time in twenty one years, to finish a thought she had been walking around since the Hartsett inquiry.

*They did not want her caught. They wanted her followed.*

She turned that over carefully, looking for the flaw in it, and could not immediately find one, and the absence of a flaw made her feel physically unwell.

Because if you wanted a woman found and stopped, you circulated her details, you notified ports, you put out a general alert, and you had her inside a fortnight. And if instead you gave one officer with a good record and a personal reason to be dogged a file with no inventory in it and a rolling instruction to keep after her, what you got was not an arrest.

What you got was a very reliable record of exactly where Wren Halloway had been, and when, updated forty three times over two years, filed promptly, acknowledged within two working days.

Bel Carrow had been the tracking device.

---

The back room of the Ram had a window onto the yard, a bed, a chair, a table with a lamp, and a lock that Sheila had fitted in 1998 for reasons involving a stag party. Bel unlocked it at ten past one and pushed the door open and stood in the doorway.

Wren was sitting on the floor with her back against the bed, which was, Bel thought, exactly where a person who has spent two years in rooms would sit. She looked up. She did not get up.

"That was quick," she said. "Where are we going?"

"Nowhere."

Something in Wren's face closed. "Right. So it's here, then."

"No." Bel came in and shut the door behind her, which she was aware was procedurally indefensible, and sat down on the chair. "I'm releasing you under caution. That's the instruction from my superior as of ninety minutes ago. There's no transport. There isn't going to be transport. The Assize is not coming to Lost Ember."

The silence went on for a while.

"Say the rest of it," Wren said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"You've come in and shut the door, and you've sat down on the chair instead of standing in the doorway, and you've told me the Assize isn't coming, and your hands are shaking." Wren's voice was not unkind. "I've been arrested by four wardens in two years and not one of them has shut the door. So say the rest of it."

Bel looked at her own hands, which were, in fact, shaking slightly.

"Forty three reports," she said. "Two years. They knew where you were every eleven days on average and they never once put out a general alert, and when I asked what you'd taken they told me it was coven business, and I let them, and I have been carrying their file across eleven counties like a very expensive dog."

Wren went absolutely still.

"You worked that out today," she said.

"In the car. In the last hour."

"I worked it out in the fourth month," said Wren, "and I've been carrying it on my own ever since, and I want you to know that I am not enjoying this even slightly."

Bel put her head back against the wall.

"There's a girl of seventeen in this village," she said, "who put her hands on the ground on Friday night and closed three feet of it, and a man in Kendal with a plate in his skull because we tried it and got it wrong, and my organisation has just told me, in a pleasant voice, that the village is a county matter and I should not make it mine." She looked across at Wren. "So I am going to ask you the question I asked on Wednesday, and I am going to ask it as a woman sitting on a chair in a pub and not as a warden, because as of eleven forty this morning I am fairly sure I am not one of those in any way that means anything."

"Ask it."

"What did you take from Ashmoor?"

And Bel watched Wren Halloway open her mouth, and hesitate, and she saw it happen, she saw the exact instant where the answer was available and was not given, and something in her chest went very tired.

"I can't," Wren said.

"Can't or won't?"

"Both, and I know how that sounds." Wren dragged both hands down her face. "Bel, if I tell you now, in this room, with the ground open out there, I will have handed you a reason to put me in a car and drive me out of this valley, and you would be *right* to. And there's four of us and one of me and I can't be certain enough that I'd be wrong."

"That is not an answer."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's the same non answer you got on Wednesday. I'm aware. I'm going to give you the real one, and I'm going to give it to all four of you at once, and I need three more days."

Bel stood up. She put the key on the table.

"Door's open," she said. "The road out goes past the barrier and there's a diversion at the top of the pass, and by tonight there'll be a man from the highways department sitting in a van at the bottom of the lane. If you're going, go before six."

She was at the door when Wren said, "Warden."

"Not any more, apparently."

"Bel." A pause. "Your sister. At Hartsett."

Bel stopped with her hand on the frame and did not turn round.

"How do you know I had a sister at Hartsett," she said.

The room behind her was quiet for a long moment.

"Three days," said Wren.
