# Four Is the Number

It took them until Saturday to agree to do a thing all four of them had privately decided on by Thursday lunchtime, which Wren thought afterwards was probably a fair summary of the entire species.

They met in Ada's back room because there was nowhere else in Lost Ember where four women could sit for two hours without the village constructing a reason for it. Ada made tea in a pot with a cosy and then sat back in her chair by the shelves and said almost nothing for the first hour, which Wren came to understand was a technique.

"Right," said Bel, and put the marbled book in the middle of the table. "I've read it four times. I want to say what I think it says, and then I want each of you to tell me where I've got it wrong."

"You're chairing," said Marged.

"Somebody has to."

"I'm only observing that you're chairing."

"Marged," said Ada, from her chair, "let the woman chair."

Bel opened the book to the ribbon. "It says the tending is worked by four joined. Not four present. Four *joined*, which as far as I can make out from the rest of it means a formal binding between the four of us that holds for the duration and then some. It says never by one. It says the old book has more on the joining than this one does, and the old book is where, Mrs. Fenwick?"

"In the box," said Ada. "Eighteen thirty nine. And I'll get it out when there's a reason to, and not before, because it's in pieces and every time it's handled it's in more pieces."

"That's a reason to."

"That's a reason to *try the copy first*," said Ada, "which is what the copy is for."

Bel let it go, which Wren noted. Two days ago she would not have.

"So," Bel said. "Four. Which brings us to the part where we go round the table and everybody says why they can't."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Then Marged put down her cup.

"I'll go first, since it's obvious," she said. "I haven't worked anything in eleven years. I stopped for reasons this village thinks it knows and doesn't, and I have no intention of explaining them at this table or any other. What I will say is this. A joining is not a handshake. It's not four people standing in a field agreeing to cooperate. It opens you to the other three. What I have got in me is not something I am willing to open to three women I met on Wednesday, and if you had any idea what you were asking you would not be asking it."

"That's fair," said Wren.

Marged turned on her. "Don't."

"I'm not managing you. It's fair. It's the most sensible thing anybody's said in this room." Wren put both hands flat on the table. "So here's mine. I'm not staying. I've been in this village eleven days and I've been in eleven other places before it and I have never once stayed longer than a fortnight, and there's a reason for that, and the reason is sitting on the other side of this table with a warrant in her coat."

"You could remove the reason," Bel said evenly, "by coming in."

"To Ashmoor."

"To the Assize."

"There is no difference between those two things and you know it, or you'd have been able to tell me by now what it is I'm supposed to have stolen." Wren's voice did not rise, and it cost her something to keep it there. "Two years. Two years you've had that file. Go on. Tell me what's on the property line."

Bel Carrow looked at her for a long moment.

"Unspecified," she said. "Coven property, value substantial, no inventory. I asked twice and was told it was coven business."

"And that didn't strike you as odd."

"It struck me as extremely odd," Bel said, "which is why I asked twice."

Ada Fenwick, from her chair, made a small pleased sound like a woman watching a knot come loose.

---

"Mine's short," said Nessa.

They looked at her. She had said perhaps nine words in an hour, sitting with her chair pulled back from the table by a foot, in the position Wren recognised as the one you take when you have learned that being fully at a table is an invitation for somebody to ask you to leave it.

"I've never been taught anything," Nessa said. "Not one thing. I don't know what a joining is. I don't know what a binding is. Two nights ago I sat on the grass and something happened and I still don't know if I did it. Everyone at this table has spent their whole life around this and I've spent mine being told I'm difficult." She looked up. "So I'm not saying I can't. I'm saying I'm the one who'll get it wrong, and I want it said out loud now by me, instead of said about me later by one of you."

Nobody had an answer for that immediately.

"That was not short," said Ada, "and it was the best thing anybody has said today."

"And mine," said Bel, "is that I am an officer of the Assize, I have an active warrant against a woman sitting eighteen inches away from me, and if I formally join with her the warrant is finished. Not delayed. Finished. There is no version of that I can write up afterwards that doesn't end my career, and I have given the Assize twenty one years."

"So don't," said Wren.

"I've been thinking about very little else since Thursday."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Bel agreed. "It isn't."

The room was quiet. Outside, in the lane, somebody's dog was going up and down the fence.

Ada Fenwick put her cup down in the saucer with a small definite click.

"Three and a quarter inches on Thursday morning," she said. "Four and a half this morning. I went out at seven and measured it myself, and I want you all to know that it is the first time in fifty one years I have written a number in that book in my own hand instead of copying somebody else's."

She reached over and turned the black ledger round.

"There's a column there," she said, "that says KEPT. Thirty two years of women wrote *kept* in it three times a year, and I don't imagine for a moment that all of them liked each other. Elsie Tanner and Annie Ridley didn't speak from nineteen thirty one to nineteen thirty six over a matter concerning a fence. They still went up on the third Sunday. They stood in the same circle and wrote *kept* and went home and didn't speak."

She looked round at the four of them over the top of her glasses.

"You are all very impressive," said Ada. "You've each got a reason and every one of them is a good reason and I've enjoyed listening. But not one of you has said the thing that matters, which is what happens to four hundred people if the four of you are right about yourselves."

---

Nobody moved for a while after that.

It was Nessa who broke it, which surprised all of them including Nessa.

"Can I ask something stupid," she said, "and can everyone please not do the face."

"What face," said Bel.

"The face where you're patient with me." She pulled her chair in six inches, which Wren noticed and said nothing about. "What actually happens if it just keeps going? Nobody's said. Everyone keeps saying it gets wider and everyone says it like that's the end of the sentence, and I want somebody to finish the sentence."

Marged and Wren looked at each other, and neither of them wanted it.

"It's a seal," Wren said finally. "Not a lid. It's not holding a thing down in a box, it's holding a place shut, the way a stitch holds a wound shut. Take the stitches out of a wound and the wound doesn't explode. It just stops being closed, and then everything that was outside it is inside it, and everything that was inside it is out."

"That's not a finished sentence either."

"No," Wren admitted. "It isn't."

"Then finish it."

Wren opened her mouth, and closed it, and looked at the table.

"Hartsett," said Bel.

She said it quietly, to the room, and she was watching Wren while she said it, and Wren did not look up.

"Eleventh of October, two thousand and eleven. The lane above the village subsided in a straight line and took four buildings with it." Bel's voice stayed absolutely level, which cost her something none of them could see. "Eleven people. It's in the county record as a landslip. There was an inquiry and the inquiry used the phrase *irregular ground conditions* and then never went back to it."

The room had gone very quiet.

"Eleven people," Nessa said.

"Eleven."

"And Lost Ember's got four hundred and six."

"Yes," said Bel.

Wren still had not looked up from the table, and Ada Fenwick, in her chair by the shelves, noticed that, and wrote nothing down, and did not forget it either.

Nessa sat with that. Then she nodded once, briskly, the way she had seen adults do, and said, "Right. Thanks. That's a finished sentence," and put her hands in her lap, and Bel Carrow found that she had to look away from her for a moment.

They tried it at two o'clock on the Sunday morning.

It was not a decision anybody announced. What happened was that Bel said "once", in the tone of a woman conceding a point in an argument she intended to reopen, and Marged said "under my terms and I'll walk away the moment I say so", and Nessa said nothing at all but had already stood up, and Wren, who had spent two years learning exactly how long she could stay anywhere, heard herself say "I'll give it till Tuesday" and understood that she had just made the first promise of any kind since she left Ashmoor.

Ada photocopied the diagram at the shop on Saturday afternoon, on the machine by the door, for eleven pence.

It seemed to Wren, watching her feed the ancient marbled page under the lid with enormous care, that this was the most extraordinary thing that had happened all week.
