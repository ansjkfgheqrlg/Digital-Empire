# The First Attempt

The green at two in the morning was colder than Nessa had expected, and quieter, and much bigger.

That was the thing nobody warned you about. In daylight the green was a scrap of grass with a bench on it that you crossed in forty seconds. At two o'clock on a Sunday morning in late September, with the street lamp off because the street lamp went off at midnight, it was an enormous dark field with a church at one end of it and a hole down the middle, and the fells on both sides came down much closer than they did in the day.

She had the photocopy in her hands. That had been Ada's decision, delivered without discussion: *the youngest holds the paper, that's how it was always done, and don't ask me why because I've no idea, it's simply what the book says and the book has been right about everything else.*

"Right," said Marged. "Positions. And I'll say this once so nobody has to guess later. If I stop, I stop. I'm not being difficult and I'm not letting anybody down. If I say the word, you all step back and we go home, and we discuss it at a reasonable hour like people."

"What's the word?" said Bel.

"*Stop*," said Marged. "I've never seen the sense in a code."

They took the four points off the diagram. It was not a square. That was the first thing that had surprised all of them when Ada laid it out: the four positions were uneven, one much closer to the well than the others, two nearly side by side, and the fourth well back, and the marbled book had a note beside it in the same brown ink that said only *as the ground gives*.

Marged took the near point, closest to the well lid. Bel took the far one. Wren and Nessa took the two together, six feet apart, on the eastern side, with the crack between them and the church.

Nessa found that she could not stop looking at the well.

It sat eight feet from Marged's position, a round drum of dressed stone about waist high, with a flat circular lid on top of it that had a lifting ring set into the middle, gone orange with rust. She had walked past it perhaps ten thousand times. There was a plaque on it, from the council, that said EMBER WELL, CAPPED 1911, and a chewing gum wrapper had been wedged in the gap between lid and rim since roughly Easter.

It looked so ordinary that she wanted to laugh, and the wanting to laugh was the fear finding somewhere to go.

"Don't stare at it," said Wren quietly, from six feet away.

"I'm not."

"You are, and I've done it too, and it doesn't help." Wren shifted her feet on the wet grass. "Look at the church tower. Pick a thing that's been there four hundred years and isn't doing anything tonight."

Nessa looked at the church tower. It helped, slightly, which annoyed her.

"Have you done this before?" she said. "Not this. Something like it."

There was a pause long enough that she thought she was not going to get an answer.

"Yes," said Wren.

"Did it work?"

"Yes," said Wren, in a completely different voice, and did not say anything else.

"Hands," said Marged.

Nessa put the photocopy inside her coat and held out her hands.

"Not to each other," Marged said. "Not yet. That comes at the joining, and we're a long way off the joining. Down. Palms down, six inches above the ground, and hold them there, and whatever happens do not put them on the grass."

"Why not?"

"Because you're the only one of us it will talk to," said Marged, "and I would rather it had to shout."

---

It began the way Nessa had never imagined it would begin, which was gently.

Marged spoke the opening off the copy in a low, unhurried voice, and it was not an incantation, or nothing like the thing Nessa had assembled in her head out of films. It was closer to a list. Names of places: the beck, the pass, the four fields, the church. Names of people who had done this before, read straight off the black ledger, twenty six of them, ending with *Annie Ridley, who kept it alone and poorly, and was not to blame.*

And then a long line that Marged called the thread, spoken slowly, and Nessa felt it go out from the four of them and down.

It caught.

She would try many times afterward to explain what that felt like and never manage it. The nearest she ever got was that it was like the moment in a car when the clutch bites: a whole system that had been spinning free suddenly taking hold of something and becoming one machine. The green went taut. The humming in her hands, that awful eight year old humming that had never once meant anything good, ran up her arms and joined with three other things she had never felt before and settled, all four together, into something that had a shape.

*Oh,* Nessa thought, with a clarity that had nothing to do with words. *Oh, this is what it's for.*

For nine seconds it held.

She counted them afterward, over and over, and always got nine.

In the ninth second she felt something in the shape go wrong. Not break. Wrong, the way a chord is wrong, one note in four sitting a half step off and dragging at all the others. It came from the western side, from the far point, from Bel, and even as Nessa registered that she felt a second wrongness from the near point, from Marged, in a completely different direction, and then a third, close by, so close it was almost against her, from Wren.

And then, because she was seventeen and had never done this and because the shape was pulling and she wanted more than anything in her life for it to hold, Nessa Quill did the one thing she had been told not to do.

She put her hands on the grass.

---

The backlash went out from the green in a ring.

It did not make a sound. That was the detail the village argued about for weeks afterward, because eleven people woke at four minutes past two and not one of them could say what had woken them. It was not a bang. It was a pressure, a single hard shove outward through the ground and the air, and it went through the four of them and through the wall and out into the lane.

Nessa was thrown backward off her hands and landed badly on her shoulder. Marged went down on one knee. Wren stayed up, somehow, and Bel was already turning, because Bel had heard the one thing the rest of them had not, which was the sound of somebody at the gate.

Gil Tanner had come out because his dog would not settle.

He had been standing at his own gate on the far side of the lane for perhaps ninety seconds, twenty five feet from the western point of the circle, watching four women stand round a hole in the dark and not understanding a single thing about it.

The ring caught him full on and put him into the stone wall of his own garden.

---

Afterward Nessa remembered it in pieces that would not go in order. Bel running. Bel's voice on a phone, giving a grid reference, absolutely level, the voice of somebody who has done this before and hated it. Marged on her knees on the tarmac with her cardigan under Gil's head and both hands somewhere Nessa could not see, saying *no, no, no, not this, not again* over and over under her breath in a way that Nessa did not understand at all and would not understand for another two weeks. Wren holding Nessa's face in both hands and asking her something and asking it again.

The ambulance came from Kendal and took fifty one minutes.

And at some point in the middle of all of it, standing in the lane with a blanket round her and Gil Tanner's blood on her sleeve, Nessa turned and looked back at the green.

The crack was six inches wide.

It had been four and a half at seven o'clock the previous morning. They had come out at two to close it, and they had worked the tending for nine seconds, and it was six inches wide and glowing dully along its entire length from the church wall to the road.

"We made it worse," she said.

Nobody answered her, which she understood, even then, was because all three of them had worked it out at least a minute before she had.
