# Nessa Says It

"Me," said Nessa. "I'll go. Because if I don't go now I'll spend two hours working out how to say it and then it'll be worse."

She had her chair pulled in to the table for the first time in a fortnight. She had noticed herself do it and had decided not to be embarrassed about it.

"Right," she said. "There's three."

"Take your time," said Ada.

"No, that's what I mean, if I take my time I'll pick the easy one and stop." Nessa put her hands on the table, flat, because that was apparently what people did in this kitchen. "First one. I knew four days early."

Nobody said anything.

"The crack came on the Tuesday, everybody knows that, Gil nearly put his foot in it. I woke up on the Friday before with my jaw locked and the taste of pennies, and it didn't stop after twenty minutes, and it didn't stop on the Saturday or the Sunday or the Monday either, and on the Sunday night I stood at the Harrisons' sink at half four in the morning with my hands under the cold tap knowing perfectly well that something was coming."

"You were seventeen," said Marged, "and you'd been told for eight years that it was in your head."

"That's the reason and it's a good one and it's not the true one." Nessa made herself keep going. "The true one is that for four days I was the only person in Lost Ember who knew something. That's it. That's the whole of it. Eight years of being the girl who was left at a church door and gets talked about kindly, and for four days I had a thing that was mine and nobody else had it, and every hour I didn't tell anybody it stayed mine a bit longer."

She stopped. The kitchen was very quiet.

"And on the Tuesday Gil found the crack," she said, "and I sat on the wall outside the shop with a bacon roll going cold and I watched the whole village stand round it, and I thought, *I knew about you on Friday.* And it felt good. It felt good for about four seconds and then it felt like being sick, and I've been carrying that since the twenty third of August."

"Nessa," said Wren.

"Don't be nice to me yet, there's two more."

---

"Second one. It talks to me and it always has, and I've told everybody that, but I've never told anybody how long the conversation is."

She looked at her hands.

"It's not two or three times a year. That's what I say, because it's what's survivable to say. It's most nights. It's been most nights since I was about thirteen. And I've been going down to the culvert since I was eleven, and I go because it's quieter down there, and I've told myself for six years that I go to make it stop." Nessa's voice was very flat. "I don't. I go because down there I can hear it properly. It's the only place in the world where I'm not straining."

"You told me that," said Wren. "In the culvert."

"I told you it talked. I didn't tell you I go and sit in a drain three nights a week to listen to it." Nessa looked up. "Because that's the sentence where somebody's face changes. I've watched about eleven adults' faces change and I've got very good at knowing which sentence does it, and that's the one."

Ada Fenwick said, "Mine hasn't changed."

"No," said Nessa. "I know. That's why I said it."

---

"Third one," she said, and this time she had to wait a moment before she could start.

"Right. So." She pressed her palms into the table. "There have been nights when I've wanted it to come up."

Nobody moved.

"Not lots. I want that on the record because it matters to me that this is accurate. Not lots, and never for long, and never when anything else was going right. But there have been nights, and there was one in February and there was one about three weeks before all this started, where I've been down at that grille at two in the morning and I've thought: *go on then.*"

"Why," said Bel. Not gently. Just asking.

"Because it's the only thing that's ever wanted me."

The kitchen was completely silent.

"That's the sentence," Nessa said. "That's the actual one. I've known it for about two years and I've never said it because it's pathetic and because if a social worker heard it I'd have been in an assessment by Thursday." Her eyes were streaming and her voice was perfectly steady, which she would find strange afterward. "Everybody here has been somewhere and lost it. Marged had a husband. Bel had a sister. Wren had a whole house full of people who taught her, even if they were awful. I have never had one single person, not one, in seventeen years, who was pleased I was in the room. And there is a thing under that green that has been talking to me since I was nine years old and has never once stopped and has never once wanted me to be different, and some nights at two in the morning that has been the only relationship I've got."

She wiped her face with the back of her wrist.

"So when Marged says *it isn't angry*, and Wren says *it's talking, not fighting*, you should both know that I am not a neutral witness. I have wanted to be right about that for eight years. That's a thing I'm holding back, and the book says it matters, so there it is."

---

Nobody spoke for a long moment.

Then Marged Pryce got up, came round the table, and put both arms round her, which was not a thing Marged did, and which Nessa would think about for the rest of her life.

"Right," said Marged, into the top of her head, briskly, in the voice of a woman refusing to make a scene while making one. "Well. That's the worst thing anybody's said in this kitchen today and I include mine, and I want you to notice that nobody has left the room."

Nessa made a noise that was half a laugh.

"There's a thing I'd like to say back," said Bel, "and I'd like to say it as somebody who has spent two years wanting a village to come open so that her sister's death would mean something." She had not moved from her chair. "You're not the only person at this table who has caught herself hoping for the wrong thing. Mine had a body count of eleven attached to it. Yours has nobody at all. And I said mine out loud in this room three nights ago and every one of you is still sitting here."

"That's the point of it, isn't it," said Ada. "That's the whole point of the line. *Told each other true.* It isn't a test of whether you're good. It's a test of whether you'll say it."

---

"There's four of us," Nessa said, later, when the tea had gone round again and the kitchen had loosened. "And three have gone."

She looked across the table.

Wren Halloway had not said anything for an hour and a half. She was sitting with the cold hearthstone in front of her on the folded tea towel, and she had the look of somebody at the top of a very high place who has already decided to jump and is only waiting for her feet.

"Wren," said Bel.

"I know."

"You said this morning that one of us would have to decide something in a second and a half."

"Yes," said Wren.

And then, outside, in the lane, a car door shut.

Nobody in Lost Ember shut a car door like that. It was too heavy, and there had been no engine noise coming down the pass, which meant the car had been coasting.

Ada Fenwick got up and went to her front window and looked out, and stood there for a moment, and when she turned round her face had gone the colour of the paper in the eighteen thirty nine book.

"There are three of them," she said. "And the one in front is a woman of about sixty in a very good coat, and she's looking at my door."
