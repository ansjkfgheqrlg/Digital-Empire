# The Girl on the Green

Marged found her at ten to midnight, because Marged had taken to walking past the green last thing and had stopped pretending to herself that it was for the air.

The girl was sitting at the very edge of the crack with her legs folded under her and both hands laid flat on the grass, palms down, on either side of the gap. She was in a T shirt. It was four degrees.

Marged stopped at the low wall and made herself stand still, which took some doing.

She had seen this before. Once, at her grandmother's table, when she was nine and her cousin Bethan had put her hands on a thing she should not have touched and had gone that same particular kind of quiet. There was a stillness that came over a person when a working had hold of them from the outside instead of the other way round, and it did not look like concentration and it did not look like fear. It looked like listening.

"Nessa," she said, keeping her voice low and level. "Nessa Quill."

No answer.

Marged came forward slowly, and as she came she felt it rise around her like heat off a road, the whole green humming with something that was not sound, and her own hands went warm in her coat pockets and she let them, because there was no time now to be busy refusing.

Six feet away she stopped again, because from here she could see the crack.

It was three and a quarter inches wide at half past six that morning. Wren Halloway had said so in Ada's back room and Marged had gone and checked it herself afterwards, because she had not lived sixty one years in order to take a stranger's word for a measurement.

It was not three and a quarter inches now. It was easily four, and along its whole length, in the dark, it was showing a faint dull orange, the colour of the inside of a stove an hour after you have banked it down.

"Nessa," Marged said. "I want you to take your hands off the grass."

The girl's head turned. Her eyes were entirely present, which was somehow far worse than if they had been rolled back.

"It's easier when I'm touching it," she said. Her voice was perfectly conversational. "It doesn't push as hard."

"Easier for you or easier for it?"

Nessa opened her mouth, and stopped, and Marged watched the question land somewhere the girl had never in her life thought to look.

"I don't know," Nessa said.

"No," said Marged. "That's what I was afraid of."

Footsteps on the path behind them, fast. Marged did not turn round. She heard the canvas bag hit the ground and then Wren Halloway went past her at a half run and dropped to a crouch behind the girl and got both arms round her from behind, one across the collarbone, one at the waist, and hauled her back off the grass in a single hard movement, and the two of them went over together onto the wet turf four feet from the edge.

The green went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a machine you had stopped noticing switches off.

"Get off," Nessa said, in a voice that had gone very young.

"In a minute." Wren did not let go. She was breathing hard. "In a minute."

Marged crouched down beside them, ignoring her knee, and looked past both of them at the crack.

It had closed.

Not all of it. Not by much. But at the point where the girl's hands had been, over a stretch of perhaps three feet, the gap had drawn in to a hand's width less than it had been sixty seconds earlier, and the dull orange had gone out of it, and the turf at the very edge had crumpled inward slightly the way skin does at the edge of a healing thing.

"Marged," said Wren.

"I see it."

"I want you to tell me I'm seeing it too, because I have had a very long two years and I would genuinely like to know."

"You're seeing it," Marged said.

---

They took her to Marged's kitchen, because it was the nearest and because Marged had a range that was still warm and a blanket in the airing cupboard, and because it did not occur to any of the three of them to take a seventeen year old girl who was shaking with cold back to a foster placement at midnight and explain it.

Marged put the kettle on the hot plate and then stood at the range with her back to the room for longer than the kettle required.

Behind her, at the table, the girl was going through the shivering that comes ten minutes after the cold rather than during it, and Wren Halloway was crouched by the chair with a hand on the blanket, not talking, which Marged approved of more than she was prepared to say.

There had been a time when this kitchen had people in it at midnight fairly regularly. That was the thing that kept ambushing her. Owen's sisters, and old Peg Ridley before her chest went, and once, memorably, a man from Hexham who had driven ninety minutes with a jar wrapped in a tea towel and would not say what was in it until he had been given something to eat. Marged had run the tap and put the kettle on for all of them, in this room, with these cups, and had thought nothing of it whatsoever, and then Owen had died and the kitchen had become a place where one person made tea for one person.

She had not once, in eleven years, resented that. She had chosen it. What she had not expected was how quickly the room remembered how to be the other thing.

"There's biscuits in the tin behind you," she said, without turning round. "The tin, not the packet. The packet's stale, I keep it for the birds."

Bel Carrow arrived eleven minutes later, having been telephoned by Wren from Marged's hall phone in a conversation of four sentences, and stood in the kitchen doorway in a coat over pyjama trousers taking in the scene with the flat professional eye of a woman who has walked into a great many kitchens at midnight.

"Is she hurt?"

"She's cold," said Marged.

"I'm here," said Nessa, from inside the blanket.

"You are." Bel came in and sat down at the table, uninvited, which Marged decided to allow. "Right. Somebody tell me what happened, in order, and don't leave things out to be kind."

So Marged told her, in order, and did not leave things out.

When she had finished, Bel sat for a while with her hands flat on the tablecloth.

"How long," she said to Nessa, "have you been able to do that?"

"I've never done that," Nessa said. "I've never done anything. That's the whole point of me. Everyone's been waiting eight years for me to do something and I never have."

"You closed three feet of it with your hands."

"I didn't *close* anything." The blanket came down. The girl's face was blotchy and furious and about nine years old. "I sat there and it stopped shoving. That's all it's ever been. It's like, if you've got a dog barking at a door all night, and you go and sit against the door, it stops. That's not doing something. That's just being there."

Nobody spoke.

Wren, leaning against the sink with her arms folded, said quietly: "That's exactly what it is, though. That is the whole of it. That's what a coven is."

Nessa looked at her.

"Somebody sitting against the door," Wren said. "Three times a year. That's all it ever was. They just called it tending because the other word had gone out of fashion."

And Marged Pryce, standing at her own range with her hands still faintly warm, watched a girl who had spent eight years being told she was broken hear, for the first time in her life, an explanation in which she was not.

"There's four of us," Nessa said. "In this kitchen. Right now. There's four."

"No," said Bel.

"There's four of us in this kitchen."

"There are four people in this kitchen," Bel said. "That's not the same thing, and tomorrow morning I still have a warrant in my coat pocket with her name on it, and I would like everybody to be very clear about that before anyone says another word."

But she did not get up, and she did not take the warrant out, and she stayed at Marged Pryce's kitchen table until twenty past two in the morning, and at no point in those two hours did any of the four of them go anywhere near the door.
