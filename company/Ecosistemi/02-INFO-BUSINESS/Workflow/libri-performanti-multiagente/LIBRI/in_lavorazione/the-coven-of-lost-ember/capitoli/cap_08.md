# The Blame

The consultant at Kendal used the phrase *depressed skull fracture* at ten past six in the morning, and then the phrase *cautiously optimistic*, and Bel Carrow wrote both of them down in her notebook because writing things down was what she had instead of a way of feeling about them.

She drove back over the pass at half past seven with the sun coming up behind her and Gil Tanner's daughter's face doing something in the back of her mind that she could not put away.

They gathered in Ada's back room at nine because there was nowhere else, and because Ada opened the door before anybody knocked, which meant she had been at the window.

Nobody sat down.

"He's alive," Bel said. "Fracture to the left parietal. They've taken pressure off. They're saying cautiously optimistic and they're saying it's too early, and both of those are true at once and I'd ask you not to pick the one you prefer." She put her notebook on the table. "His wife is with him. His daughter is nine."

The silence went on a long time.

"Right," said Marged. "Then I'll say it, because somebody's got to and I'm the oldest. That was my fault."

"It wasn't," said Wren.

"I laid the thread. I set the points. I read the opening. If a working comes apart, it comes apart at the hand that built it, and the hand that built it was mine, and I have known that since about four minutes past two this morning." Marged's voice was flat and enormously controlled. "So that's said. And the second thing I have to say is that I am finished. I told you all I would walk away when I said so. I'm saying so."

"Marged."

"Don't." Her head came up. "Don't you dare stand in that woman's back room and *Marged* me. I have done this once before in my life, eleven years ago, and a man went into a wall then too, and I have spent every day since building a life in which that could not happen again, and it took me thirty six hours in your company to take it apart. So no. Never again. Not for the well, not for the village, not for a girl of seventeen, not for anything."

She picked up her bag.

"I'll say one more thing and then I'm going home. It didn't come apart because I set it badly." She looked round at them, and there was something underneath the control that Bel could not name. "It came apart because it was *pulling in four directions*, and I have never in my life felt a joining do that, and I want every one of you to think very hard about which direction was yours."

Then she went out, and they heard the front door, and then nothing.

---

"She's right," Wren said, after a while.

"About which part?"

"All of it, but particularly that." Wren had not moved from the wall. She had the drawn look of somebody running on nothing. "I felt it too. In the ninth second. It wasn't weak, it was *fighting itself.* Four things pulling apart."

"You'd know," said Nessa.

It came out of the corner of the room like a thrown stone, and everybody turned.

The girl was white to the lips and had been crying and had reached the point past crying where what is left is much colder.

"Nessa," said Bel.

"No, I want to say it, because you're all being very grown up about it and taking turns being sorry, and the actual truth is that we did it two nights after we met and one of us has been lying since the minute she got here." Nessa's chin was going. "You've had a bag packed by the door for eleven days. You measure it twice a day and you write it down and you don't tell anyone. You went white when she said *Hartsett*, I was there, I saw you. Everybody in this room has been very polite about not asking you what you took from Ashmoor and I'm seventeen so I don't have to be polite. What did you take?"

Wren Halloway did not answer.

That was the moment. Bel returned to it many times afterward, and it was always the same: not a denial, not an outburst, not a story. Two full seconds of a woman deciding not to speak.

"Right," said Bel, and something in her chest went cold and professional and almost restful, because at least this part she knew how to do. "Wren Halloway, I'm arresting you under section four of the Practitioners Act on suspicion of unauthorised removal of coven property. You do not have to say anything. Anything you do say may be recorded and given in evidence."

"You've got to be *joking*," said Nessa.

"I am not joking, and I would like you to remember, when you go back over this later, that I asked her a direct question on Wednesday evening and she has had four days."

"She's the only one of us who's been out on that green every single night."

"That," said Bel, "is not the point I would choose to make in her defence."

Wren still had not moved from the wall. She looked, Bel thought, less like a woman being arrested than like a woman hearing a diagnosis she had privately been expecting for some time.

"Where?" she said.

"The Ram's got a back room with a lock and Sheila's got the only key and I've asked her already." Bel picked up her notebook. "It's not a cell. I'm not pretending it's a cell. It's a room with a lock on it in a village with one road out, and if you want to walk out of it tonight you'll manage, and then I'll be after you again in the morning and we'll both be exactly where we were two years ago."

"And the well?"

"The well is a hole in the ground and Gil Tanner is in Kendal with a hole in his head," Bel said, "and I have been an officer for twenty one years and I do one thing at a time."

---

Sheila at the Ram took the news of her back room's new purpose with the equanimity of a woman who had run a pub for twenty two years.

"Is she dangerous?"

"No," said Bel.

"Is she going to be difficult?"

"Almost certainly not, and that's the part I like least."

Sheila handed over the key without further comment and went back to the barrels, and Bel stood in the corridor for a moment with a key in her hand, feeling the whole shape of her professional life bend quietly out of true.

Because the correct procedure, the one she had followed eleven times in twenty one years, was a transport request to the nearest Assize station and a formal handover within six hours. She had filed the transport request from her car at eight fifteen that morning. She had received an automated acknowledgement at eight sixteen, and nothing since, which had never once happened before in her career, and it was now past four in the afternoon.

She had checked the acknowledgement three times to be certain she had not misread the case reference.

She had not misread it.

---

Ada Fenwick, who had said nothing at all for forty minutes, spoke as they reached the door.

"Four and a half on Saturday morning," she said. "Six at three o'clock this morning. I've been out."

Bel stopped.

"That's an inch and a half in one night," Ada said. "It's been going a quarter of an inch a day since Tuesday. Last night it did six days' worth."

"Because of what we did."

"I've no idea why. I'm the one who writes it down." Ada closed the black ledger. "But I'll write that down too, and in eighty years' time somebody will read that page and think we were very stupid, and they'll be right."

---

At twenty past eleven that night the crack reached the road.

It did not crack the tarmac, which the highways report later found remarkable. It went under it. The surface of the lane subsided by two and a half inches over a stretch of four yards, in a straight line continuous with the line on the green, and by morning there was a barrier across it and a diversion sign at the top of the pass and a very tired man from the council photographing everything.

And at some point in the small hours, on the north face of the church, a single dressed stone came out of the wall about eight feet up and fell into the graveyard, and lay there in the wet grass among the Ridleys and the Sowerbys and the Fenwicks with eighty one years of moss still on one face of it.

Bel found it at seven, on her way to the Ram with two coffees and no idea what she intended to say.

She stood in the graveyard for a long moment looking at the hole it had left, a black gap in a wall that had stood since 1740, and then at the stone in the grass, and then at the names on the nearest headstones, which were the same names she had spent all week reading in a column headed KEPT.

*Four in the morning,* she thought. *And there's four of us, and one's under arrest, and one's gone home, and one's a child, and I'm the one who put the first two of those where they are.*
