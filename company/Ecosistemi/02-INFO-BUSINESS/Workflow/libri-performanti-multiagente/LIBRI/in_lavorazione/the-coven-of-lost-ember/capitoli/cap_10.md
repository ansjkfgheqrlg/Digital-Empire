# The Widow's Hands

Marged went up to the green at ten past seven on the Tuesday morning to prove a point to herself, and was still there at noon, which rather settled the point in the other direction.

The proposition had seemed reasonable at five in the morning, lying awake with the light coming grey round the curtains. She had said she was finished. She had meant it. And a woman who is finished with something ought to be able to stand within twelve feet of it without difficulty, in the same way that a woman who has genuinely given up cigarettes can sit in a pub garden next to somebody smoking. If she could not do that, then she was not finished, she was only abstaining, and Marged Pryce had never in her life had any patience for people who confused the two.

So she went up. And she stood at the low wall by the church path, which was twelve feet from the near end of the crack, with her hands in her cardigan pockets.

The tape was still across it. The council had put in two steel pins with a wire between them and a laminated card that said MONITORING POINT DO NOT DISTURB, and somebody from the highways department had chalked a number on the tarmac at the bottom, and there was a set of tyre tracks on the grass where a van had come up onto the green and been reversed off it again.

The crack itself was seven inches at the widest point.

She stood there.

At half past eight the school bus went through, slowly, over the subsided section of the lane, and eleven faces turned to look at the green out of the near side windows.

At nine Reverend Attwood came out and looked at the hole in his north wall for a while with his hands on his hips, in the attitude of a man doing arithmetic about a diocesan grant, and went back in.

At half past ten it rained for eleven minutes and then stopped.

And the whole time, the entire four hours and fifty minutes, Marged Pryce stood at the wall with her hands in her pockets and did not move, because the moment she relaxed her attention even slightly the working began to assemble itself behind her ribs, and she had to put it out, and putting it out required all of her, and so she stood there putting out the same small fire over and over for the better part of a morning.

It was, she thought, at about eleven o'clock, exactly like being an alcoholic left alone in a room with a bottle and no one to tell.

Except that was not quite it either, and she made herself be precise, because precision was the only thing she had left that she trusted.

An alcoholic in a room with a bottle wants the drink.

Marged did not want the working. She wanted to be *used*. That was the shameful thing, that was the thing she had never once said out loud in eleven years, not to the Reverend, not to her sister, not to the woman in Kendal she had gone to see four times in 2016 and had then stopped seeing. She did not miss magic. She missed being the one they came to. She missed the knock at ten at night and the man from Hexham with a jar wrapped in a tea towel, she missed being the person in this valley whose hands were worth waking up for, and she had spent eleven years understanding perfectly well that this was vanity and that it had killed her husband, and understanding it had not diminished it by one single ounce.

---

Ada Fenwick came at noon, with a folding stool.

She set it up beside Marged at the wall, unhurried, and sat down on it with a small grunt, and produced from her coat pocket two wrapped sandwiches and handed one over without asking whether Marged wanted it.

"You've been here since seven," Ada said.

"How would you know that?"

"I live in a village of four hundred people, Marged, and I've been keeping its records for fifty one years." Ada unwrapped her sandwich. "Sheila saw you at twenty past. She told Gil's wife on the phone from the hospital at half eight. Gil's wife told the Reverend, who came out at nine and looked at his wall so that he could look at you and then decided not to. Alan Beaton has driven past twice."

Marged took a bite of the sandwich because it was easier than arguing.

They sat and stood in silence for a while.

"I'll tell you a thing," Ada said, "and then I'll go, because I can see you don't want company and I've never let that stop me. Do you know what happened at Owen's funeral?"

"I was there."

"You were not." Ada said it without any cruelty at all. "You were in the second pew and your eyes were open, and I have been to a great many funerals and I know the difference. You were not there, and while you were not there, a decision got made about you, and I watched it happen, and it took about four minutes."

Marged said nothing.

"It was in the hall afterward, over the ham. Peg Ridley said something to Dorothy Beaton about how it was a mercy you'd been there, because at least Owen hadn't been on his own. And Dorothy said, and I heard this with my own ears, *poor Marged, she'll never touch it again after this.*" Ada folded her sandwich paper into a small precise square. "And that was it. That's all it took. By the Wednesday it was what everybody knew, and by the following month it was what everybody had always known, and I don't believe a single person in this village has asked you a question about it from that day to this."

"They were being kind."

"They were being *comfortable*," said Ada. "There's a great overlap and it's not the same thing. A question would have meant hearing an answer, and nobody in that hall wanted an answer, because the answer might have had a piece of the village in it."

Marged found that she was gripping the top of the wall with one hand.

"You've had eleven years," Ada went on, "of a whole village agreeing not to ask you anything, and you've called it kindness and I expect you've been grateful for it, and I'd like to point out, as the person who writes things down, that it is also the single most convenient arrangement I have ever seen a village come to. Nobody has to know. And you never have to say."

"Ada."

"I'm going." She stood up, with difficulty, and folded the stool. "One more and I'm done. That book of mine has twenty six names in it and I have read them all a thousand times, and there is not one entry in a hundred and fifty one years that says *worked well.* Not one. They wrote *kept*. That's the whole word. Not *did it beautifully*, not *did it without anybody getting hurt*, not *deserved to.* Kept."

She tucked the stool under her arm.

"You're the only person left alive in this valley who was taught properly," Ada said. "That's not a compliment, it's an inconvenience, and I'm sorry it landed on you and not on somebody who'd enjoy it. But it did land on you. And whatever you decide, I'd rather you decided it because of what's true and not because of a sentence Dorothy Beaton said over a plate of ham in 2014."

She went off down the path toward the lane.

Marged Pryce stayed at the wall for another forty minutes.

Then she went home, and got out the tin box from the bottom of the wardrobe that she had not opened in eleven years, and sat with it closed on her knees for a very long time, and did not open it that day either.

But she brought it downstairs, and put it on the kitchen table, and left it there.
