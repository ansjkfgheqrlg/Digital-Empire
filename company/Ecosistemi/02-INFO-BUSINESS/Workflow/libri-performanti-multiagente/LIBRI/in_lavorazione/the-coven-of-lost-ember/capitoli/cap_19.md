# Marged Says It

Marged Pryce went home first.

Nobody stopped her. She said she needed an hour and would come back at ten, and she walked down the lane past the barrier with her hands in her cardigan pockets, and let herself into her own house, and stood in the kitchen looking at the tin box that had been sitting on the table since the Tuesday.

Eleven years.

She had rehearsed this. That was the thing nobody would ever believe. She had rehearsed it in this kitchen perhaps two hundred times, at three in the morning, in the years when she was not sleeping: the words in order, the tone, the face she would make. She had rehearsed it for the Reverend and for her sister and for the woman in Kendal, and she had got as far as opening her mouth exactly twice, and both times what had come out was something about the weather.

She opened the tin box.

Inside was her grandmother's working knife with the horn handle, and a bundle of her mother's notes in a rubber band that had perished and left a brown mark on the top sheet, and a photograph of Owen at thirty in a boat he did not own, and under all of it, folded in four, the sheet of paper she had written on the fourteenth of October eleven years ago, three days after, when she had been unable to sleep and had believed she was going to tell somebody.

She read it. It took about forty seconds. Then she folded it back along the same creases and put it in her cardigan pocket and went back up the lane.

---

They were all still in Ada's kitchen. Nobody had moved much. Somebody had made more tea.

Marged sat down in the same chair and put the folded paper on the table in front of her without opening it.

"Right," she said. "Owen."

She had thought she would need the paper. She did not touch it once.

"The village has a story and you'll all have heard it, because everybody has. Owen Pryce took on a working beyond himself out of pride, his wife was there and couldn't stop him, and afterwards she put it all down and never picked it up again, poor woman, and hasn't she been marvellous about it." Marged's hands were quite steady. "Every single part of that is nearly true. That's what makes it work."

"Marged," said Bel. "You don't have to do it as a performance. Just say it."

"I do have to do it in order, because if I don't I'll arrange it." She took a breath. "It was the fourteenth of October. There was a thing in the field above the Ridley place, an old cattle drove that had gone bad, and it needed a binding, and it was a two person job and there were two of us. It wasn't beyond him. It wasn't beyond either of us. We'd done bigger."

"What went wrong?"

"I did," said Marged.

She let that sit for a second, because she had learned in two hundred rehearsals that if she ran past it she would never come back to it.

"A binding like that has one person holding it open and one person laying the line. Owen held. He always held, he had better hands for it and less imagination, which is what you want in the one who holds. I laid." She looked at the middle of the table. "And the last line of a binding closes on itself, and there are two ways to bring it round, and the short way is faster and the long way is safer, and I had done the short way perhaps forty times in my life and never once had a problem with it, and I was cold, and it was half past nine at night, and I wanted my tea."

Nobody said anything at all.

"I brought it round the short way and I brought it round wrong," said Marged Pryce. "By about a hand's width. And it closed on him instead of on itself."

---

"He was alive for eleven minutes," she said. "That's the bit I've never said out loud to anybody in eleven years, and I'd like everybody at this table to notice that I've just said it and that the roof hasn't come in."

Ada put a hand flat on the table near hers, not touching it.

"He knew," Marged said. "That's the other bit. He was perfectly clear the entire time and he knew exactly what had happened, because he'd have felt it come round wrong before I did, he always felt things first. And he had eleven minutes, and he did not use one second of them to say what I'd done."

"What did he say?"

"He said, *tell them I was holding.*" Marged's voice went, finally, on the last word, and she stopped and waited for it and went on. "Which was true. He was holding. He was holding beautifully. He'd have held it all night if I'd laid the thing properly, and he knew I hadn't, and with the time he had left he chose the one sentence that was true and that would let the whole valley build the wrong story out of it."

"He was protecting you," said Nessa, very quietly.

"He was giving me a door," said Marged, "and I walked through it. That's the part I need said correctly, because everybody always wants to make it about him being noble, and it lets me off. He gave me a door. Nobody made me walk through it. At the funeral Dorothy Beaton said *poor Marged, she'll never touch it again after this*, and I heard her say it, and I could have opened my mouth in that hall, and instead I let a hundred and forty people in a village decide something about my husband that was not true, and I have let them go on deciding it for eleven years, every day, including days when his sister has said to my face that he could be reckless."

---

"That's why I stopped," she said, after a while. "Not grief. Everybody's very comfortable with grief, it's tidy, it explains a woman going quiet. I stopped because the working is the only thing in the world that can tell when you're lying, and I had one enormous lie in me, and I could not hold a line with three other people without them feeling it."

"You held one with us," said Wren. "Twice."

"And it came apart both times in exactly the same place," said Marged, "and I want you all to be very clear that a good deal of that place was me."

"Not all of it," said Bel.

"No. Not all of it. That's rather the point of this morning." Marged picked up the folded paper at last, and looked at it, and did not open it. "I wrote this three days after, when I still thought I was going to tell somebody. It's four lines and it says what I've just said. I've carried it from that kitchen to this one and I've never shown it to a living soul, and now I've said it out loud instead, which is better, and I find I don't want the paper any more."

She put it in the middle of the table.

"Ada. You keep things. Keep that."

Ada Fenwick took it without a word and put it inside the front board of the eighteen thirty nine book, where it stayed.

---

Afterward, when Bel had gone out to make a telephone call and Nessa had gone to the tap for water and the kitchen had that loosened feeling a room gets when something has finished happening in it, Wren came and sat down next to her.

"Can I say one thing," Wren said, "and then I'll leave you alone."

"You can say it."

"You laid a line wrong at half past nine at night after forty successful ones, when you were cold and you wanted your tea." Wren's voice was low. "At Ashmoor there is a word for that and the word is *accident*, and it is used four or five times a year, and nobody has ever once had to leave the craft over it."

"He died."

"Yes," said Wren. "And the reason he died is that a binding of that size is a two person job in a book written in eighteen thirty nine, when people were three inches shorter and the field wall was in a different place, and there has not been a coven in this valley since nineteen forty four to notice that the book might be wrong."

Marged sat with that.

"That is very kind," she said, "and it is not the same as being true."

"It's exactly as true as the other thing," said Wren, "and you've had eleven years of only telling yourself one of them."

Marged Pryce did not answer.

But at eleven o'clock, when Ada said they should try to do this properly and asked who was going next, Marged found that she had put her hand flat on the table beside Wren's without deciding to, and that it stayed there.
