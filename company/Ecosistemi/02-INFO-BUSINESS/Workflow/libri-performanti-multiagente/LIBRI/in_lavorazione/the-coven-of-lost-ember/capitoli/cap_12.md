# The Second Attempt

They did everything right the second time, which was what made it unbearable.

Wren had insisted on the list. She had written it out on the back of an envelope in the Ram on the Tuesday night and had gone through it with the other three at Ada's on the Wednesday morning, item by item, and had refused to be hurried, and by the end of it even Marged had stopped looking at the door.

Daylight, not two in the morning. Fed, all four of them, properly, at Ada's table. Nobody within a hundred yards: Bel had walked the lane and spoken to Sheila and to the Reverend and to the man from highways, and the green had been cleared and the lane closed at both ends with two of Gil's cones and a piece of paper that said COUNCIL WORKS in Bel's handwriting, which was a lie, and which Bel had committed with a steadiness that Wren privately found impressive.

Positions rehearsed dry, three times, without any working at all, until all four of them could walk to their points in the dark.

Nessa's hands off the ground, agreed in advance, and agreed again out loud on the green, and Nessa saying, in a flat voice, "I know. I've said I know. I'm not going to."

And the thread laid slowly, in daylight, at four in the afternoon on the first Wednesday in October, with Ada Fenwick sitting on her folding stool by the church wall holding the marbled book open on her knees.

There was one argument, and it was about Ada.

"You sit at the wall," Bel said. "Not on the green."

"I have sat on that wall for the last two and I shall sit on it for this one."

"Ada, on Saturday night a man twenty five feet outside the circle went into stonework."

"Then you had better do it properly this time," said Ada Fenwick, and set up her folding stool, and that was the end of the argument.

Wren watched Marged look at the old woman for a moment and decide, visibly, not to spend anything on it. It was the first time she had seen Marged Pryce concede a point to somebody who was simply more stubborn than she was, and she filed it away as useful.

At four o'clock the light was flat and yellow and there was no wind at all. Bel had walked the lane twice. The Ram was shut. Somewhere over toward the Crozier place a dog was going up and down a fence and would not stop, and all four of them heard it and nobody mentioned it.

"Right," said Marged. "One thing before I start, and I want it said out loud rather than assumed. If this works, the crack closes and we have all had a very interesting fortnight and we go back to our lives. If it does not work, we will know something we do not know now, and that is also worth having. What we are not doing is standing here hoping. Hope is not a method."

"Is that from the book?" said Nessa.

"That is from my grandmother," said Marged, "who would have had all four of us out of this circle by teatime for talking during the setting."

It caught in eleven seconds and it held for four minutes and eleven seconds.

---

Wren had worked bindings at Ashmoor from the age of fourteen. She had worked in circles of six and circles of nine and once, memorably and horribly, in a circle of twenty two. She knew what a joining felt like when it was going well.

This went well.

That was the thing she could not get past afterward. For four minutes it was *good*. The four of them locked in and the shape came up clean and Wren felt the whole green come into focus underneath her the way a picture comes into focus, and she could feel the other three the way you feel people in a dark room, their edges, their weight: Marged very close and very controlled, holding the spine with an economy that told Wren more about who Marged Pryce had been than eleven days of conversation had; Nessa off to her left, vast and completely untaught and thrumming like a struck wire; Bel behind, dogged, unpractised, absolutely refusing to let go of a thing she did not understand.

And under them, through the turf, the long slow rhythm that Wren had sat in a culvert on Tuesday afternoon and wept at.

For four minutes the crack narrowed. Wren could see it going. It came in from seven inches to something under five along its whole length, and Ada, from the wall, said "It's closing" in a voice with something in it that none of them had heard from her before.

And then, at four minutes and eleven seconds, it went.

It did not tear. It did not snap. It came apart along a seam, the way a badly sewn hem comes apart, and it came apart in exactly the same place it had come apart on Saturday night: the same four directions, the same pull, the same four things dragging against each other in a shape that was supposed to be one thing.

Wren felt it go and threw everything she had into holding it and it went anyway, and the whole working slid apart and grounded itself out harmlessly into the turf, and the four of them stood on a village green in the daylight with their hands out and nothing between them at all.

Nobody was hurt.

That was the part.

---

"Right," said Bel, after a while. "Somebody say something, because I've got nothing."

Nobody said anything.

Marged sat down on the wall. She did it slowly and carefully, the way people do when they have decided not to let anybody see that their legs have gone.

"Nobody's hurt," Nessa offered.

"Yes," said Marged.

"That's good. Isn't that good?"

"It's the worst thing that could have happened," Marged said.

Nessa looked at her.

"On Saturday we could tell ourselves it was the hour," Marged said. "It was two in the morning, we'd never met, we'd not rehearsed, you put your hands down when you'd been told not to, and a man was standing at a gate where nobody should have been standing. Every one of those is a reason, and any one of them could have been the reason." She looked up at the three of them. "Today there were none of those. We ate a meal at that woman's table. We rehearsed it dry three times. It was four in the afternoon. Nobody's hands were on the ground and nobody was within a hundred yards, and it came apart in exactly the same place after four minutes instead of nine seconds."

"So we're better at it," said Bel.

"We're *considerably* better at it," Marged agreed. "That's my point. We have removed every single external reason, and the fault is still there, and it is in exactly the same place. Which means the fault is not in how we're doing it."

"Then where is it?"

Marged did not answer that.

Wren, who had known where it was since about the third minute, and who had felt it come from her own point in the circle as clearly as she had felt it come from the other three, did not answer either.

---

Ada closed the book on her knees.

"Come and look at this," she said. "All of you. Now, while it's fresh, because I have had a thought and I don't trust my head to keep it past teatime."

They came and stood round the folding stool.

The marbled book was open at the diagram, and Ada had her finger on the outer margin, on a line of brown ink so faded and so small that Wren, who had looked at this page perhaps thirty times in eight days, had taken it for a smudge.

"I've been reading this page for fifty one years," Ada said, "and I have always taken that for foxing. But I sat here for four minutes and eleven seconds with nothing to do but hold the book, and I put my glasses on it."

She turned the stool so the light came over her shoulder.

It was a hand. Not the copperplate of the main text. Something later, smaller, crabbed, written by somebody in a hurry with a worse pen, running up the outer margin beside the diagram.

Wren crouched down and read it twice before it went in.

*it will not knit where a thread is held back*
