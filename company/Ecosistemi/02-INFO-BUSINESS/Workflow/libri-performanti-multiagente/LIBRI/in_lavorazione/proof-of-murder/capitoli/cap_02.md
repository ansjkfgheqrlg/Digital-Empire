# The Card in His Pocket

By seven o'clock the alley behind the bakery looked like a movie set: two county cruisers, an unmarked sedan, a white van from the medical examiner's office in Montpelier, and a knot of forensic techs in blue paper booties picking their way across the frost with the particular unhurried care of people who did this for a living and had learned not to rush it. Maggie sat at her own dining room table, three doors down in the little apartment above the bakery that she had painted a soft buttery yellow just that spring, and watched through the front window as her neighbors gathered on the sidewalk in twos and threes, coffee mugs in hand, not quite looking at her window and not quite looking away from it either.

Chief Bill Torres let himself in without knocking, which she supposed was his right, given everything, and settled his considerable bulk into the chair across from her with a small grunt, setting a manila folder on the table between them like a man laying down cards he already knew were winning.

"Rough morning," he said.

"Yes."

"You want coffee? I could send someone down for coffee."

"I want to know what happened to Harold Pruitt in my kitchen."

Torres had a broad, sun spotted face and pale blue eyes that had gone flat and careful the moment he walked in, and Maggie remembered, with a small cold jolt, that this was the same Bill Torres who had come to her mother's funeral three years ago and stood at the back of the church the whole service, never once coming up to pay respects at the casket, though he'd been the one to sign off, in the end, on calling Rose Doyle's death a heart attack and closing the file inside of a week.

"We'll get there," he said. "First I need you to walk me through your morning. Every minute of it, from when you woke up."

She did. The alarm at three fifty. The drive over. The stuck key. The lights. The open drawer. Her voice stayed steadier than she expected, though her hands, wrapped around a mug of tea gone cold, would not stop trembling no matter how tightly she held on.

"And last night," Torres said. "You locked up yourself?"

"I always lock up myself. Front and back, both deadbolts, I checked them twice because of the festival stock in the walk in."

"What time was that?"

"Nine thirty. Maybe quarter to ten. Josie can tell you, she was with me until closing."

"Anybody have a key besides you and Josie Alvarez?"

"No. I had the locks changed when I bought the place from the estate." She heard her own voice catch on estate, the clean legal word for what had actually happened, which was that her mother had died and left behind a bakery mortgaged twice over and a daughter three hundred miles away in Boston who had come home to bury her and never quite managed to leave again.

Torres wrote something down, unhurried, and then opened the manila folder.

"I want to show you something," he said, "and I need you to look at it careful before you say anything."

He slid a clear plastic evidence sleeve across the table. Inside it was a recipe card, index sized, cream colored, the kind that came a hundred to a box from the hardware store, and it was stained dark along one long edge in a way that made Maggie's stomach turn over slow and sick, because she understood exactly what kind of stain did that to paper, and it was not cinnamon.

The handwriting on the card, though. That she would have known anywhere, in any light, on any surface. Her mother's hand had a particular slant to it, the capital letters a little too large, the lowercase g's looped twice like a schoolgirl's, and Rose had written out every recipe in this exact hand for thirty years, taped to the inside of cabinet doors, tucked into cookbooks, filed by the hundreds in the battered wooden box that still sat on the counter downstairs, sealed now behind yellow tape.

"Where did you find this," Maggie said, and her voice had gone very quiet.

"In Harold Pruitt's coat pocket. Inside pocket, folded twice." Torres watched her while he said it, his pale eyes steady on her face in a way that made the hair rise on her arms, and she understood, with a lurch, that this was not idle information. This was a test. "That your mother's writing?"

"You know it's my mother's writing. You've seen a hundred of her recipe cards, Bill, she used to bring you a plate of her shortbread every Christmas for fifteen years."

"I need you to say it for the record, Maggie."

"Yes. That's my mother's handwriting."

"Recognize the recipe?"

She made herself look at it properly, past the stain, past the dread pooling low in her stomach. Molasses Spice Cookies, the top of the card read, in Rose's careful slant, and below it a list of ingredients Maggie could have recited in her sleep, dark molasses and clove and a pinch of black pepper that always made people ask what her mother's secret was. It was a recipe Maggie had made a hundred times herself. It was also, she saw now, a card she had not seen in the recipe box in longer than she could place, though that meant nothing, there were four hundred cards in that box and she'd only owned the place three months.

"It's her molasses cookies," she said. "It's one of maybe two hundred cards in her recipe box. I don't understand why Harold would have it in his pocket."

"Neither do I," Torres said. "Yet."

"You think I gave it to him."

"I didn't say that."

"You're watching my face like you said that."

Something flickered behind Torres's flat eyes, not quite embarrassment, more like a man recalculating how much rope to give. "Here's what I know for a fact," he said. "Harold Pruitt was found dead in your kitchen at four thirty this morning, in a building only you and your assistant have keys to, with no sign of forced entry on either door. He's got one of your mother's recipe cards folded up in his coat like it mattered enough to carry around. And the coroner's van just left with a body that's going to tell us a lot more once they get him on a table in Montpelier. I've got a dead man, a locked building, and a piece of paper with your mother's name all over it in everything but ink. You want to tell me I shouldn't be asking questions?"

"I want to tell you I didn't do this." Maggie's voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it, hated that he could see it crack, hated that three months into owning this place she was sitting in her own dining room defending herself against a word neither of them had said out loud yet. "I want to tell you that whatever happened to Harold Pruitt, it did not happen because of me, and I would like very much to know why you're more interested in watching my face than in finding out who else might have had a reason to walk into my bakery last night."

Torres held her gaze a moment longer, then slid the evidence sleeve back into the folder and closed it, and something in the gesture felt final, like a door shutting on a room she hadn't finished searching. "Nobody's charging anybody with anything," he said, in the mild, unbothered voice of a man used to being obeyed rather than believed. "We're going to process the scene, we're going to run the card for prints, and we're going to talk to everybody who saw Harold yesterday. That includes you. It'll include a lot of people before we're done."

Through the window behind him, Maggie could see Nate Coleman standing at the mouth of the alley, arms folded, saying something low to one of the techs, and even from here she could read the tension in the line of his shoulders, the careful, deliberate way he was not looking up at her window. She thought of the two of them at seventeen on the loading dock, splitting a cruller, certain nothing bad could ever really touch them here.

"Bill," she said, before he could push back from the table, "was there anything else on him? In his pockets, I mean."

Torres paused, halfway to standing, and for just a second something passed over his broad face that might have been calculation, or might have been simple fatigue. "Wallet. Keys. A committee binder with the festival schedule in it." He said it too smooth, too fast, the way a man says a short list when there is a longer one he isn't ready to share. "Nothing that concerns you."

"Everything in my kitchen concerns me."

"Not anymore it doesn't," Torres said, not unkindly, and let himself out, leaving the manila folder's shadow burned into her mind long after the door had clicked shut behind him: her mother's careful, looping handwriting, stained dark along one edge, folded twice and carried close to a dead man's heart.
