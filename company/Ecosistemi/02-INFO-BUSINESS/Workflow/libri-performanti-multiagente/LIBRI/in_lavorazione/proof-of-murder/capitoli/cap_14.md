# A Question of Cardiology

Dr. Alan Ferris had been Rose Doyle's physician for eleven years, and he remembered her well enough that his voice caught slightly over the phone when Maggie explained what she needed.

"I still think about her," he admitted. "She used to bring the whole waiting room molasses cookies at Christmas. Broke about a dozen HIPAA posters worth of small talk with every patient in there." He sighed. "You want her file released, you'll need to sign the request as next of kin, and I'll need to pull the physical chart, we hadn't fully digitized back then. Give me until tomorrow afternoon."

Nate drove her to the medical building himself, off duty, in his own truck instead of the cruiser, which Maggie understood without being told was its own kind of statement. They sat across from Dr. Ferris's cluttered desk while he laid the folder open between them, Rose's handwriting nowhere in it, only nurses' notes and lab results and the doctor's own cramped shorthand going back over a decade.

"Here's her blood pressure history," Ferris said, running a finger down a column of numbers. "Mild hypertension, diagnosed about six years before she passed. Nothing alarming. We had her on a low dose of lisinopril, adjusted once when her numbers crept up a little, but she responded well. Look here." He tapped a page dated four months before Rose's death. "Last full physical. EKG normal. Cholesterol fine. No arrhythmia, no history of it in her family that she ever reported, no chest pain, no shortness of breath, nothing that would have made me worry about a cardiac event."

"So she wasn't at risk," Maggie said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to.

"Not from anything I could see. Which isn't nothing, sudden cardiac events happen to healthy people, it's part of why they're called sudden. But." He hesitated, closing the folder halfway. "I'll be honest with you, Maggie, at the time I signed off on it because it fit a pattern I'd seen before. Woman in her fifties, mild hypertension, no autopsy requested by the family because there was no reason to suspect foul play. It gets called a cardiac event and everyone moves on. If someone had asked me to look harder, I might have. Nobody asked."

Nate leaned forward. "Doctor, is there anything about her medication, the lisinopril, that could interact badly with something else? Anything that could push a controlled condition into a fatal one?"

Ferris was quiet for a moment, the kind of quiet that told Maggie he'd already turned the question over in his own mind more than once since her call. "Blood pressure medications are forgiving in normal doses. But get the dosage wrong, badly wrong, or combine it with the wrong thing, and you can trigger a cardiac event that looks, on the surface, exactly like the one that killed your mother. No obvious signs unless somebody's specifically testing for it after the fact. And by the time anyone would have thought to test, she'd already been embalmed."

Maggie sat with that in the car the whole way home, Nate driving in silence beside her, the hills going gold and rust past the windows, festival banners already strung between the lampposts on Main Street for an event that suddenly felt very far away and very close at the same time.

"I need her pharmacy records," Maggie said finally. "Whoever filled that prescription would know if anything was off."

"One thing at a time," Nate said, but he was already nodding, already thinking two steps ahead the way he did. "I'll ask around who filled prescriptions in town back then. Let's get through tonight first."

She didn't know yet how wrong that would turn out to be.

---

Maggie closed the bakery at seven, same as always, wiped down the counters, ran the register tape, counted the drawer into the bank bag and locked it in the small safe under the front counter the way she'd done every night since reopening. Josie left around eight to meet a friend, though something in the way she said it made Maggie suspect the friend had a last name that started with Finch, not that she pushed. Maggie herself went upstairs a little after nine, tired in a way that had nothing to do with flour and everything to do with the folder still sitting on her kitchen table with her mother's name typed across the tab.

She woke a little after one in the morning to a sound that didn't belong in the building. Not the settling creak of old joists she'd long since learned to sleep through, but a sharper sound, wood against wood, something being pried.

She lay still in the dark and listened. It came again, downstairs, from the direction of the kitchen.

Her phone was on the nightstand. She texted Nate with fingers that wouldn't quite stop shaking, someone's in the bakery, and then, because some instinct older than fear told her not to go down there alone, she sat on the edge of her bed and waited, straining to hear over the pounding of her own pulse.

Nate called back in under a minute, his voice low and urgent. "Stay upstairs. Lock your door. I'm three minutes out, I'm calling it in on the way."

The sounds from downstairs went on for what felt like much longer than three minutes, a rustle of paper, something heavy dragged across the floor, a soft, systematic sound that was somehow worse than a violent one, because it told her whoever was down there wasn't panicking, wasn't in a hurry. They knew exactly what they were looking for and were taking their time to find it.

Then, all at once, silence. A door easing shut, not slammed. Gone.

She heard Nate's truck in the alley before she heard the sirens, heard him call her name up through the stairwell door, and only then did she trust her legs enough to go down.

The kitchen looked like a small storm had passed through it, but a strange, selective storm. The recipe box sat overturned on the worktable, cards scattered across the stainless steel and the floor in a wide, careless fan, some of them face up, some bent, one soaked through with what turned out to be spilled vanilla extract from a bottle knocked from the shelf above. The back door stood ajar, its old lock, the one Harold Pruitt had a spare key to, splintered clean out of the frame.

But the register drawer, sitting open beneath the front counter where Maggie always left it unlocked and empty overnight precisely so a break in wouldn't cost her a broken drawer along with whatever cash was inside, sat completely untouched. The bank bag was still in the safe. Nobody had so much as glanced at it.

Nate stood in the doorway taking it in, his face gone hard and still in a way she hadn't seen since the morning he'd found Harold's body. "They weren't here for money," he said.

"No," Maggie said, kneeling slowly beside the scattered cards, her mother's handwriting staring up at her from a dozen different angles, the ordinary and the coded mixed together now in a mess that would take hours to sort. "They were here for something specific. And I don't think they were alone in wanting it."

Behind them, out on the street, the first blue light of a second patrol car swept silently across the paper covered windows, and somewhere above the ridge line the sky was already beginning, faintly, to gray toward morning.
