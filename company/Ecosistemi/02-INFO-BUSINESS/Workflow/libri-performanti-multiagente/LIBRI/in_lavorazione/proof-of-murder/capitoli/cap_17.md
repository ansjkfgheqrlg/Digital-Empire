# Closing Ranks

The Grange hall smelled like old coffee and fresh paint, and someone had propped the double doors open with folding chairs so the evening air could cut through the crowd. Maggie stood near the back with Josie, arms crossed, watching the committee members file up to the long table at the front like they were taking their seats in a courtroom. She had almost not come. Then she had thought of Rose, of the recipe cards, of Denise Cobb's face closing shut over that green ledger, and she had put on a clean apron shirt and walked over anyway.

"Half this town thinks you did it," Josie murmured, "and you're standing in the front row like you own the place."

"I'm standing where I can see faces," Maggie said. "That's different."

Denise Cobb sat at the center of the table, a navy blazer buttoned over a blouse the color of a robin's egg, her hair sprayed into place despite the humidity. She had a gavel, an actual small wooden gavel, and she used it now, three sharp raps that silenced the murmuring room.

"I want to thank everyone for coming tonight," Denise said, her voice pitched for the back row. "I know it's been a difficult week for Cedar Hollow. A terrible, terrible week. Harold was a friend to all of us, and his loss is felt by this whole committee personally."

A murmur of assent rolled through the folding chairs. Diane Pruitt was not in the room, Maggie noted. She would have had every right to sit at that table and did not.

"But," Denise continued, and here her hands came together, fingers laced, the gesture of a woman steadying herself, "Founder's Day is five days away. Vendors have contracts. The town has expectations. And I believe, I truly believe, that the very best way we can honor Harold's memory is to give Cedar Hollow the festival he spent thirty years building. We cannot let one tragedy, however painful, undo three decades of tradition."

Walter Finch sat two seats down from her, a legal pad in front of him that he had not written on. Maggie watched him look at his own hands.

A voice rose from the third row, an older man in a canvas barn coat, one of the town councilmen, Ed Whitcombe, who ran the feed store and had known Rose Doyle for forty years. "Denise, before we get to festival logistics, I'd like an update on the rezoning question. It's been on the agenda for three years running now and every time it comes up, it gets tabled. Folks are asking."

The room did not go silent so much as it seemed to inhale.

"Ed, that's not really festival business," Denise said, and her smile did not move but something behind her eyes did, a small flinch, quickly smoothed. "The rezoning proposal for the north parcel is a separate matter entirely, and it's been under review by the county."

"Under review for three years," Ed said, not unkindly, just stubborn, the way men who have farmed the same land their whole lives are stubborn. "Seems like an awful long review for a piece of paper. And I heard the appraisal on that parcel's changed twice since Rose Doyle asked for an audit, God rest her."

The mention of her mother's name in that room, spoken so plainly, sent something cold down Maggie's spine. She saw Denise's hand tighten on the gavel handle, saw the knuckles go pale.

"Rose's request was addressed at the time," Denise said, too quickly. "I don't think tonight is the appropriate venue to relitigate committee history. We have vendors waiting on confirmation, we have the parade route to finalize, we have the ribbon cutting schedule to lock down. I'd ask that we table the rezoning question again and revisit it after the festival, when cooler heads can look at it properly."

"Table it again," Ed repeated, but he let it go, the way people in small towns let things go when they sense the room wants them to, and Maggie felt a flash of frustration so sharp it nearly brought her to her feet. She stayed seated. Watching. That was what she had come to do.

Walter Finch's pen moved now, aimless circles on the legal pad, and Maggie realized she'd been staring at him. He caught her eye across the room, held it for one beat too long, and looked away.

The meeting rolled on through parade routes and vendor slots and a squabble over whether the Ferris wheel company from Rutland could get their trucks through Main Street before eight in the morning. Denise handled it all with the same bright, brittle competence, but Maggie noticed she never fully unclenched again. Every time a new topic came up, her eyes flicked first to the door, as if she expected someone else to walk in and say something she couldn't control.

When the gavel came down for the last time and folding chairs began scraping back across the linoleum, Maggie stood and let the crowd carry her toward the exit, half listening to two women in front of her debating whether the bake sale table should go near the gazebo or the fire station.

Outside, the parking lot had gone the deep blue of early dusk, gravel crunching under boots, headlights swinging in slow arcs as people pulled out. Maggie found her car near the tree line and was digging her keys from her bag when a voice stopped her.

"Maggie. Wait."

Walter Finch stood a few paces off, his suit jacket over one arm, looking like a man who had aged five years in the last week. His car, a silver sedan too clean for a Vermont dirt lot, was parked two spaces from hers, but he hadn't gone to it.

"Walter." She kept her keys in her hand, angled slightly, the small defensive habit of a woman who had learned to be careful in empty parking lots. "You've been busy lately. Surveys, appraisals. I saw them on Denise's desk."

His mouth twitched, something between a wince and a smile that didn't reach his eyes. "You shouldn't have seen those."

"I wasn't looking for them. They were just there."

He glanced back toward the hall, where the last of the committee members were locking up, then took a step closer, lowering his voice even though there was no one left to hear. "Maggie, I need you to listen to me, and I need you to actually hear what I'm saying, not what you think I'm saying."

"I'm listening."

"Stop digging." He said it fast, like he'd been holding the words behind his teeth all evening and they'd finally broken loose. "Whatever you and that deputy have been doing, poking around Harold, poking around your mother's old ledgers, I'm asking you to stop. Not because I want to hide something from you. Because I don't want anyone else to get hurt."

Maggie's pulse ticked up. "Anyone else. Meaning Harold already did."

"I'm not saying that." He ran a hand through his hair, and it came away trembling slightly. "I'm not saying anything, that's the whole point, I've spent three years not saying anything, and look where that got Harold. Look where it got your mother." He caught himself, heard his own words land, and his face went the color of old paper.

"Say that again," Maggie said quietly. "Say what you just said about my mother."

"I didn't mean." He stopped. Started over. "I meant generally. Everybody in this town who tangles with the wrong things ends up hurt, that's all I meant."

"You don't sound like a man giving me a warning, Walter." Maggie's voice was low but it did not shake. "You sound like a man who's scared."

His jaw worked, and for a moment she thought he might actually tell her, might let the whole thing spill out right there between a silver sedan and a stand of maples going orange at the edges. Then a car door slammed somewhere near the hall, and he flinched like he'd been struck, and the moment closed over like water.

"I have debts," he said instead, so quietly she had to lean in to catch it. "I have debts that this land deal was supposed to clear, and if it falls apart because of an investigation, I lose everything I've built. That's all I'll say. But Maggie, please. For your own sake. Let the state police handle Harold. Let them handle whatever happened to Rose. Don't be the one who forces it into the open."

"Someone has to," she said. "Nobody else in this town seems willing."

"Then let it not be you." His eyes were wet now, and he looked, for just a second, like an old man instead of the sharp-suited developer who'd sat calm as glass through committee meetings for years. "I have known Denise Cobb for eleven years. I have done business with her, made money with her, and I am telling you, as someone who has watched her operate up close, that she does not lose. Not gracefully. Not ever. Whatever she's protecting, she will protect it."

"Is that a threat, Walter?"

"No." He shook his head hard, like the idea itself frightened him. "God, no. It's the opposite of a threat. It's me telling you to be careful of someone who scares me, and I don't scare easy."

He turned before she could answer, walked quickly to his car, and pulled out of the lot with his headlights cutting twin trails through the settling dark, gravel popping under his tires. Maggie stood alone by the tree line for a long moment, the cold work of his words settling into her chest.

Josie came jogging up from the hall, breath fogging faintly in the cooling air. "What did Walter want? You looked like you'd seen a ghost."

"He wanted to warn me off," Maggie said slowly. "But Josie, he wasn't threatening me. He was scared. Scared for me, maybe. Or scared of what happens if I keep going." She looked back toward the Grange hall, dark now except for a single light in the office window where Denise Cobb still sat, a shadow bent over her desk. "He said Denise doesn't lose gracefully. Not ever."

Josie followed her gaze to that lit window. "So what do we do with that?"

Maggie unlocked her car, but she didn't get in right away. "We find something that makes her lose anyway."
