# The Unveiling

The morning fog burned off Cedar Hollow by ten, and by noon the town green looked like something off a postcard nobody would believe was real. Hay bales stacked at the corners of Main Street wore pumpkins like crowns. The high school brass band ran through "Turkey in the Straw" for the third time, slightly better each pass. Booths sold cider donuts and corn dogs and hand-carved birdhouses, and somewhere past the gazebo a dunk tank sloshed every few minutes to a burst of laughter.

Maggie stood behind the long folding table that had been dragged out from the Grange hall, staring at the sheet covering the festival cake, and tried to remember how to breathe like a person who wasn't about to walk into a trap she'd built herself.

"You look like you're about to be sick," Josie murmured beside her, adjusting the strap of her apron. "Please don't be sick. Not on the cake."

"I'm not going to be sick."

"You're a little green."

"I'm fine." Maggie smoothed her hands down her skirt. Under the table, out of sight, her knees were shaking in a way that had nothing to do with nerves about frosting. Three days ago she'd sat across a folding table from Priya Adjei in the back room of the state police barracks in Rutland, laying out recipe cards and torn ledger pages and a hospital chart that shouldn't have existed the way it did, and Priya had listened to all of it with a stillness that told Maggie she was already halfway to believing her.

"If Denise Cobb makes a run at you today," Priya had said, tapping a pen against the table, "she's going to do it when she thinks no one's watching. People like her don't panic in a crowd. They panic in the quiet after."

So today there would be no quiet moment that wasn't watched. Priya had two investigators in plain clothes somewhere in this crowd right now, one dressed like a dad in a flannel shirt with a kid's balloon animal tied to his belt loop, the other posing as a photographer for the local paper, camera bag heavy with something that wasn't a lens. Nate was here too, in jeans and a Cedar Hollow High sweatshirt instead of his uniform, technically still suspended, technically not supposed to be within a hundred yards of this investigation, and here anyway because Priya had quietly deputized him for the day and told Chief Torres it wasn't his department's call to make anymore.

Torres stood near the gazebo steps in his dress uniform, arms crossed, jaw tight. He hadn't spoken to Maggie once since she'd arrived. She didn't blame him. She wasn't sure yet whether to hate him or pity him.

"Ladies and gentlemen," came the mayor's voice through the crackling PA system, "if I could have your attention for the Founder's Day tradition that goes back, oh, longer than most of us have been alive."

The crowd drew in around the table, kids on shoulders, dogs on leashes, Ruth Ann Sawyer somewhere near the back looking like she'd rather be anywhere else, Diane Pruitt standing very straight beside Eleanor Vance with a black ribbon still pinned to her coat. Maggie found Nate's face in the crowd, half hidden behind a woman with a stroller, and he gave her the smallest nod. *You're okay. Go.*

"This year," the mayor continued, and there was an uncomfortable little pause where everyone in Cedar Hollow remembered exactly why this year's festival had almost been canceled twice, "we're grateful to the Doyle family, grateful to Maggie for carrying on what her mother built, for keeping this town's traditions alive even through a hard season. Maggie, if you'd do the honors."

Applause, uneven, some of it clearly reluctant. Maggie stepped up to the table and took hold of the sheet.

Under the linen was a three-tier spice cake, the kind Rose had made every year for as long as Maggie could remember, dense and dark with molasses and cinnamon, its top dusted white like the first frost on the hills. Maggie had baked it herself two nights ago, alone in the kitchen at two in the morning, crying into the batter a little, because it felt like the last thing she could do that was purely, simply about her mother and not about any of this.

She pulled the sheet away. The applause came louder this time, real this time, and for one clean second Maggie let herself feel it, the good kind of eyes on her, the kind that used to mean something in this town before everything went sideways.

Then the ceremony dissolved into the usual chaos, kids crowding the table, someone cutting the first slice for the mayor, the band striking up again. Maggie stepped back, and back again, until she was behind the tent flap where the extra folding chairs were stacked, catching her breath, waiting for the signal that this part was over and the real part could begin.

"There you are."

Denise Cobb's voice, warm as ever, warmer than the day was cold. She came around the tent flap with a paper cup of cider in each hand, offering one to Maggie like this was any ordinary afternoon between two women who ran committees together.

"That cake was beautiful, honey. Your mother would be so proud." Denise's eyes were doing something careful behind the kindness, cataloguing. "You holding up all right? It's a lot, isn't it. All this, after everything."

"I'm managing." Maggie took the cider because refusing it would have looked wrong, and because her hands needed something to hold that wasn't shaking on their own.

"I know things have been ugly for you. People talk in this town, always have. I've tried to tell folks to give you the benefit of the doubt." Denise glanced toward the gap in the tent flap, where the crowd noise rolled on, oblivious. "I heard the state investigator's been asking around. Priya something."

"Adjei." Maggie kept her voice light. "She's been thorough."

"I'm sure." Denise sipped her cider. "It's a terrible thing, what happened to Harold. I still can't picture it. Poor man, all alone in your kitchen like that." A pause, small, almost nothing. "Slumped up against that drawer of yours, they said. The proofing one."

Maggie's stomach dropped so fast and so completely that for a moment she thought she might actually be sick after all, right there behind the tent, in front of God and Denise Cobb and the brass band still playing twenty feet away.

*Nobody released that.* Torres had told her himself, sitting across the dining table that first horrible morning, that the position of the body hadn't gone in any statement, hadn't gone to the paper, hadn't gone anywhere except the incident report and the medical examiner's file. Maggie had never once, not to a single soul in this town, described exactly how she'd found him, slumped against the open drawer with the dough spilling over his shoulder like something had been trying to bury him gently. She had told Josie the bare fact of it. She had told Nate. She had told Torres. She had never told anyone the detail of the drawer.

She kept her face as still as she could manage, forced a small, sad nod, the kind a shaken person might give.

"It's hard to talk about," Maggie said carefully, testing the ground. "I try not to think about exactly how I found him."

"Of course. Of course you don't." Denise's hand came to rest briefly on Maggie's arm, motherly, terrible. "I only meant, it must haunt you. Finding him against your own equipment like that. Something so specific."

*She said it twice now.* Maggie's mind was running fast and cold, cataloguing every possible innocent explanation and finding none of them held water. Torres hadn't leaked it. The coroner's office wouldn't have. Nate certainly hadn't. There was exactly one way Denise Cobb could know precisely how Harold Pruitt's body had been positioned against that proofing drawer, down to the detail of it being open, down to the dough spilled over his shoulder.

She had been there.

Maggie's pulse thundered so loud she was sure Denise could hear it. She made herself smile, made herself take another sip of cider she didn't taste, made herself say, "You're sweet to check on me, Denise," in a voice that came out almost normal.

But something had shifted in Denise's face while Maggie was busy hiding her own reaction, some small flicker gone cold and assessing, and Maggie understood, a half second too late, that she had not hidden it well enough. Denise's eyes had gone from warm to still, the particular stillness of someone recalculating everything they thought they knew about the person standing in front of them.

"You didn't know that detail," Denise said quietly. Not a question.

The band kept playing. Somewhere out past the tent flap, a child laughed at something, and the ordinary, oblivious world of Founder's Day rolled on twenty feet away, close enough to touch and a thousand miles from where Maggie suddenly, sickeningly understood she was standing.

"Denise," Maggie started, and didn't know what word was supposed to come after that.

Denise Cobb set her paper cup down on a folding chair, very deliberately, and the last of the warmth went out of her face like a light switched off.

"No," she said. "I don't think you did."
