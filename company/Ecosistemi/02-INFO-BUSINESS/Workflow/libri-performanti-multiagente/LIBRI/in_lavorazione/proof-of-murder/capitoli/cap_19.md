# Rose's Letter

Maggie spent the rest of that day in the apartment above the bakery with the shades drawn, the CLOSED sign turned outward on the front door for the first time since she'd reopened. Josie had offered to stay, and Maggie had sent her home instead, needing, for once, to sit alone with the wreck of the week without an audience.

She was still sitting at the kitchen table that evening, a cup of tea gone cold in front of her, Rose's recipe box pushed to one side like something she couldn't quite look at, when a soft knock sounded at the back door. Not Nate's knock, she knew his rhythm by now. Slower, more hesitant.

She almost didn't answer it. Then she thought of Walter Finch's face in the parking lot, and made herself get up.

Eleanor Vance stood on the back step, a cardigan pulled tight against the evening chill, silver hair pinned up the way she'd worn it for as long as Maggie could remember. She had taught fourth grade at Cedar Hollow Elementary for thirty one years, had taught Maggie herself long division and cursive handwriting at a desk by the window, and had been, before anything else, Rose Doyle's closest friend since the two of them were girls.

"Mrs. Vance." Maggie's voice cracked around the old habit of calling her teacher. "I'm sorry, I wasn't expecting."

"I know you weren't." Eleanor's eyes were kind but tired, the eyes of a woman who had been carrying something heavy for a long time and had finally decided to set it down. "Can I come in, Maggie? I should have come sooner. I've been telling myself all week that I would, and then I didn't, and I'm ashamed of that."

Maggie stepped back and let her in, and Eleanor settled into the chair across the table like she'd sat in that exact spot a hundred times before, which, Maggie realized, she probably had. How many afternoons had Rose and Eleanor sat right here, drinking coffee and trading gossip while a young Maggie did homework at the counter.

"I heard about the note," Eleanor said without preamble. "The forged one. I want you to know I never believed a word of it, and neither did anyone with sense in this town, whatever the loud ones are saying." She folded her hands on the table, and Maggie saw they were trembling slightly. "I should have come to you the day after Harold died. I've been a coward, Maggie, and I'm too old to keep being one."

"Come to me about what?"

Eleanor reached into the oversized handbag at her feet and drew out an envelope, cream colored, gone soft and slightly yellowed at the corners with age, Rose's name never printed on it, only Maggie's, in her mother's unmistakable hand. *For Maggie, if anything happens to me.*

Maggie's breath caught somewhere in her chest. "What is this."

"Your mother gave it to me six weeks before she died." Eleanor's voice wavered, and she pressed her lips together before continuing. "She came to my house on a Tuesday evening, sat right where you're sitting now, actually, in my kitchen, and she told me she was frightened. She said if anything happened to her, anything at all, even something that looked natural, I was to give this to you, but only when I was certain you were ready to fight for it. Not before." She wiped at her eyes with one knuckle. "I told myself for three years that you weren't ready. That you were still grieving, still building your life elsewhere. And then you came home and reopened the bakery and I thought, perhaps now, and then Harold died and I got scared all over again, scared that whatever was in this letter would only put you in more danger. I am sorry, Maggie. I should have trusted you sooner."

Maggie's hands shook as she took the envelope. She turned it over once, then carefully worked the flap open, the old glue giving way with barely a sound. Inside was a single folded page, Rose's handwriting dense and hurried, nothing like the careful hand on her recipe cards. This was the writing of a woman working fast, afraid of being interrupted.

She read it once silently, then again aloud, her voice catching on every other line.

*Maggie, if you're reading this, something has happened to me, and I need you to know it wasn't an accident, whatever anyone tells you. I have been keeping records on the committee's finances for two months now. Numbers that don't reconcile, land appraisals that don't match what's on file with the county, money moving through accounts that shouldn't exist. I brought my concerns to Harold first, because I trusted him, because I thought he was a decent man caught up in something bigger than he understood. He wouldn't look at what I found. He kept saying it wasn't his place to question Denise, that Denise had built this committee from nothing and deserved the benefit of the doubt.*

*Maggie, D is the one you need to watch. Not Harold, whatever happens, whatever you hear about him. D controls the money, D controls the land deal, and D has made it very clear to me, in ways I can't put on paper even now, that this needs to disappear before the festival or there will be consequences. I don't know what those consequences look like. I am frightened in a way I have never been frightened in my life, and I am forty six years old and I have never once been afraid of anyone in this town until now.*

*I love you more than this bakery, more than this town, more than anything. If I am gone, please don't let this die with me. Find someone you trust completely and finish what I started. Be careful. Please, please be careful.*

*Mom*

Maggie set the letter down on the table with hands that would not stop shaking, and for a long moment neither woman said anything at all. Outside, the evening had gone fully dark, the first crickets of the season sawing away somewhere in the grass behind the bakery.

"She knew," Maggie said finally, her voice barely above a whisper. "She knew someone might kill her, and she still went after them."

"Your mother was the bravest woman I ever knew," Eleanor said, tears running freely down her face now. "And I let her secret sit in a drawer in my house for three years because I was too frightened to hand it to you. I am so sorry, Maggie. I should have trusted that you were her daughter."

Maggie reached across the table and took the older woman's hand, surprising herself with the steadiness in her own fingers now, as if reading her mother's fear had somehow burned some of her own away. "You're giving it to me now. That's what matters."

"D," Eleanor repeated, looking at the letter again. "Denise. It has to be Denise, doesn't it. Your mother never would have used just an initial for someone she wasn't afraid to name outright. She was protecting herself, in case anyone else found this before you did."

"It's Denise," Maggie said, and for the first time since Torres's officers had walked her out of her own bakery that morning, something in her chest unclenched, replaced by something harder and colder and infinitely more useful than despair. "It was always Denise."

She called Nate the moment Eleanor's car pulled away from the curb, and he arrived within twenty minutes, still in the flannel and jeans he'd worn that afternoon in the police station parking lot, his face falling into careful stillness as he read the letter twice at the kitchen table, the way Maggie had.

"This is it," he said finally, setting the page down with the same reverence he might show a piece of evidence, which, Maggie realized, was exactly what it was. "Maggie, this is everything. Motive, the timeline, your mother naming the threat in her own hand, written weeks before she died. Combined with the ledger fragment, the storage unit files, the ten recipe cards with the coded numbers." He looked up at her, and something had shifted in his expression, an urgency that hadn't been there in the parking lot that morning. "This isn't circumstantial anymore. This is a case."

"A case that Bill Torres won't touch," Maggie said. "Not with Denise's hand on his career the way it apparently is."

"No," Nate agreed slowly. "Which means we don't take it to Torres."

"Then who."

He was quiet a moment, turning something over in his mind, and when he spoke again his voice had the low, careful cadence of a man choosing his next move with real weight behind it. "There's a state investigator handling the transition on Harold's case, name of Priya Adjei. She's sharp, she's not political, and she's already been asking questions about why the local department sat on the homicide classification as long as they did. If we bring this to her directly, all of it, laid out clean, she won't be able to ignore it, and more importantly, she won't be beholden to whatever debt Torres owes Denise Cobb."

Maggie looked down at her mother's letter one more time, at the frantic, hurried hand, so different from the careful loops on the recipe cards, and felt something settle into place inside her, resolve where an hour ago there had only been exhaustion and the temptation to run.

"Then that's what we do," she said. "Tomorrow. First thing."

"Tomorrow," Nate agreed, and for the first time in days, when he looked at her, she saw something that might have been hope.
