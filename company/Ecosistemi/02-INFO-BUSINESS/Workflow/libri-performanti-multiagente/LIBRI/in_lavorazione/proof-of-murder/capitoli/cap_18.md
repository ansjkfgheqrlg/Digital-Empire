# The Setup

The knock on the bakery's front door came at seven fifteen the next morning, hard enough to rattle the glass, and Maggie knew before she reached the lock that it wasn't a customer. Chief Torres stood on the sidewalk with two uniformed officers behind him, and beyond them, parked crooked against the curb, a state police cruiser idled with its lights dark.

"Maggie Doyle." Torres held up a folded paper like it was a subpoena, though she could see even from here it wasn't. "I need you to come down to the station with me. Now, not later."

"I have bread proofing." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "What is this about?"

"It's about a note." Torres's jaw was set in the particular way of a man who had already made up his mind and wanted the world to catch up to him. "Anonymous tip came in overnight, dropped through the station mail slot. Note in what looks like your mother's handwriting, naming Harold Pruitt and an old grudge. I need you to explain it to me."

Josie appeared in the kitchen doorway, flour still on her hands, eyes wide. "Maggie, don't say anything, call someone first."

"I don't have anyone to call," Maggie said, and it came out more bitter than she meant it to. She untied her apron, hung it on the hook by the register, and walked out the door between the two officers like she was being marched to her own execution, aware of Ruth Ann Sawyer's shop across the street, its blinds already tilted open, a shape moving behind the glass to watch her go.

The interview room at the station had not improved since the last time she'd sat in it. Same table, same buzzing fluorescent tube overhead, same faint smell of burnt coffee. Torres set a plastic sleeve on the table between them, and inside it, a single sheet of yellowed paper covered in handwriting that made Maggie's stomach drop the moment she saw it. It looked like her mother's hand. The same looping R, the same short, decisive crossbars on her T's.

"Read it," Torres said.

Maggie leaned in without touching the sleeve. The note was short, three sentences, and cruel in its economy. *Harold knows what he did to me and mine. He will pay for it one way or another. Rose.*

"That's not her handwriting," Maggie said, but even as she said it, doubt needled through her, because it was close, unnervingly close, the kind of close that came from someone who had studied Rose's recipe cards carefully enough to copy the shape of her letters.

"Looks close enough to me," Torres said. "Close enough that a jury might think so too."

"Then you'd be showing a jury a forgery." Maggie's hands were flat on the table, and she made herself keep them still. "Chief, think about this. My mother has been dead three years. If she wrote that note, when did she write it, and how does a three year old note about Harold suddenly appear in your mail slot the week after he's murdered? Someone typed this up in their head yesterday and someone else practiced her handwriting to make it convincing."

"Or your mother left it for you," Torres said, "and you've been sitting on it, and when Harold turned up dead in your kitchen you got scared and didn't want anyone finding a motive note in the trash."

"That's insane, and you know it." Her voice cracked upward despite herself. "You have real evidence sitting in an evidence locker somewhere, a ledger page with numbers that match my mother's recipe cards, a storage unit in Rutland stuffed with financial records about the festival grounds, and instead you're chasing an anonymous note that conveniently drops into your lap five days before Founder's Day. Who benefits from you looking at me instead of the committee's books, Chief? Ask yourself that."

Something flickered behind Torres's eyes, a flash of doubt maybe, quickly buried. "I ask myself plenty of things, Ms. Doyle. Right now I'm asking whether you've got a documented history of exactly this kind of accusation. Boston. A recipe theft scandal that ran you clean out of your career there. Seems like trouble follows you into kitchens."

The words landed like a slap, precise and old, dredged up from wherever Ruth Ann or someone worse had planted them. Maggie felt heat climb her throat. "That was a lie too," she said, low and even, forcing the shake out of her voice through sheer will. "And it cost me everything once already. I am not going to sit here and let it happen again in my own mother's town."

They held her for two hours. No charges came, because there was nothing to charge her with beyond a piece of paper that any handwriting expert worth their fee would eventually flag as a poor imitation, but by the time she walked out through the station's front doors into the gray, drizzling late morning, half the town had already heard some version of the story, each one worse than the last.

Nate was waiting by his truck at the edge of the lot, not in uniform, his jaw tight in a way she had never seen on him.

"You're not on shift," she said.

"I'm on suspension pending review," he said flatly. "Torres pulled my file this morning. Says I've been running my own investigation parallel to his, feeding you information I shouldn't have, and he's not wrong, Maggie, I did all of that. He wrote me up for the storage unit trip, for the Grange hall, probably for standing in your kitchen at midnight looking at recipe cards. I told him I'd do it again if it kept an innocent woman from getting railroaded, and that didn't help my case any."

Guilt hit her harder than the interrogation had. "Nate, you can't lose your badge over this. Not because of me."

"I'm not losing it over you." His voice softened, just slightly, the version of him she remembered from eighteen years old, steady under pressure in a way most boys weren't. "I'm losing it, if I lose it, because Bill Torres would rather close a case fast and keep Denise Cobb happy than actually look at what's in front of him. That's not your fault. That's his."

They stood together in the gray drizzle a moment, saying nothing, and then Maggie's phone buzzed in her coat pocket. A text from Josie. *Two calls came in. Founder's Day Petting Zoo and the Rutland cider stand both cancelled their bread orders. Said they're going with Ruth Ann instead. I'm so sorry.*

Maggie read it twice, feeling something in her chest that had held steady through the wake, through Torres's questioning, through the vandalized kitchen, finally begin to slip.

"What is it," Nate asked, watching her face.

"Two vendors just dropped my bakery," she said quietly. "For Ruth Ann." She laughed, short and humorless. "Of course they did. Everyone in town thinks I killed a man with a rolling pin and forged my dead mother's handwriting to frame him for something. Why would anyone want my bread on their table this weekend."

"Maggie."

"I keep telling myself I'm doing this for the bakery," she went on, the words coming faster now, some dam finally cracking after days of holding steady. "For Mom. To save what she built. But maybe I'm just making it worse. Maybe if I'd never come back, never reopened, never started asking questions, Harold would still be alive and nobody would be looking at me like I'm a murderer in my own mother's town." She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Maybe I should just sell it, Nate. Take whatever Ruth Ann or some developer offers, and go back to Boston, or go anywhere that isn't here."

Nate didn't say anything right away. He reached out, not touching her, just close enough that she could feel the warmth of him against the cold drizzle. "You don't mean that."

"I don't know what I mean anymore." Her voice had gone thin and tired. "I came home to save something. I'm starting to think I might be the one thing standing in the way of it being saved."

"Listen to me." His voice had an edge now, urgent, the same tone he must have used with Torres that morning. "Your mother didn't die of a heart problem she never had. Harold didn't die by accident against your proofing drawer. Somebody in this town has killed two people and is trying to pin a third crime, forgery, on you, this week, five days before the festival. That is not a coincidence, Maggie, that is someone panicking because you and I are close to something. People who are winning don't forge notes. People who are losing do."

She wanted to believe him. Standing there in the wet gravel with her name smeared across the whole town by breakfast, it was hard to feel like she was winning anything at all.

"I need to think," she said finally. "I need to go home and think, and not talk to anyone, and not answer the phone."

"Okay." He nodded, stepped back to give her room, though the look on his face said he didn't want to. "I'll be around. I mean it, Maggie. Whatever you decide, call me first."

She walked back toward the bakery alone, past Ruth Ann's shop where the OPEN sign glowed cheerfully in the window, past two women on the sidewalk who stopped talking and watched her pass with the particular silence reserved for someone the whole town has already decided about, and for the first time since she had driven back into Cedar Hollow with her mother's bakery keys in her coat pocket, Maggie let herself imagine, seriously, what it would feel like to simply leave.
