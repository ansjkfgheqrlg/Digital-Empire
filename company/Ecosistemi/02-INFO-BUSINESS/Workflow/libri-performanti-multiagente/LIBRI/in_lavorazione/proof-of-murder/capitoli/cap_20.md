# Building the Case

Priya Adjei's temporary office was a repurposed storage room at the county building in Montpelier, one fluorescent tube flickering overhead and a folding table doing the work of a desk, but the woman herself carried an unhurried authority that neither the room nor her rank quite explained. She was younger than Maggie expected, maybe thirty five, with close cropped hair and reading glasses pushed up into it, and she listened to the first two minutes of Maggie and Nate's account without writing down a single word, simply watching them both with the careful attention of someone deciding, in real time, whether she was dealing with cranks or with something real.

"Start over," she said finally. "Slower. And show me everything you brought, in the order you found it."

They laid it out across the folding table like a exhibit in a very small, very tired museum. The green ledger page pulled from the trash bin behind the Grange hall, its torn edge still ragged, the numbers on it inked in a hand Nate had already had a forensic contact confirm as Denise Cobb's. The boxes of financial records from the Rutland storage unit, photographed and organized into a binder Nate had put together over two sleepless nights, showing land appraisals filed with the county that didn't match the numbers quoted to the committee, and a pattern of transfers routed through an account with Walter Finch Development LLC in the memo line. The ten recipe cards, in careful plastic sleeves now, Rose's cheerful handwriting for Molasses Spice Cookies and Apple Cider Donuts sitting right alongside columns of pencil figures that matched, line for line, the numbers on the ledger page. And last, Rose's letter, which Maggie set down with both hands like it might still bruise if handled roughly.

Priya read the letter twice, the same way Nate had, and when she looked up her expression had changed entirely.

"This D," she said. "You're confident it's Denise Cobb."

"She's the treasurer," Maggie said. "She controls the committee's finances. She's the one who slammed the ledger shut the moment I looked at it. She's about to run for mayor on the strength of exactly the kind of civic reputation this would destroy. And Walter Finch all but told me in a parking lot two nights ago that she doesn't lose gracefully, not ever."

"And your local chief," Priya said carefully, "has shown no interest in any of this."

"He forged, or accepted without question, an anonymous note supposedly in my mother's handwriting naming me as a suspect in a grudge against Harold Pruitt," Maggie said. "The morning after we gave Diane Pruitt's storage unit lead a real look. He suspended Deputy Coleman here for helping me investigate honestly. I don't think Chief Torres is corrupt exactly. I think he's spent years leaning on Denise's political favor and doesn't know how to stop now that it matters."

Priya sat back, tapping one finger against the folder, and for a long moment the only sound in the cramped office was the fluorescent tube buzzing overhead.

"I can't make an arrest off a letter and a ledger fragment," she said finally. "Any defense attorney worth their fee would call the letter grief, speculation, an old woman's paranoia. The ledger page ties Denise to numbers, not to a murder. What I need is something that connects her directly to Harold's death, or to your mother's, something that isn't three years old and circumstantial."

"So we get her to show her hand," Nate said.

Priya's eyes moved to him, sharp and assessing. "Meaning what, exactly, Deputy."

Maggie leaned forward. She'd been turning the idea over since the drive down from Cedar Hollow, and now, in this small flickering room, it finally found its shape out loud. "Founder's Day is in three days. The closing ceremony has always ended with the unveiling of the Founder's Day cake, it's a hundred year tradition, and this year, because I own the bakery, that's me. Standing on a stage in front of the entire town, with Denise Cobb standing right beside me as committee president, because Harold's gone and she's stepped into his role for the event." She looked between Nate and Priya. "If we can put pressure on her before that moment, something specific, something that makes her believe her cover is unraveling in front of witnesses, in front of press, in front of half the county, she may do something she wouldn't do in private. People who've spent three years being careful make mistakes when they're cornered in public and running out of time."

"That's a significant risk," Priya said slowly, though Maggie could see the idea taking hold behind her eyes rather than being dismissed. "You're talking about pressuring a homicide suspect in a public venue during a town's biggest event of the year, with no guarantee she reacts the way you want."

"I'm talking about doing it with you there," Maggie said. "With real investigators, not Bill Torres and two officers I don't trust. We don't need her to confess on a stage. We need her rattled enough, watched closely enough, that whatever she does next, walking away, making a call, reaching for something she shouldn't, gives you what you need to move."

Priya was quiet for a long moment, turning the recipe cards over one at a time, studying the pencil figures against the photographed ledger page. "Show me the storage unit inventory again," she said, and Nate slid the binder across, and she went through it page by page with the focus of someone building a case in real time rather than reviewing one already built.

"All right," she said at last, closing the binder. "Here's what I can commit to. I'll pull jurisdiction on Harold Pruitt's homicide investigation as of tomorrow morning, state authority, which takes it out of Torres's hands whether he likes it or not, and gives me legal standing to have people on-site at your festival. I'll have two plainclothes detectives there Saturday, positioned near the ceremony stage, and I'll be there myself. We are not going to stage a public confrontation, Ms. Doyle, I want to be very clear about that. What we're going to do is create the conditions where, if Denise Cobb feels the walls closing in, she makes a mistake we can act on immediately. That means eyes on her from the moment she arrives to the moment the ceremony ends. It means a warrant ready to move on if she tries to leave town or destroy records. And it means you," she looked hard at Maggie, "doing nothing except your job. Baking the cake, standing on that stage, being exactly the target she already thinks you are. Nothing more. No accusations, no confrontations, nothing that could compromise what we're building. Can you do that?"

Maggie thought of her mother's letter, of the careful, frightened hand that had written *be careful, please, please be careful*, three years before anyone had listened.

"I can do that," she said.

"Good." Priya stood, gathering the folder and the binder into a battered leather bag. "Deputy Coleman, I'll need a formal statement from you about the storage unit, the Grange hall, all of it, on the record, first thing tomorrow. I understand you're currently suspended."

"I am," Nate said.

"That won't last," Priya said, with the first trace of something like dry humor Maggie had seen from her all evening. "Not once my office formally requests your cooperation as a material witness. Chief Torres can suspend you from local duty. He can't stop a state homicide investigation from using you."

Outside, the parking lot had gone fully dark, the Montpelier streetlights buzzing to life in their orange sodium glow, and Maggie sat for a moment in the passenger seat of Nate's truck before he started the engine, looking back at the squat brick county building where, three days from now, everything might finally come apart in the open.

"Three days," she said.

"Three days," Nate agreed. He didn't reach for the ignition right away. "Maggie, I need you to hear me say this once, clearly. Saturday, on that stage, you are not investigating anyone. You are not confronting anyone. You are baking a cake and smiling for a hundred year old tradition, and you are letting trained people do the rest. Your mother spent two months building a case quietly and it still got her killed. I am not going to let the same thing happen to her daughter."

"I hear you," Maggie said, and meant it, though some small, stubborn part of her, the part that had walked into Ruth Ann's shop and Del Sutter's pharmacy and the Rutland storage unit without waiting for anyone's permission, knew that if Saturday came down to it, she would do whatever it took to make sure Denise Cobb never hurt anyone else the way she had hurt Rose and Harold. She kept that thought to herself, folded away like a recipe card with numbers only she could read, and watched the lights of Montpelier fall behind them as Nate pulled out onto the dark road home.
