# Downplayed

Chief Torres arrived twenty minutes after the second cruiser, unhurried, a coffee from the gas station still in his hand, and Maggie watched something in Nate's shoulders go rigid the moment the chief walked through the splintered back door and surveyed the mess with an expression closer to mild inconvenience than alarm.

"Kids," Torres said, before he'd finished his first slow circuit of the kitchen. "Probably the same ones who've been driving by all week trying to get a look at where it happened. Somebody dared somebody else to break in and take a souvenir, panicked, tore through what was closest, and ran."

"They didn't touch the register," Nate said. "It's sitting open. Empty, sure, but any kid looking for a souvenir or a thrill goes for the cash drawer first, every time. They went straight for the recipe box and left everything else alone."

"Kids don't always think logically, Coleman, that's sort of the defining feature of being a kid." Torres took a sip of his coffee, glanced at Maggie with an expression she couldn't quite read, something between sympathy and suspicion that never seemed to fully resolve into either. "I'll have somebody dust the door frame for prints, but I wouldn't get your hopes up. This kind of thing, it's a headline for two days and then it's forgotten."

"Chief." Nate's voice had an edge to it now that Maggie had never heard him use with a superior, careful but unmistakably firm. "This happened three days after we pulled a torn ledger page out of a trash bin behind the Grange that matches a code hidden in Rose Doyle's handwriting. It happened the same week the state police are getting ready to take over a homicide investigation tied to that same code. I don't think it's kids. I think somebody's cleaning up loose ends, and I think we need to treat it that way starting tonight."

The kitchen went very quiet. One of the forensic techs near the door found something urgent to photograph on the far wall.

Torres set his coffee down on the stainless counter, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something flatter and colder. "You want to walk me through what you and Ms. Doyle have been doing on your own time these last few nights, Deputy? Because from where I'm standing, it looks like the two of you have been running a private investigation into a case that isn't yours to run, and now you want me to build a theory around a break in that could just as easily be exactly what it looks like."

"I want you to at least consider that it isn't," Nate said. "That's all I'm asking."

"I'll consider everything the evidence supports," Torres said. "Get me prints. Get me a witness. Get me anything besides a hunch and a stack of old recipe cards, and I'll consider it plenty. Until then, this goes in the log as an attempted burglary, no items confirmed missing." He looked at Maggie. "Nothing missing that you can tell, is there, Ms. Doyle?"

Maggie looked at the scattered cards, at the mess still waiting to be sorted, and chose her words carefully. "I haven't finished checking."

"Let me know if that changes." Torres pulled his jacket tighter and headed for the door, pausing only to add, not unkindly but not warmly either, "Get that back door fixed today. And Coleman, walk with me a second."

Whatever passed between them in the alley, Maggie didn't hear, but Nate came back inside ten minutes later with his jaw tight and a muscle working near his temple that told her the conversation hadn't gone well. He didn't say much about it, only that he'd been reminded, in no uncertain terms, whose case this officially was, and that free lancing his way through it a second time wouldn't be treated as generously as the first.

"Are you in trouble," Maggie asked quietly, once the last cruiser had pulled away and it was just the two of them and Josie, who'd come running the moment Maggie called her, standing amid the wreckage of the kitchen in the gray early light.

"I'm always a little bit in trouble these days," Nate said, with a tired attempt at a smile that didn't fully land. "Let's just get this sorted before the morning crowd starts wondering why the paper's off the windows and the door's got a two by four across it."

They worked for the better part of an hour, the three of them on their knees on the kitchen floor, gathering cards into careful stacks, checking each one for damage, cross referencing them against the photographs Josie had taken the night they'd first found the pencil marks. Most of it was tedious, methodical work, the kind that gave Maggie's hands something to do while her mind turned over Torres's dismissal again and again, looking for the seam in it, the place where his indifference stopped being ordinary bureaucratic caution and started looking like something closer to protection.

It was Josie who noticed first, sitting back on her heels with her list in hand, running a finger down the names she'd recorded that first night. Buttermilk Biscuits. Apple Stack Cake. Cranberry Walnut Bread. Molasses Spice Cookies, already gone, already sitting in an evidence locker somewhere with the police department, taken from Harold's coat pocket the morning he died. Twelve cards total, marked with the faint pencil numbers and initials, twelve cards Josie had photographed one by one with the heat lamp bulb propped at just the right angle to bring out every hidden digit.

"Eleven," Josie said slowly, counting the stack in front of her a second time, then a third. "Maggie, I only count eleven."

Maggie's stomach dropped. She spread the cards across the worktable herself, checking names against Josie's list, checking the backs of each one for the telltale ghost of pencil pressure. Buttermilk Biscuits, there, its margin faintly marked with a date and a number that matched a deposit slip from the storage unit. Apple Stack Cake, there. Cranberry Walnut, there. One by one she accounted for every card on the list, felt her hope rise absurdly for a moment that Josie had simply miscounted, until she reached the last name and found nothing to match it.

"Grandma Ruth's Sugar Cookies," she said, her voice barely above a whisper. "That's the one that's gone."

She remembered the card clearly now that she was looking for it and not finding it, remembered the back of it under the heat lamp two nights before, the pencil marks denser there than on any of the others, two sets of initials instead of one, D.C. and W.F. side by side, and beneath them a single number larger than anything else they'd found, underlined twice in Rose's careful hand.

"That was the one with the full total," Josie said, understanding arriving in her voice at the same moment it arrived in Maggie's chest. "That was the one that actually named an amount and both of them together on the same line. The others are just pieces. That one was the whole picture."

Maggie sat back against the base of the worktable, the splintered back door still propped shut behind her with a two by four, the morning light finally strong enough to slide gold across the floor where flour dust still hung in the air from the night's chaos. Somebody had torn through an entire box of her mother's handwriting, scattering years of memory across her kitchen floor, and had walked away with exactly one card.

Not the one already in police custody. The one that would have told them, in her mother's own hand, precisely how much money and precisely which two people had been standing together on the wrong side of it.

"They knew which one to take," Maggie said. "That means they already knew what was in the box before they ever broke in here."

Nate crouched beside her, his voice quiet, steady, the voice she remembered from seventeen years old walking her home in the dark and telling her nothing bad was going to happen. It didn't sound quite as certain now. "Then we go find out how they knew," he said, "before they decide there's anything else in this town worth taking."
