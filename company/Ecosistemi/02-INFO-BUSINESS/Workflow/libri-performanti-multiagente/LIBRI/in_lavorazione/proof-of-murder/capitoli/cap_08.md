# Invisible Ink

"We need light," Maggie said, "raking light, that's what Nate called it. Something low and angled so the indentations catch shadow instead of just washing out flat."

It was nearly midnight, the shop dark and locked, brown paper still taped over the front windows so no passerby could see the two of them hunched over the stainless counter with the recipe card and a battery lantern between them. Josie had come the moment Maggie called, still in her pajama bottoms under an old barn coat, hair scraped back in a knot, and she had not asked a single question beyond do you have the pencil box, which told Maggie everything she needed to know about how much her friend had already guessed.

"The proofing cabinet's got that heat lamp bulb in it, the amber one, low and warm," Josie said, already moving toward the wrecked, taped off kitchen station, then catching herself and stopping short of the tape line. "Except that whole corner's a crime scene, we can't exactly go pulling equipment out of it."

"There's a spare bulb in the pantry, same fitting, I bought two when I installed the unit in case one blew during a bake." Maggie was already digging through the metal shelving unit against the back wall, pushing aside sacks of bread flour until her hand closed around the small box. "I can rig it in a desk lamp. Low angle, warm color, that's exactly the kind of light document techs use for exactly this, Nate said so."

They worked at the counter with the bulb screwed into an old gooseneck lamp that usually sat by the register for doing paperwork, the shade tilted almost flat so the light skimmed sideways across the surface of the card instead of shining straight down on it. At first nothing showed, just the familiar looping hand of Rose's molasses spice cookie recipe, the ordinary instructions for creaming butter and sugar, the pencil notations in the margin that Maggie already knew by heart. 4,200. R.C. init. Then the date, three years old now, faded but legible.

"Try the pencil trick too," Josie said. "Like rubbing a coin under paper. Soft pencil, light strokes, right over where the impression should be."

Maggie held the card steady while Josie, hands surprisingly gentle for someone who could sling fifty pound sacks of flour without a second thought, took the softest pencil from the drawer and began dragging it sideways across the blank space at the bottom of the card, feather light, barely more than shading, the graphite catching in the pressed grooves left behind by someone else's pen weeks or months before.

Letters began to surface out of the gray shading like something rising through fog. Not full words at first, just fragments, a curve here, a hard angled stroke there, and then, slowly, as Josie worked the pencil back and forth in short careful strokes and Maggie angled the lamp lower, shapes resolved into something like handwriting, blocky and hurried, nothing like Rose's careful looping script.

"Grange," Maggie read aloud, her voice barely above a whisper. "There. G, r, a. That's Grange."

"Keep going, don't stop the light," Josie murmured, shading a fresh patch just below it.

More letters swam up out of the graphite haze. numbers. dont. add. A gap where the pencil hadn't caught anything, maybe a word pressed too lightly to leave an impression, and then, further down, up. three years.

Maggie sat back slowly, her heart hammering, and read the fragments over again in order, filling in the gaps the way you'd fill in a crossword from context. Grange numbers dont add up. Three years. Something else after that, a partial word that might have been proof or maybe prove, and beneath it, fainter still, barely a shadow of a shadow, what looked like the letter D and then nothing, the impression giving out entirely as though the pen pressing it into existence had run out of paper, or the writer had been interrupted.

"He knew," Maggie said. "Harold knew what my mother found. He was writing it down himself, working it out on top of her own card like he was trying to finish what she started."

"D," Josie said quietly, staring at the last faint mark. "Denise."

"That's my first thought too. Diane said Harold was on the phone two nights before he died, saying he couldn't carry it anymore, that he had to make it right before the festival, and he said Denise's name right to her face on that call." Maggie set the card down carefully on a clean sheet of paper, almost reverent now, aware suddenly of how fragile the evidence was, how easily a careless hand could smudge weeks of hidden proof into nothing. "And today at the committee office, the second my eyes drifted toward her ledger, she slammed it shut like it had teeth. She knew exactly what I might be looking for, Josie. Or she's paranoid enough about everything right now that anybody looking anywhere near her books sets her off."

"Either way, that's not nothing." Josie wiped graphite dust off her fingers onto her barn coat, unconcerned about the smear it left. "We need to photograph this before it fades or smudges, and we need copies, more than one, hidden somewhere that isn't this building, in case."

"In case someone decides the recipe box needs to disappear too," Maggie finished. The thought had been sitting under her ribs all night, unspoken, and saying it out loud made it heavier and more real. "I'll email myself photos right now, and I'll put a set in the safe deposit box at the bank first thing tomorrow."

They were still bent over the counter photographing the card at every angle the lamp would allow, Josie holding the light steady while Maggie clicked away with her phone, when someone knocked hard on the front door, three sharp raps that made both women jump and sent the recipe card sliding half off the counter before Maggie's hand shot out and caught it.

Through the gap where the brown paper didn't quite meet the doorframe, Maggie could see the flash of a badge and, behind it, a familiar squat silhouette in a department jacket. Chief Torres. Behind him, unmistakable even in silhouette, the sharper, taller shape of Ruth Ann Sawyer, arms crossed, practically vibrating with satisfaction.

"Open up, Maggie," Torres called through the door, his voice carrying the particular flatness of a man who has already decided how a conversation is going to go before it starts. "It's late, I know, but this can't wait until morning."

Maggie swept the card, the lantern, the pencil, everything, into a dish towel and shoved the bundle into a flour canister on the shelf before crossing to unlock the door, Josie a half step behind her, both of them arranging their faces into something like calm.

Torres stepped inside without waiting to be invited, Ruth Ann trailing at his shoulder like she had every right to be there, which made the hair on the back of Maggie's neck stand up before a single word was spoken.

"Ms. Sawyer here came to see me tonight," Torres said, "with some things she felt I needed to know. About your temper, Maggie. About some trouble you had back in Boston with a stolen recipe, something that ended your career there rather suddenly. About how Harold Pruitt, as head of the Founder's Day Committee, was the one holding the final say on whether the bakery kept its festival contract this year, and how you'd told more than one person around town that losing that contract could sink you before you even got your feet under you."

"That's not," Maggie started, her face going hot, "Ruth Ann is twisting things, I never said Harold was going to pull the contract, I said the committee was reviewing vendor terms, that's a completely different"

"I'm not finished." Torres's voice cut across hers, flat and final. "Combine that with a stolen recipe card, in your mother's hand, no less, found on the victim's body. Combine it with the fact that you're the one who discovered him, alone, before dawn, in a kitchen only you had keys to. I've been patient, Maggie, more patient than some folks in this department think I should have been, waiting to see if something else would turn up to point elsewhere. Nothing has."

Behind him, Ruth Ann's mouth curved in something that wasn't quite a smile, careful to keep her expression somber for the chief's benefit, but not careful enough to hide the gleam of triumph underneath it.

"Chief Torres," Maggie said, forcing her voice level, "I did not kill Harold Pruitt. I have never had a reason to. Whatever Ruth Ann told you about Boston has nothing to do with any of this, and you know it."

"Maybe. Maybe not. That's what the investigation's for." Torres reached into his jacket and produced a folded paper, formal looking, official letterhead visible even in the dim light of the shop. "As of tonight, you're being named a formal person of interest in the death of Harold Pruitt, pending further investigation. I'd advise you to have a lawyer present for any further questioning, and I'd advise you not to leave town before Founder's Day. That's not an arrest. Not yet. But I want you thinking real hard about anything else you might want to tell me, before this gets any harder for you than it already is."

He set the paper on the counter, gave Josie a brief unreadable glance, and turned to go, Ruth Ann falling into step behind him without a backward look at Maggie, though the set of her shoulders radiated satisfaction all the way out the door.

The bell jangled shut behind them, and the shop went silent except for the hum of the walk in cooler and the soft tick of the old wall clock.

Josie broke the silence first. "Well," she said, her voice unsteady but her chin lifted, "he can call it whatever he wants on a piece of paper. Doesn't make it true."

Maggie stood very still in the middle of her mother's bakery, the flour canister with its hidden recipe card sitting on the shelf behind her, the formal paper naming her a suspect lying on the counter in front of her, and felt something in her chest settle into place, cold and clear and absolutely certain.

"He's not going to look any further than me," she said quietly. "Not with Denise Cobb's fingerprints all over this town's politics and Ruth Ann feeding him whatever story makes his job easiest. Nobody is coming to save the bakery, or my name, or find out what really happened to my mother. If it's going to get done before Founder's Day, I'm the one who has to do it."

She looked at Josie, and Josie looked back at her, and neither of them said anything else, because nothing else needed saying. Outside, the town slept on, unaware that somewhere in its quiet streets a killer was still walking free, and that the woman they had already tried and convicted in whispers over coffee had just decided to stop waiting for anyone's permission to prove them wrong.
