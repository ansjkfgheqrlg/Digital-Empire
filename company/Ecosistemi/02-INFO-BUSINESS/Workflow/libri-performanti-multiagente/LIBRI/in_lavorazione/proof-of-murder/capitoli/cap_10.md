# A Petty Rivalry

Ruth Ann Sawyer's shop sat two doors down from the hardware store, a narrow storefront with a hand-painted sign reading SAWYER'S PIE CORNER and a window display of fruit pies arranged in a pyramid that looked, Maggie thought, more like a dare than an invitation. She'd walked past it a hundred times without going in. Today she walked straight through the door.

The bell over the frame jingled. Ruth Ann looked up from behind the counter, a rolling pin in one hand and flour dusted up to her elbows, and her expression did a quick, unmistakable slide from surprise to something more careful.

"Maggie. Well. This is a surprise."

"Is it?" Maggie kept her voice even, though her pulse was going hard. She'd rehearsed this on the walk over, had told herself she would stay calm, would not give anyone another story to tell about her temper. "Because I've been hearing a lot about myself around town lately, Ruth Ann, and your name keeps coming up as the source."

Ruth Ann set the rolling pin down with a clatter that suggested she hadn't meant to make it that loud. "I don't know what you think you heard."

"I heard you told Chief Torres I had a temper. I heard you told half the diner I had a motive because of the festival contract. I heard you told a woman buying muffins from my own shop this morning that I had a dispute with Harold Pruitt." Maggie folded her arms. "That's a lot of talking for somebody who claims not to know anything."

A flush crept up Ruth Ann's neck, blotchy and unglamorous, and for a second she looked less like a rival and more like a woman about ten years past the point where petty gossip should have stopped feeling satisfying.

"I never said you murdered anybody," Ruth Ann said, too fast. "I said you had a temper. Which you do. You threw me out of your shop, if you'll recall, in front of Josie and God and everybody."

"You showed up uninvited trying to poach my festival order the morning after I found a body in my kitchen."

"Somebody had to be ready to step in! The town needed bread for Founder's Day, Maggie, festival or no festival, murder or no murder, and it's not like anyone was going to blame me for having a business mind about it."

"You went to Bill Torres," Maggie said. "That's not having a business mind. That's trying to sink me."

The silence that followed had teeth in it. Ruth Ann's eyes darted toward the pie case, the window, the door, anywhere but Maggie's face, and when she finally spoke again her voice had lost its bluster entirely.

"You want to know the truth? Fine. The truth is I've hated watching you walk in and take over that bakery like it was nothing. Your mother built that place from a card table and a coffee can, and everybody in this town loved her for it, and then you show up from Boston with your fancy pastry school and your fancy scandal trailing behind you, and within six months you've got the festival contract I've been trying to land for eleven years." Her chin trembled. "Eleven years, Maggie. I've been baking pies in this town since before you could reach the counter, and the second Rose's bakery needed a new owner, everybody just assumed it should go to you. Nobody asked me. Nobody even considered me."

Maggie opened her mouth to argue that it hadn't gone to her by some town vote, that she'd inherited it because her mother was dead, but something in Ruth Ann's face stopped her. It wasn't malice. It was hurt, old and petty and small, but real.

"So you started spreading rumors," Maggie said instead, quieter now. "About a murder. Because you were jealous of a bakery."

Ruth Ann's face crumpled with something close to shame. "When you say it like that it sounds terrible."

"It is terrible, Ruth Ann. Somebody died in my kititchen. People already think I did it. You handing Bill Torres a motive on a silver platter isn't gossip, it's dangerous."

"I didn't think it would go that far! I just." Ruth Ann pressed her floury hands against her apron, leaving pale prints. "I wanted people to wonder about you the way they used to wonder about your Boston thing. I wanted, I don't know, for once in my life for people to look at somebody else and not me, not my pie crust that always comes in second place at the fair, not my shop that everyone says is 'cute' instead of 'the best.' It was small of me. I know it was small of me."

Maggie let out a breath she hadn't realized she was holding. Ruth Ann looked so thoroughly miserable, standing there in her flour-dusted apron with her pyramid of pies behind her, that it was hard to sustain much anger.

"I'm not going to pretend that's okay," Maggie said. "You need to tell Torres you were exaggerating. All of it. The temper, the motive, whatever you told him about a dispute with Harold."

"There wasn't any dispute," Ruth Ann admitted. "I made that part up entirely."

"I know."

Ruth Ann wiped her hands on a towel, twisting it between her fingers. "I'll call the station. I will, Maggie, I promise. I don't want. I don't actually think you hurt anybody. I just wanted you to struggle a little, the way I've had to struggle. That's not the same as wanting you in prison."

"It could end up being the same thing if Torres runs with it."

That seemed to land. Ruth Ann's face went pale under the flour dust, and she sat down hard on the stool behind her counter, as though her legs had stopped agreeing to hold her up.

"Oh, God. I didn't think about it like that."

Maggie almost left it there. She had what she'd come for, an admission, a promise to walk it back. But something in Ruth Ann's rattled, guilty posture made her linger a moment longer, and on instinct she asked, "Is there anything else? Anything you saw or heard that you haven't told anyone, because you were embarrassed, or because you didn't think it mattered?"

Ruth Ann's eyes flicked up, and something shifted behind them, a different kind of discomfort than the jealousy had been.

"What do you mean?"

"I mean exactly what I said."

Ruth Ann hesitated so long that Maggie thought she might refuse to answer at all. Then the words came out in an awkward rush, like she'd been holding them and was relieved, despite herself, to finally set them down.

"I was driving home from my sister's in Barre the night before Harold died. Late, past midnight, I'd stayed for her kid's birthday party and lost track of time. I came in on Route 14, past the Grange hall, and there was a car in the lot. Harold's car. That old blue Buick he's had forever, everybody knows it, it's got that dented fender from when he backed into the fire hydrant two Founder's Days ago." She twisted the towel tighter. "I didn't think anything of it at the time. Committee business, I figured, though it seemed awful late for that. And then when he turned up dead the next morning I just, I didn't want to say anything, because it would mean admitting I was out that late, and my sister doesn't know I drove home instead of staying over like I told my husband I would, and it's a whole thing, and."

"Ruth Ann." Maggie's voice cut through the rambling, gentle but firm. "What time was this?"

"After midnight. Maybe twelve thirty, one. There were lights on inside. Just the one row, up near the front, like somebody didn't want the whole place lit up." Ruth Ann looked genuinely rattled now, the jealousy forgotten entirely, replaced by something closer to fear. "I should have told Chief Torres that instead of all the other nonsense, shouldn't I."

"Yes," Maggie said. "You should have."

"Will you tell him for me? I don't think I can walk in there and admit I was gossiping about you and hiding real information at the same time. He'll think I'm a lunatic."

Maggie thought of Nate, of the narrowing window he'd warned her about, of the state police circling closer every day. A car in the Grange hall lot after midnight, the night before Harold died. Someone he'd met there, in the dark, with only a single row of lights burning.

"I'll tell Nate," Maggie said. "But you're going to confirm it if he asks. All of it. The car, the lights, the time."

Ruth Ann nodded, small and chastened. "I really am sorry, Maggie. About the rest of it too."

"I know," Maggie said, and found, to her own surprise, that she believed her. "Fix it with Torres. That's the apology that matters right now."

She left the pie shop with the bell jingling behind her, already reaching for her phone, already composing the message to Nate in her head. The Grange hall. Past midnight. The night before Harold died.

Whatever her mother's numbers meant, whatever Denise Cobb was hiding behind that green ledger, it had happened, or continued happening, inside that building, in the dark, while the rest of Cedar Hollow slept.
