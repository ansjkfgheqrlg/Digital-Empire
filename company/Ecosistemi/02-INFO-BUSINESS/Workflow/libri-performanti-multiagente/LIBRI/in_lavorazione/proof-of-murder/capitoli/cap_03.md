# Persons of Interest

By the time Josie Alvarez pushed through the bakery's front door at eight that morning, ducking under a strip of tape gone slack in the wind, the story had already made its lap around Cedar Hollow twice, gathering detail the way a snowball gathers bark and gravel on its way downhill.

"Ruth Ann's telling the girl at the pharmacy counter that they found a recipe card soaked in blood," Josie said, dropping her bag on Maggie's dining table and not bothering to take her coat off. "Soaked, Maggie. I heard it standing in line for aspirin. By noon it's going to be a whole cake."

"It was a corner. A stain along one edge." Maggie hadn't slept, hadn't tried to, had spent the hours since Torres left sitting at this same table watching the activity in the alley through gaps in the curtain. "How does Ruth Ann even know there was a card."

"Because Deb at the diner heard it from her cousin who does dispatch part time, and Deb told Ruth Ann over the breakfast rush because Ruth Ann tips her twenty percent and asks about everything." Josie dropped into the chair across from her, her dark eyes soft with worry under the toughness she always wore like armor. "You look terrible. Did you eat?"

"I found a body in my proofing drawer eleven hours ago, Josie."

"Right. Stupid question." Josie reached across and squeezed her hand once, hard, then let go, because that was as much softness as either of them generally allowed before there was work to be done. "Okay. Tell me what Torres said. All of it."

Maggie told her. The card, the stain, the way Torres had watched her face like he was waiting for it to slip. By the time she got to the part about the wallet and the committee binder and everything not concerns you anymore, Josie's jaw had gone tight in a way Maggie recognized from a dozen years of friendship, the look Josie got right before she said something she'd later have to apologize for.

"He's not looking anywhere else," Josie said. "Is he. He's got you sitting right there in front of him and he's not going to bother looking past the end of his own nose."

"I don't know what he's doing. I know the whole town thinks I did it."

"Not the whole town."

The bell over the front door jingled before Josie could finish the thought, and both of them turned to see Ruth Ann Sawyer letting herself in, her coat buttoned to the throat, her round pleasant face arranged into an expression of careful sympathy that did not reach her eyes.

"Maggie, honey, I heard, I came the second I heard, are you all right." Ruth Ann owned the bakery two towns over in Millbrook, had owned it for eleven years, and had made no secret, in the eighteen months since Rose's death, that she'd hoped to buy Cedar Hollow Bakery herself once the estate settled, hoped right up until Maggie came home from Boston with a stack of savings and a lawyer and closed the deal out from under her. "This must be just awful for you. Just awful."

"It's been a morning," Maggie said carefully.

"I only wanted to say, before anybody else gets to you with it," Ruth Ann went on, folding her hands in front of her like a woman about to deliver difficult news at a hospital bedside, "the committee's already called me about the festival order. The pancake breakfast rolls, the donuts, all of it. They can't very well have baked goods coming out of a crime scene, can they, and somebody's got to feed four hundred people Saturday morning, so I told them I'd be glad to step in. Just to help. Just until things get sorted."

Josie's chair scraped back hard enough to bark against the floor. "You've been trying to get your hands on this bakery since before Rose was cold in the ground, Ruth Ann, and now there's a dead man in it and you're here inside of four hours with a proposal."

"I am here," Ruth Ann said, color rising in her cheeks, "because somebody has to think about this town, and about that poor Pruitt family, and about four hundred people who are going to want their pancakes Saturday morning whether or not this kitchen's been cleared by the police. I'm not the one who left a body in a proofing drawer."

"Get out," Maggie said, and was surprised at how level her own voice came out, how little of the tremble from that morning remained in it. "Get out of my dining room, Ruth Ann, and take your casserole dish sympathy with you."

Ruth Ann's mouth opened, closed, and she gathered herself with visible effort into something like dignity before she let herself back out, the bell jingling behind her a second time, sharper now, almost accusing.

Josie let out a breath. "That woman."

"She's not wrong that somebody needs to feed four hundred people Saturday," Maggie said, and hated how tired she sounded saying it.

"She's wrong about who that somebody should be," Josie said. "You didn't do anything, Maggie, and Founder's Day isn't Ruth Ann Sawyer's for the taking just because Harold Pruitt had the bad luck to die in your kitchen instead of hers."

They spent the rest of the day fielding calls Maggie mostly let go to voicemail, and by the time the light started going purple over the ridge behind the church, a small, steady trickle of cars had begun rolling slow past the bakery's front window, brake lights flaring, phones raised. A boy on a bicycle stopped dead in the street to stare up at the apartment for a full thirty seconds before his mother, half a block back, called him on.

"That's it," Josie said, watching a minivan do a deliberate loop of the block for the second time. "We're taping paper over these windows tonight. I am not sitting here being somebody's true crime content."

They found a roll of brown kraft paper in the storeroom, leftover from wrapping loaves for the farmers market, and worked through the evening cutting it to size and taping it up in long, crackling sheets over every window that faced the street, Josie up on a kitchen chair with the scissors, Maggie steadying the ladder and handing up tape. It was strangely soothing work, the kind of simple physical task that asked nothing of the parts of her mind still turning over Torres's flat pale eyes, still turning over the stained edge of that recipe card.

When the windows were dark and the apartment finally felt like her own again, sealed off from the street, Maggie found herself standing over her mother's recipe box, which she'd carried up from the sealed kitchen weeks ago, before any of this, just to keep it safe from flour dust and use. It sat on the sideboard the way it always had, scarred wood, brass corners, the same box Rose had kept on the bakery counter for thirty years, and Maggie ran her fingers over the worn lid before lifting it.

She wasn't looking for anything in particular. She only wanted to touch something of her mother's that hadn't been bagged, tagged, and carried out of the building in a tech's gloved hands. She lifted the lid and began, without quite meaning to, running her thumb along the tabbed dividers, Breads, Cookies, Pies, Preserves, in her mother's own handwritten labels, the same looping g's that had stared up at her from that plastic evidence sleeve.

She got as far as the Cookies section before something in the rhythm of the cards snagged, the way a fingertip catches on a torn nail.

There was a gap.

Not a big one. Not the kind of thing anyone else would ever notice, running a casual hand through the box. But Maggie had spent three months since her mother's death sorting through this exact box, transcribing recipes, testing them in her own kitchen one by one to relearn her mother's hands through her own, and she knew the order of these cards the way she knew the freckles on her own arm. Somewhere between Ginger Snaps and Oatmeal Raisin there should have been a card for Molasses Spice Cookies, and there was not.

She stood very still, the box open in her lap, the brown paper crackling faintly against the window glass in a draft from somewhere, and could not, no matter how hard she reached for it, remember pulling that card herself.
