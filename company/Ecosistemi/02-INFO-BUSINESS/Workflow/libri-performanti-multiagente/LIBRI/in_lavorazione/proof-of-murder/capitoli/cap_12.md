# Diane's Confession

Diane Pruitt answered the door in a cardigan the color of weak tea, her hair pulled back loosely, and for a moment she simply looked at Maggie standing on the porch steps without saying anything at all.

"I wondered when you'd come back," she said finally, and stepped aside to let her in.

The house smelled of coffee and, faintly, of the lilies someone had sent for the wake that Diane clearly hadn't had the heart to throw out yet, their petals browning at the edges in a vase on the hall table. Diane led her into the same small sitting room where they'd first talked at the Grange hall wake, though the mood between them now felt entirely different, less like strangers finding common ground and more like two people bracing for something.

"I heard the state police are involved now," Diane said, settling into her chair. "Officially, I mean. Bill Torres came by yesterday to tell me himself, very solemn about it, like he was doing me some kind of favor."

"They are," Maggie confirmed. "Which is part of why I'm here. Diane, I need to tell you some things, and I need you to know I'm telling you because I think you deserve to hear them from someone who cares about getting this right, not from a stranger reading you a report."

Diane's hands folded in her lap, very still. "That sounds like the start of something I won't want to hear."

"Probably," Maggie admitted. "I'm sorry."

She told her carefully, in order. The recipe cards and the hidden numbers. The rezoning proposal at the library, her mother's name standing against Walter Finch's development three years ago. Ruth Ann's sighting of Harold's car at the Grange hall, alone, past midnight, the night before he died. The chairs arranged in that private half circle, the two cold cups of coffee no one had cleared away. The torn page from a green ledger, found in a trash bin behind the hall, its numbers matching her mother's hidden code exactly.

She left out that it was Nate who'd found it, and how, because that part wasn't hers to give away, but she made sure Diane understood the shape of what it meant.

By the time she finished, Diane's face had gone the color of the ash in the cold fireplace behind her, and her hands, still folded in her lap, had begun to tremble in small, involuntary movements she didn't seem to notice.

"A meeting," Diane said, very quietly. "He met someone there. That late. The night before."

"I think so, yes."

Diane got up. She crossed to the window and stood with her back to the room, and Maggie let her have the silence, because there was nothing useful to put into it.

"Do you know what I've been doing for two weeks?" Diane said at last. "I've been going through his shirts. Every one. Checking the collars." She laughed, and it came out badly. "Forty one years married and I stood in my own laundry room holding my dead husband's shirts up to the light looking for lipstick, because that was the only story I could think of that explained a man who stopped sleeping and stopped talking and started going out at eleven at night without saying where."

"Diane."

"I was so certain." She turned around. Her eyes were dry, which was somehow worse. "I had a whole life built for it. I knew how I was going to feel at the funeral, I knew which of my friends I'd tell and which I wouldn't. I had it all arranged, Maggie, I had grieved a marriage that never happened, and now you're standing in my sitting room telling me he was frightened. That he was carrying something so heavy it kept him up at night, in this house, twelve feet from me, and he never said a word."

"Maybe he couldn't."

"He could have." It came out flat and certain. "That's the part. He could have. He chose not to, and I've spent two weeks angry at him for the wrong reason entirely, and now I don't even get to be angry properly, because how do you stay angry at a man who was that afraid?" She pressed the back of her wrist briefly against her mouth. "I'd rather it had been a woman. God forgive me. At least then he'd have been happy about something."

Maggie stayed where she was. She had learned, in three years of people saying the wrong kind and the right kind of thing to her about Rose, that the worst instinct was to reach for a person too early.

"He wasn't a bad man," Diane said, quieter. "I need you to know that, whatever comes out of this. Whatever he did or let happen, he wasn't a bad man. He was a weak one, and he knew it, and I think he'd been trying to stop being one right at the end. That's not nothing, is it? Trying at the end?"

"No," Maggie said. "It isn't nothing."

Diane nodded once, twice, and then seemed to gather herself all at once, the way women of her generation did, folding the whole thing away somewhere to be dealt with privately later.

"I heard him on the phone two nights before he died," Diane said, as if reciting something she'd told herself so many times it had worn smooth, like a stone. "I told you that already, at the Grange. He said he couldn't carry it anymore. He said he needed to make it right before the festival." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I thought. God help me, Maggie, I thought it was another woman. I thought my husband of thirty-one years had found somebody else, and he was trying to work out how to leave me gently, and I was so ashamed of even thinking it that I never said a word to anyone, not even Bill Torres, because I didn't want it written down somewhere, I didn't want it to be true just because I'd said it out loud."

"Diane."

"It wasn't another woman, was it." It wasn't really a question. "It was this. Whatever this is. Money, and the festival grounds, and Rose's numbers, and some ledger somebody tore up and threw in the trash like it was nothing."

"I think so," Maggie said gently. "I think your husband was trying to fix something before it was too late. I don't think there was ever anyone else."

Diane pressed both hands over her mouth for a moment, and when she lowered them her eyes were bright and wet, though she did not quite cry. "Thirty-one years and I doubted him for the wrong reason entirely. I was so busy being humiliated I never once stopped to think he might have been drowning."

Maggie reached across the small space between their chairs and took Diane's hand, cold despite the warmth of the room. "You couldn't have known. He didn't want you to know. Whatever he was carrying, he was trying to carry it alone until he could put it right."

Diane was quiet for a long moment, staring at some middle distance past Maggie's shoulder, and when she spoke again her voice had changed, gone lower, rougher, like something long buried was finally working its way to the surface.

"There's something I haven't told anyone," she said. "Not Bill Torres. Not that nice deputy who came by asking his careful questions. Not even my sister, and I tell my sister everything."

Maggie went very still, afraid that any movement might close the door Diane had just cracked open. "What is it?"

"After Harold died, I couldn't bring myself to open his study. Not for weeks. It felt like trespassing, like I'd be finding out things he never meant for me to know, and some cowardly part of me decided I'd rather not know than know and have to live with it." She twisted the hem of her cardigan between her fingers. "The bank statements came in the mail like always. I just set them on his desk, unopened, in a little stack, like he was going to walk back in and deal with them himself. I know how that sounds."

"It doesn't sound cowardly," Maggie said. "It sounds like grief."

"Grief with a mailing address, apparently." Diane let out a small, broken laugh that had no humor in it at all. She rose from her chair, unsteady, and crossed to a rolltop desk in the corner of the room that Maggie hadn't noticed before, tucked behind a reading lamp and a framed photograph of Harold in a fishing vest, grinning at some long-ago catch. She pulled open a drawer and lifted out a small stack of envelopes, most still sealed, and sorted through them with shaking hands until she found the one she wanted.

She held it out to Maggie without quite looking at it herself.

"This one I did open. Three days ago. I told myself I was being brave, finally being brave, and then I read it and I understood why I'd been too scared to look in the first place."

Maggie took the envelope. It was a billing statement, the return address printed in small, businesslike type: GREENFIELD SELF STORAGE, RUTLAND COUNTY. The account holder's name was Harold Pruitt. The monthly charge was modest, sixty dollars, the kind of unremarkable expense that could hide in a budget for years without anyone noticing, and the account had been open, according to the anniversary date printed at the bottom of the bill, for just over three years.

"A storage unit," Maggie said slowly. "In Rutland. I didn't know Harold had any property outside of Cedar Hollow."

"Neither did I," Diane said, and her voice broke fully now, the composure she'd held onto through the wake and the funeral and every polite condolence call finally giving way. "Thirty-one years married to that man, and I didn't know he was paying for a storage unit forty minutes from here, and it's been open since right around the time Rose Doyle died, Maggie. Right around then. What in God's name has my husband been keeping in a storage unit in Rutland County for three years that he never once mentioned to me?"

Maggie looked down at the bill in her hands, at the account number and the modest monthly charge and the unit number printed near the bottom, C-14, and felt the same cold certainty settle over her that she'd felt standing in the Grange hall parking lot with Nate's flashlight beam trembling over a torn green page.

Whatever Harold Pruitt had been trying to make right before the festival, he hadn't kept all of it in a ledger or a recipe card.

Some of it, he had kept locked away in Rutland, waiting for someone brave enough, or desperate enough, to finally go and look.
