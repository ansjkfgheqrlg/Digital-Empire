# What the Pharmacist Remembers

Del Sutter had run Cedar Hollow Pharmacy for forty one years before he sold the business and retired to a farmhouse three miles outside of town, and according to nearly everyone Maggie asked, he still kept a filing cabinet of old prescription logs in his garage out of a stubbornness his late wife used to call either endearing or infuriating depending on the day.

He answered the door in a cardigan with suspenders showing beneath it, squinting at Maggie over a pair of reading glasses pushed too far down his nose, and broke into a real smile the moment he recognized the box in her hands.

"Rose's molasses cookies," he said. "Or close enough to fool an old man's memory."

"Close enough that it's her actual recipe," Maggie said. "I'd like to talk to you about her, if you have a few minutes."

He waved her inside without hesitation, sat her at a kitchen table cluttered with seed catalogs and crossword books, and listened with his hands folded while she explained, as plainly as she could bear to, what she and Nate had learned about her mother's medical file, and why she'd come to him instead of waiting for it to go through official channels that seemed, more and more, inclined to move as slowly as possible.

"I filled her lisinopril for years," Del said, when she'd finished. "Same dose, ninety day supply near the end because her insurance liked it that way. I remember because your mother used to bring me a dozen of whatever she'd baked that week whenever she came in, said it was payment for putting up with her asking a hundred questions about drug interactions. She read the pharmacy inserts front to back, every refill. Sharp as anybody I ever filled a script for."

"Dr. Ferris said the medication itself is forgiving at the right dose," Maggie said carefully. "But that it wouldn't take much to push it the other way. Wrong dose, or the wrong thing mixed in with it."

Del's smile faded into something more careful, more considering, the look of a man doing arithmetic he'd never expected to be asked to do out loud. "That's true enough. Blood pressure medications like hers work by relaxing the vessels, keeping the pressure down where it belongs. Give somebody two or three times what they're supposed to have, especially somebody her age, and you can crash their blood pressure hard enough to stop a heart that was never in danger otherwise. It would present almost exactly like a natural cardiac event. No obvious sign unless somebody ran a tox screen specifically looking for it, and nobody had cause to look, not with her history."

"Would that be hard to do? To swap her medication, or double a dose, without her noticing?"

"If somebody had a reason to be in her kitchen and knew which bottle was which, no. Not hard at all. Pills look like pills." He was quiet a moment, turning his coffee mug slowly on the table. "I did wonder, back then. Not enough to say anything, understand, there wasn't anything solid to say, just a nagging little itch a pharmacist gets sometimes when a healthy woman drops dead of a heart that never gave her trouble in the eleven years I filled her scripts. I told myself it happens. It does happen. I told myself that for three years."

Maggie leaned forward. "Do you remember anyone in particular who might have had reason to be alone in her kitchen those last few weeks? Anyone who came by regularly, who wasn't family?"

Del didn't answer right away. He got up instead, moved to a cabinet by the back door, and pulled out an old accordion folder gone soft with handling, the kind of thing Maggie suspected he'd promised his wife a dozen times he'd throw away and never had. He flipped through it until he found a slim stack of index cards, his own private records, dates and customer names in handwriting nearly as cramped as Dr. Ferris's.

"I used to keep track of who picked up what, back before everything went digital and half of it got lost in some system upgrade," he said. "Your mother's account has three pickups in her last two months I can't explain, because they weren't her signature on the log."

Maggie's pulse climbed. "Who signed for them?"

"Committee friends, mostly, the way I remember it. People running errands for a busy woman, which wasn't unusual, plenty of folks around here picked up a neighbor's prescription if they were headed that way anyhow." He ran a thumb down the page, and Maggie watched his expression shift, some old discomfort surfacing after three years of being allowed to settle. "Denise Cobb signed for one of these. Right here, see, three weeks before Rose passed. And I remember now, seeing her car outside the bakery some mornings that fall, earlier than made sense for committee business, before the ovens were even going. She used to bring your mother coffee. Said it was just being neighborly."

"Just Denise?"

"Harold Pruitt picked one up too, a little earlier in the summer, but Harold ran errands for half this town, that never struck me as odd on its own." He closed the folder gently, as if closing it too fast might undo the admission. "But it was Denise's name that came to mind first when you asked, Maggie, and I want to be honest with you about that, because I don't think that's an accident of memory. I think some part of me noticed something back then and didn't let myself finish the thought."

Maggie sat with that for a long moment, the kitchen quiet except for the tick of a clock somewhere in the next room and the distant sound of a dog barking down the road. Outside Del's window she could see the hills rolling away gold and red toward the ridge line, the same hills her mother had looked at every morning from the bakery's back window, never knowing which of her friends might already be counting the days until she stopped asking questions for good.

"Would you be willing to say that to Deputy Coleman?" she asked. "Officially, I mean. Written down."

"I'll say it to anybody who'll listen," Del said. "I should have said it three years ago. I told myself it wasn't my place, that I didn't have proof of anything, just a habit of noticing who came through my door. I let that be enough of an excuse for a long time." He looked at her steadily. "Seems like there was a lot of that going around, people telling themselves silence was the responsible choice. I'm sorry, Maggie. I mean that."

Del walked her out to the car, and at the door of the truck he stopped her with a hand on the frame.

"One more thing, and then I'll let you go, because it's been bothering me since you sat down." He squinted off toward the ridge. "Your mother came in on her own, that last time. Late October, I want to say, close to the end. She didn't need a refill, she'd have had thirty days left on the ninety day supply. She came in and asked me a question."

"What question?"

"She asked me how a person would know," Del said, "if the pills in a bottle weren't the pills on the label."

Maggie's hand went still on the door handle.

"I told her the truth, which is that for most of them you wouldn't, not by looking. Different manufacturers stamp different markings, sizes vary, but if you don't know what yours are supposed to look like in the first place you'd never notice a swap. I told her to bring the bottle in and I'd check it against the log for her." He shook his head slowly. "She said she would. She never did. And I never followed up, because people ask pharmacists odd questions all day long and half of them are about a magazine article they read. I filed it under that."

"Did you tell anyone? After."

"Who would I have told?" It came out with real bitterness, the first Maggie had heard from him. "Bill Torres, that a woman who died of a heart attack had asked me a general question about pill identification a few weeks earlier? I'd have been the crank pharmacist wasting the chief's afternoon. So I didn't. And then a year went by, and then two." He let go of the truck door. "You put that in front of your deputy along with the rest. It's not proof. But it's not nothing either, and I'd rather be a crank in a courtroom than keep sitting on it out here."

Maggie found she couldn't answer for a second. Her mother, standing at that pharmacy counter in the last weeks of her life, holding a suspicion she hadn't been able to prove and hadn't been able to let go of, asking the only expert she trusted the one question that mattered, and getting an answer three weeks too late to save her.

*She knew,* Maggie thought. *Not everything. But she knew enough to be afraid, and she went and asked anyway.*

"She'd have liked that you told me," she said finally. "She never could stand a thing left half asked."

Del smiled at that, small and sad. "No," he agreed. "She surely could not."

She thanked him, left the box of cookies on his counter despite his protests, and drove back toward town with the windows down and the cool September air pouring through the cab, Main Street already strung with bunting for the festival now only days away, banners reading CEDAR HOLLOW FOUNDER'S DAY snapping gently over the sidewalks where vendors were beginning to stake out their tents.

She still had no proof, nothing a court would look twice at, only a pharmacist's uneasy memory and a name that kept surfacing first no matter which direction she came at it from. But for the first time since she'd found Harold Pruitt slumped against her proofing drawer, Maggie understood the shape of what had happened to her mother, and she understood, with a clarity that settled cold and certain in her chest, exactly whose name belonged at the center of it.

Denise Cobb had three days left before the town crowned her its next mayor in everything but name, and Maggie intended to make sure she didn't get the chance.
