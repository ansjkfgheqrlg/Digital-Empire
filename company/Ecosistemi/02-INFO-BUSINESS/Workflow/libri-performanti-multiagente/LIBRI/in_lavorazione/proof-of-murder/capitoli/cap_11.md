# The Grange Hall at Night

"This is a spectacularly bad idea," Nate said, and unlocked the side door of the Grange hall anyway.

"You keep saying that and then doing the thing," Maggie pointed out, following him into the dark.

"I'm aware. It's becoming a pattern I should probably examine." He found the light switch, and a bank of fluorescent tubes stuttered to life overhead, buzzing faintly, casting the big open room in flat white light. "We're here because Ruth Ann Sawyer says she saw Harold's car in the lot after midnight the night before he died, and because that falls under things I should probably check out anyway. Not because you asked nicely. For the record."

"For the record," Maggie agreed, though they both knew it was exactly because she'd asked.

The Grange hall smelled the way it always had, of old wood polish and coffee and the particular must of a building that got heavy use twelve days a year and sat closed up the rest of the time. Rows of stacked folding chairs lined one wall. A long table ran along the back, home to the industrial coffee urn that had fueled every town meeting Maggie could remember since childhood, and above it hung a corkboard three layers deep in flyers, half of them for Founder's Day, faded schedules and volunteer sign-up sheets and a hand-drawn map of the festival grounds with vendor spots numbered in marker.

But it was the front of the room that stopped Maggie in the doorway.

Six folding chairs stood pulled away from the stacks, arranged in a loose half circle near the small stage at the front, facing a seventh chair set slightly apart, almost like a witness stand. It didn't look like the aftermath of a committee meeting, all rows and note-taking. It looked like something smaller, more deliberate. A conversation, not a session.

"Nobody's moved these," Maggie said slowly. "Since when?"

Nate walked a slow circle around the arrangement without touching anything, his flashlight beam sweeping the linoleum floor. "Grange only officially meets Tuesdays. Today's Thursday. If nothing's been rearranged since, that puts this setup right around."

"The night Harold died," Maggie finished.

On the small stage, two coffee cups sat abandoned on the edge, the kind with the Grange's own faded logo stamped in blue, the coffee inside them long gone cold and skinned over with a pale film. Someone hadn't cleared them away. In a town where every committee member Maggie had ever known treated cleanup duty like a moral obligation, that felt as loud as a shout.

"Two cups," Maggie said. "Two people."

Nate crouched to look at them without picking them up, his jaw tight. "I should be photographing this properly. Calling it in."

"Are you going to?"

He was quiet for a long moment, flashlight beam steady on the cups. "I already used up my one day of grace on you, Maggie. I don't have a lot of runway left before I'm the guy who compromised a homicide investigation for his high school girlfriend."

"I'm not asking you to hide anything. I'm asking you to look with me before you call it in, so you know what you're calling in." She kept her voice low, even though there was no one else in the building to hear them. "If Torres gets here first, this becomes something the state police process on their timeline. If we understand it first, we know what questions to ask when they do."

Nate exhaled through his nose, a sound she recognized from a decade ago, the sound he used to make right before he agreed to something he knew he shouldn't. "Ten minutes. Then I'm calling it in properly, chain of custody, all of it, and you're not in the report as having been here first."

"Deal."

He straightened, sweeping the flashlight beam across the little stage. "Chairs set up like this, off schedule, at night. Somebody wanted privacy. This isn't a Founder's Day planning session, Maggie. This is people who didn't want to be seen meeting anywhere public."

"Diane said Harold had been sneaking out for weeks. Committee business, he told her." Maggie looked at the seventh chair, set apart like a witness stand, and felt something cold settle in her stomach. "What if this wasn't the only time? What if this was where it had been happening, over and over, and the night before he died was just the last one?"

They searched the rest of the main room carefully, careful not to disturb anything they didn't have to, Nate documenting positions with photos on his phone as they went. Nothing else stood out, no obvious paperwork left behind, no scribbled notes. But the coffee cups and the chairs told their own story, one that matched too neatly with what Ruth Ann had seen from the road, headlights and one row of interior lights burning at half past midnight.

It was Maggie's idea to check outside.

"There's a dumpster and a couple of trash bins behind the building," she said. "For events. If somebody wanted to get rid of something in a hurry, on foot, in the dark, that's the fastest place to do it without driving anywhere."

Nate gave her a look that was half exasperation, half reluctant respect, and led the way out the back door into the cool night air. A motion light flickered on above the loading door, throwing long shadows across two municipal trash bins and a stack of folded tables leaned against the wall, left over from some past event and never brought back inside.

The first bin held nothing but paper plates and a few crushed soda cans, the ordinary debris of some earlier gathering. The second, smaller bin sat closer to the tree line, half hidden behind an overturned recycling crate, and it was in there, caught against the plastic side just above the level of wet leaves and coffee grounds, that Maggie's flashlight beam caught a torn corner of green.

"Nate."

He was beside her in two steps. Using a pen from his jacket pocket rather than his fingers, he lifted the corner of green paper free, careful and slow, and unfolded it enough to see what it was.

A single page, torn raggedly along one edge as if ripped from a bound ledger in a hurry, the paper the same distinctive shade of forest green Maggie had seen snap shut under Denise Cobb's hand at the committee office. Columns of figures ran down the page in neat, cramped handwriting, dates on the left, dollar amounts on the right, and beside several of the entries, small notations in pencil, initials and numbers that made Maggie's breath catch in her throat.

She didn't need to pull out her phone to compare it. She'd spent hours the past two nights staring at her mother's recipe cards under a heat lamp, memorizing the pattern of those margin notes until she could see them behind her closed eyes. But she pulled out her phone anyway, opened the photos she and Josie had taken, and held the screen up beside the torn page in Nate's gloved hand.

The numbers matched. Not similar. Not close. Exact, entry for entry, the same figures her mother had encoded in pencil in the corners of Molasses Spice Cookies and half a dozen other cards three years before she died, now written out plainly in a ledger that had been torn free and thrown away behind the Grange hall on the night Harold Pruitt met someone in a half circle of folding chairs for the last conversation of his life.

"That's it," Maggie whispered. "Nate, that's the same code. Exactly the same."

Nate stared at the page for a long moment, his flashlight beam trembling slightly, the only sign of how hard his hand was working to stay steady. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a deputy reciting facts he could not afford to get wrong.

"This is evidence in an active homicide investigation," he said. "I'm bagging it, I'm documenting where and when I found it, and I'm calling Torres right now, and when I do, your name does not come up as having been here. Do you understand me?"

"Nate."

"I mean it, Maggie. I've already bent this as far as it goes. If this page is what I think it is, it doesn't just connect to Harold. It connects to your mother." His eyes met hers in the thin wash of the motion light, and for a moment the careful, professional distance dropped away entirely. "Somebody tore this out of Denise Cobb's ledger and threw it away not thirty feet from where two people sat drinking coffee that never got cleared. If that's what happened here, then whoever did this is still walking around Cedar Hollow like nothing's wrong, and I am not going to be the reason you end up anywhere near them without a badge and a case file standing between you."

Maggie looked past him at the dark shape of the Grange hall, its windows black now that the interior lights had gone off on a timer, and thought of her mother sitting at that kitchen table three years ago, pencil in hand, hiding numbers inside recipes because she hadn't trusted anyone in this town enough to write them anywhere safer.

"Call it in," she said quietly. "I'll wait by the car."
