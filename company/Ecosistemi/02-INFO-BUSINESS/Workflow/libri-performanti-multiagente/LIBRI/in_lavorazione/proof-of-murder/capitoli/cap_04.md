# Rose's Numbers

They started at nine the next morning, coffee going cold at both their elbows, the recipe box open between them on the dining table with its dividers propped up like little wooden fences. Josie had come early, before the bakery hours neither of them had any bakery left to keep, and had simply said, "Show me," and Maggie had, and they had been at it since, going through the box card by card, checking each one against a spiral notebook Maggie had started keeping months ago, when she was only trying to learn her mother's recipes and not yet trying to solve anything.

"Nothing missing from Pies," Josie said, thumbing through the stack and setting it back behind its tab. "Or Preserves. It's just the one gap, then. Molasses Spice."

"The exact card Torres pulled out of Harold's coat pocket." Maggie said it slowly, testing the shape of it out loud, the way you test a stair tread before you put your full weight on it. "That's not a coincidence, Josie. Somebody took that card out of this box and gave it to Harold Pruitt, or he took it himself, and I don't understand why anyone would care about a cookie recipe enough to carry it around folded in their coat like it was, I don't know, like it was a photograph of somebody they loved."

"Unless it isn't a cookie recipe," Josie said. "Not really. Not only."

They had been circling that thought for an hour without either of them saying it plainly, and now that Josie had, Maggie felt something settle into place low in her chest, cold and certain. She reached for the box again and began pulling cards not by category this time but by feel, running her thumb along each one, tilting them toward the window light the way Torres had tilted that evidence sleeve toward her the day before.

It was Josie who found the first one. A card for Apple Cider Donuts, Rose's careful hand listing cinnamon, nutmeg, buttermilk, and down in the bottom corner, so faint it might have been an accident of the paper's grain if you weren't looking hard, a short string of pencil numbers. 4,200. Below that, smaller still, a date. September 12.

"That's not an oven temperature," Josie said. "Four thousand two hundred of anything doesn't go in a donut."

Maggie's hands had gone cold. She pulled the next card, and the next, working faster now, and found three more within twenty minutes, each one a recipe in her mother's familiar slant, each one carrying, tucked into a margin or under a corner fold, a faint graphite number and a date. 6,500, September 19. 3,100, September 24. 11,000, October 2.

"That's a lot of money," Josie said quietly, "if that's what it is."

"Founder's Day is October fourth." Maggie said it and felt the date land like a stone dropped down a well, waiting to hear the splash. "The festival my mother died at, three years ago. These are all from the month before."

They went through the whole box after that, painstaking, both of them silent except to call out numbers as they found them, until they had a dozen cards laid out across the dining table in a loose, uneven row, Rose's careful handwriting for donuts and pull apart bread and shortbread sitting above pencil figures that had nothing to do with flour or sugar at all. Some of the numbers repeated, close but not identical, as though someone had been correcting a running total. Two of the cards had initials as well as numbers, small looping letters Maggie didn't recognize as anyone's name she knew, D.C. on one, W.F. on another, tucked so small into the corner they might have passed for smudges of pencil lead.

"She was keeping books," Maggie said, and her own voice sounded strange to her, thin with the effort of saying something she didn't want to be true. "My mother was keeping some kind of ledger, hidden inside her own recipe cards, a month before she died. Josie, what if she found something. What if this is why."

"Why she died," Josie said, finishing it for her, because Maggie couldn't.

Neither of them heard the knock at first, soft, almost apologetic, at the back door that led up from the alley, and it was only the second knock, sharper, that made them both startle so hard that Josie's coffee mug rattled against its saucer. Maggie went to the window, lifted the corner of the brown paper they'd taped up the night before, and felt something in her chest loosen when she saw Nate Coleman standing on the landing in jeans and a flannel jacket instead of his uniform, hands jammed in his pockets, looking up at the window like he wasn't sure he should have come at all.

"He's off duty," she said, more to herself than to Josie, and went to let him in.

He came up the stairs slow, and in the kitchen light he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the early hour, dark half moons under his eyes, a two day shadow of stubble he didn't usually wear on shift. "I shouldn't be here," he said, by way of hello. "I want you to know that going in. I'm not here as a deputy."

"Then why are you here," Maggie said, though not unkindly.

"Because Torres sent Harold's effects up to the state lab this morning, along with a request for their major crimes unit to consult." Nate said it fast, like a man getting a hard thing over with. "That recipe card's got prints on it besides Harold's and I don't think Bill likes what that might mean, because the second those results come back, the Vermont State Police are going to want a look at everything, this case and maybe your mother's file too, and once that happens it stops being a small town thing that a chief can manage over coffee at the diner. I wanted you to hear that from me before you heard it from anybody else."

"You're risking your job telling me this."

"Probably." He said it simply, without drama, the way he'd always said hard true things back when they were seventeen and certain nothing could really touch them. His eyes moved past her then, to the dining table, to the row of cards laid out with their small pencil figures catching the morning light, and Maggie watched something in his face shift from tired concern into a different kind of attention altogether, the same stillness she'd seen come over him in the alley two mornings ago when he crouched beside Harold's body.

"What is that," he said, stepping closer, and Josie made room for him at the table without a word.

He picked up the Apple Cider Donuts card first, tilted it the way they had, found the faint 4,200 and the date beneath it, then reached for the next, and the next, laying them back down in the same uneven row, his jaw working the way it did when he was turning something over and did not like the shape of it.

"These are all dated the month before your mother died," he said finally.

"We noticed."

"And there's a card missing." He looked up at her sharply. "The one Torres pulled out of Harold's coat."

"Molasses Spice Cookies," Maggie said. "It's gone from the box. It has been for a while, I think, though I can't say when exactly, or by whose hand."

Nate was quiet for a long moment, looking down at the dozen cards spread across the table, Rose Doyle's careful, familiar handwriting sitting above numbers that added up, by Maggie's quick and horrified count, to something well past thirty thousand dollars in the space of a single autumn month. Outside, muffled by the brown paper taped over the glass, a car slowed as it passed the front of the building and then, after a moment, moved on.

"I have to tell you something," Nate said at last, "and I need you to hear all of it before you say anything back. Whatever your mother was doing with these cards, whatever this turns out to be, it's bigger than a missing recipe and it's bigger than what happened to Harold Pruitt in your kitchen. If the state police get involved, and I think they're going to, this stops being something I can manage quietly on your behalf. I want to help you get ahead of it. But I need you to trust me enough to let me try."

Maggie looked at him across the table, at the dozen cards lying open between them like a hand of cards finally turned face up after three long years, and felt, underneath the fear, something that was almost relief: the first solid thread, however cold it made her hands to hold it, in the unraveling of what had actually happened to her mother.

"Okay," she said. "Where do we start."
