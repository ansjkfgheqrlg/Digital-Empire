# The Storage Unit

The bill sat on Maggie's kitchen table where she'd left it the night before, a plain white envelope gone soft at the corners from being carried in Diane Pruitt's purse for three years. Rutland Valley Self Storage. Unit 114. Monthly auto pay, drawn from an account Maggie didn't recognize, the name on the account Harold Pruitt, the billing address his own house on Larkspur Lane, not the Grange, not anything to do with the Founder's Day Committee at all. A private thing. A secret thing.

She called Nate before seven, before she'd even started the ovens.

"You should turn this straight over to Torres," he said, though he was already in his truck, she could hear the engine, could hear him pulling out of his own driveway. "You know that's what I'm supposed to tell you."

"You're supposed to tell me a lot of things," Maggie said. "Are you coming or not?"

A pause, tires on gravel. "Give me twenty minutes. And Maggie, once we're inside, whatever's in there, it goes on the record by end of day. I mean that. One day. That's all I've got left to give you before this becomes something I can't unsee for the department."

"One day," she agreed, and hung up feeling the weight of what he was risking settle over her like a coat two sizes too heavy.

Rutland was forty minutes south, past the covered bridge and the last of the roadside stands selling the season's final sweet corn, past hillsides just beginning to rust at the edges with the first turning leaves. Josie rode in the passenger seat of Nate's truck with a thermos of coffee she'd made too strong, and nobody talked much. Maggie watched the fields slide by and thought about Harold Pruitt renting a storage unit under his own name, paying for it every month for three years, never telling his wife.

The facility was a flat gravel lot ringed with a chain link fence, two long rows of orange roll up doors baking under a sky the color of skim milk. A man in a canvas jacket met them at the office, introduced himself as Gary, looked at Nate's badge and then at the paper Diane Pruitt had signed the night before granting access to her husband's account, and chewed the inside of his cheek for a long moment.

"Technically I'm supposed to see a death certificate and probate paperwork before I let anybody but the account holder in," he said.

"Technically you're supposed to," Nate agreed. "I'm asking you as a courtesy, not a warrant, because I don't have one yet and I'd rather not spend the morning driving back to get one while whoever else might be interested in this unit gets there first."

Gary studied him, then studied Maggie, something in his face softening. "You're the baker. The one on the news."

"I'm the one whose bakery it happened in," Maggie said. "I didn't do it."

Gary handed over the master key without another word.

The door came up with a shriek of old metal, and dust rolled out into the daylight like something exhaling after a long time holding its breath. The unit was deeper than it looked from outside, maybe ten feet by fifteen, lined on both sides with cardboard boxes stacked to shoulder height, each one labeled in Harold's blocky handwriting. FDC 2020. FDC 2021. FDC RECEIPTS, DO NOT DISCARD.

Josie let out a low whistle. "He kept everything."

"Guilty men usually do," Nate said, pulling on a pair of gloves from his jacket pocket. "They can't stand to destroy the evidence of what they did, even when it would save them. Some part of them wants to be caught."

They worked through the boxes methodically, Nate photographing each page before anyone touched it, a habit Maggie understood now was as much for her protection as for the case. The first boxes held bank statements for an account she'd never heard of, the Founder's Day Legacy Fund, opened the same year Walter Finch had first proposed rezoning the festival grounds. Deposits that matched the committee's public ledgers for a while, and then, starting three years back, withdrawals that didn't match anything. Land survey fees paid to a firm in Burlington. An appraisal invoice for eleven acres behind the Grange, the exact parcel Maggie had seen marked on the maps in Denise Cobb's office. All of it paid from committee funds that should have gone toward the festival, toward the scholarship fund, toward the county fair booth and the fireworks and the ribbon for the pie contest.

"This is what my mother found," Maggie said quietly, sitting back on her heels with a spreadsheet in her hands, columns of numbers that lined up, digit for digit, with the pencil marks hidden in the margins of Rose's recipe cards. "This is exactly it."

"And Harold kept a copy of everything," Nate said. "For three years. Sitting on it."

Near the back of the unit, tucked behind the last box, sat a shoebox that didn't match the others, its cardboard softer, its corners worn the way things get worn from being handled again and again rather than left alone. Maggie lifted the lid herself before Nate could stop her.

Inside, resting on top of a folded dish towel, was a single brass key on a loop of twine, and taped to the twine a strip of masking tape in Harold's handwriting: ROSE'S. BACK DOOR. DO NOT LOSE.

Maggie's breath caught. She knew that key. She'd had one just like it cut new when she took over the bakery, because the old lock still worked and there'd seemed no reason to change what her mother had built. Harold Pruitt had a key to her mother's back door. A spare, kept for years, hidden in a storage unit forty minutes from town.

"Why would he have that," Josie said, her voice gone thin.

"I don't know," Maggie said, though something cold was moving through her now, the same cold she'd felt kneeling over Harold's body in her own kitchen. She set the key aside and lifted the towel beneath it.

There was a card underneath. An ordinary sympathy card, the kind sold at any pharmacy, a watercolor image of white lilacs on the front and, in gold script beneath them, the words With Deepest Sympathy. It had never been mailed. No stamp, no postmark, the envelope unsealed, the flap simply tucked in rather than licked shut, as though Harold had meant to send it and then, at the last moment, couldn't make himself finish the act.

Maggie's hands were shaking as she opened it. Inside, Harold's handwriting filled the card front and back, crossed out in places, rewritten, the ink darker where he'd pressed hard enough to nearly tear the paper.

Dear Maggie,

I don't expect you to read this and think well of me. You have every right not to. I was there the week before your mother passed, more times than I can bring myself to count, and I knew things I never said, not to her, not to you, not to anyone who could have stopped what happened. I told myself I was protecting people who needed protecting. I understand now that I was only protecting myself.

Your mother trusted the wrong people that fall, and God help me, I was one of them. I have carried that every day since, and it has not gotten lighter with time the way people say these things do. It has gotten heavier.

I don't know how to make this right. I'm not sure it can be made right. But I owe you the truth before I run out of chances to give it to you, and I am afraid, Maggie. I am so afraid of what will happen if I say it out loud. I hope one day, whatever I decide to do, you can find it in yourself to forgive an old man who ran out of time to be brave.

H.P.

There was no date, but Maggie didn't need one. She thought of Diane, overhearing Harold on the phone two nights before he died, telling someone named Denise that he couldn't carry something anymore and needed to make it right before the festival. He had been trying, at the very end, to become the man who mailed this card instead of the man who hid it in a shoebox for three years.

Someone had stopped him before he got the chance.

"Maggie." Nate's hand was on her shoulder, gentle, and she realized she'd gone very still, the card trembling between her fingers. "We have to bag this. All of it. I have to call it in."

"I know." She let him take it from her, watched him slide it into an evidence sleeve with the same careful hands that had once, a long time ago, held hers walking home from the Founder's Day fireworks. "Nate. He knew something happened to her. Not just the money. Something happened to my mother, and he knew, and he let three years go by."

"We're going to find out what," Nate said, and there was nothing soft in his voice now, only the flat certainty of a man who had stopped being able to pretend this was only about Harold Pruitt. "I promise you that. But it starts with proving your mother's heart attack was exactly what everyone assumed it was. Or proving it wasn't."

Maggie looked down at the spare key still resting in the shoebox, at the twine gone gray with age, at the two words Harold had written on it so long ago. Rose's. Back door.

She thought of every early morning she'd let herself into that same door alone, never once imagining that someone else already had a way in.
