# Open for Business, Sort Of

The crime scene tape came down at six that morning, peeled off the doorframe by a tech named Ruiz who wouldn't meet Maggie's eyes while he did it. He handed her a clipboard, had her sign for the return of her own kitchen, and told her in a flat, official voice that the proofing drawer where Harold Pruitt's body had been found was staying with the county lab for now, along with three feet of countertop they'd cut out and bagged like it was evidence instead of soapstone. Chief Torres had signed off on the release "with conditions," which turned out to mean a strip of yellow tape still cordoning off the back corner of the kitchen, a No Entry sign taped to the wall where her second proofer used to sit, and a promise that a deputy might stop by at any hour to "confirm compliance."

Maggie stood in the doorway for a long moment before she made herself walk in.

The space still smelled wrong. Under the flour and yeast and the ghost of cinnamon there was something chemical, a fingerprint-powder smell that clung to the stainless steel no matter how many times she'd wiped it down the night before. She rolled her shoulders, tied on her apron, and got to work, because the alternative was standing still and thinking, and she couldn't afford to think, not this morning.

By five thirty she had two batches of sourdough in the oven and a tray of molasses spice cookies cooling on the rack, a small, deliberate act of defiance she hadn't planned until her hands were already measuring out the blackstrap. If people wanted to whisper about that recipe card, she'd give them something to whisper about with a full mouth.

Josie arrived at six, dropped her bag behind the counter, and took one look at Maggie's face before saying, "Okay. Tell me the plan."

"Open at seven like always. Smile at everybody. Don't explain anything unless they ask twice."

"And if Torres's deputy shows up to check on us?"

"Offer him a muffin and act surprised he doesn't already know we're not criminals."

Josie snorted, but her eyes were tired, and Maggie could see she hadn't slept much either.

They unlocked the front door at seven sharp. For the first twenty minutes, nobody came.

Maggie stood at the display case rearranging trays that didn't need rearranging, watching the empty sidewalk through the window they'd finally uncovered from its brown paper the night before. Then Mrs. Whitfield's ancient green Buick eased into a spot out front, and the older woman came through the door moving slow and steady, the way she always did, cane tapping the tile.

"I'll have my usual," Mrs. Whitfield announced, loud enough to carry to the back room, "and don't you dare give me that look like you're waiting for me to ask about all this nonsense. Your mother made the best sticky buns in three counties and you're not far behind her, and that's the only thing I care to discuss this morning."

"Thank you," Maggie said, and meant it more than the words could carry.

But not everyone who walked in that morning had Mrs. Whitfield's loyalty. A woman Maggie didn't recognize, maybe a tourist getting an early start on leaf season, stood at the case for a long moment, glanced toward the kitchen's swinging door, and then asked Josie in a stage whisper, "Is this the place? Where that man was found?"

"This is a bakery," Josie said pleasantly, ringing up a loaf of rye for the customer behind her without breaking stride. "Would you like a croissant or not?"

The woman bought a croissant. She also took three photos of the storefront on her way out, and Maggie watched through the window as she showed them to someone on her phone before driving off.

It went like that all morning, a strange braided rope of kindness and suspicion. The Fenwick brothers came in for their coffee and cinnamon rolls same as every Tuesday and Thursday and made a point of sitting at the window table where everyone driving by could see them enjoying themselves. A man Maggie didn't know poked his head in, looked around like he was casing a museum exhibit, and left without buying anything. Two women from the quilting circle bought a dozen muffins each "for the meeting," which Maggie suspected meant for gossiping, but their money spent the same as anyone's.

By noon, Josie had fielded no fewer than six variations of "So did you know him well?" and "Terrible thing, just terrible, were you the one who found him?" She answered each one the same way, pleasant and unbothered, and Maggie loved her a little more every time she did it.

"You're better at this than I am," Maggie admitted during a lull, leaning against the counter.

"I've had more practice being underestimated," Josie said. "People say things around me they wouldn't say to your face. It's a skill."

"Anything useful?"

Josie considered. "Ruth Ann's been busy. Two different customers mentioned hearing you and Harold had some kind of contract dispute. One said she heard it from Ruth Ann directly at the diner."

Maggie's jaw tightened, but she filed it away. She'd deal with Ruth Ann Sawyer soon enough.

They closed at four, later than usual because a small, steady trickle of afternoon customers kept them from locking up on time, and Maggie counted that as a win even as her feet ached and her nerves felt scraped thin. She was wiping down the last table when Josie came out of the back with her coat already on.

"I had an idea," Josie said. "About the numbers on the cards."

"I'm listening."

"The library keeps old town records. Council minutes, committee filings, all of it, going back decades. Mrs. Okafor's the archivist three afternoons a week, and Thursday's one of them. If your mom was tracking something to do with Founder's Day money, there might be a paper trail that isn't locked in Denise Cobb's desk."

Maggie looked at the clock. It was nearly five. "Library closes at eight on Thursdays. Let's go before I lose my nerve."

The Cedar Hollow Free Library occupied a converted Methodist chapel on Elm Street, all dark wood and stained glass repurposed into reading nooks, and Mrs. Okafor turned out to be a brisk, delighted woman in her sixties who lit up at the mere mention of old committee records.

"Nobody ever asks for these," she said, leading them down a narrow staircase into the basement archive, where rows of gray document boxes sat on metal shelving under fluorescent light. "Everyone wants the local history section, the old photographs, the genealogy records. Nobody wants meeting minutes. But I love meeting minutes. They tell you what a town actually cares about, not what it says it cares about."

She found the Founder's Day Committee boxes without much trouble, pulling three from a shelf labeled TOWN COMMITTEES 2019 to PRESENT and setting them on a long table under better light.

"Take your time," she said. "I'll be at the desk upstairs if you need anything."

They started with the year before Rose died, working forward, Maggie reading while Josie made notes on her phone. Most of it was exactly as dull as Mrs. Okafor had promised: motions to approve the parade route, votes on vendor fees, a long and surprisingly heated debate about whether the pie-eating contest should allow gluten-free entries.

Then, three years back, in the minutes for a special session held in July, a different kind of item appeared.

REZONING PROPOSAL, MEADOW CREEK COMMONS PARCEL. Motion by W. Finch, representing Finch Development LLC, to recommend Town Council rezone the eastern six acres of the Meadow Creek festival grounds from Public Recreational to Mixed Commercial, contingent on approval of a land improvement fund to be jointly administered by the Founder's Day Committee and Finch Development LLC.

Maggie read it twice. "That's the festival grounds. That's where they set up every year."

"Six acres of it," Josie said, leaning over her shoulder. "Look what's under it."

R. Doyle (Treasurer) objected, citing lack of independent audit of proposed land improvement fund. Motion to table pending audit, seconded by H. Pruitt. Motion carried, 4 to 2.

Maggie's chest went tight. Her mother's name, right there in black ink, standing between Walter Finch and six acres of public land. And Harold seconding the motion to slow it down. Not opposing it outright, but not letting it through either.

They kept reading. The audit never appeared in the minutes again. The rezoning proposal simply vanished from the record after that July meeting, no vote, no resolution, no follow-up, as if the whole matter had been quietly set aside and left to gather dust, right around the same time her mother started writing numbers in the margins of her recipe cards. Right around the same time, Maggie realized with a cold drop in her stomach, that her mother had less than a month left to live.

"He's been trying to get this land for three years," Josie said quietly. "And it just stopped. No audit, no vote, nothing."

"Or it didn't stop," Maggie said. "Maybe it just went somewhere the minutes don't show."

She thought of the green ledger snapping shut under Denise Cobb's hand. She thought of the appraisal documents spread across Walter Finch's desk, labeled with a parcel number she hadn't paid attention to at the time but would bet everything she had matched Meadow Creek Commons.

"We need copies of this," Maggie said, already reaching for her phone to photograph the page. "All of it. Every page that mentions Finch or the land fund or my mother's objections."

Josie was already photographing the facing page. "Maggie. If this is what your mom found. If this is what got her killed three years ago and it never actually stopped."

"Then it's still happening," Maggie finished. "And somebody is still protecting it."

Outside, the streetlights had come on along Elm Street, throwing long amber pools across the sidewalk, and somewhere down the block a car idled at the curb a beat too long before pulling away. Maggie didn't see who was driving. She wasn't sure she wanted to.
