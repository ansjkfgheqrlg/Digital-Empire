# Committee Business

The Founder's Day Committee kept its office in a converted storefront on Elm Street, wedged between the hardware store and an insurance agency, with a hand painted sign in the window that had said CEDAR HOLLOW FOUNDER'S DAY, EST. 1962 for as long as Maggie could remember. She stood on the sidewalk for a moment before going in, rehearsing what she needed to say. Just paperwork. Just the canceled contract forms, so she could square away the deposit and stop pretending the festival order was still happening. Nothing else. In and out.

The bell over the door jangled when she pushed inside, and the smell hit her first, dust and old paper and the particular mustiness of a building that had once been a dry goods store and never quite lost the smell of burlap. Two gray metal desks sat at angles to each other under a bank of fluorescent lights, one stacked with festival banners and folded bunting, the other bare except for a laptop, a calculator, and a green ledger book with a cracked spine.

Denise Cobb sat behind the second desk, and she looked up the moment the bell rang, her face arranging itself into an expression of warm, practiced sympathy so quickly that Maggie almost missed the half second before it, when something flatter and more watchful had been there instead.

"Maggie. Oh, honey, come in, come in." Denise rose from her chair, smoothing her blazer, and came around the desk with both hands extended as though she meant to take Maggie's in hers. Maggie let her, because refusing seemed like more trouble than it was worth. Denise's hands were cool and dry, her grip firm. "How are you holding up? I've been meaning to call. I know this whole business has been a nightmare for you, of all people, and on top of losing the festival order too. It's a lot."

"I'm managing," Maggie said. "I actually came in about the contract. I need the cancellation paperwork so I can get the deposit sorted out with my accountant before things get more complicated than they already are."

"Of course, of course. Sit, sit." Denise gestured at a wooden chair across from her desk, and Maggie sat, glancing without meaning to toward the second desk, where a man in a checked shirt and khakis was bent over a spread of blueprints and surveyor's maps, a mechanical pencil tucked behind one ear.

"Walter, you remember Maggie Doyle," Denise said. "Rose's daughter. She's taken over the bakery."

Walter Finch looked up and offered a smile that didn't quite reach his eyes, the polished, slightly too smooth smile of a man who had spent thirty years selling things to people. "Of course. Terrible thing, what happened. Terrible for the town." He had the look of a man perpetually calculating something, a faint crease between his brows even when his mouth was smiling. "I'm sorry for your troubles, Ms. Doyle. Cedar Hollow's lucky the bakery's still in the family, whatever else is going on."

"Thank you," Maggie said, and looked back at the papers spread across his desk before she could stop herself. Land surveys, she registered, county parcel maps, and something that looked like an appraisal document with a lot of numbers circled in red pen. FESTIVAL GROUNDS PARCEL, one heading read, in bold at the top of a page half covered by his forearm.

Walter noticed her looking and shifted the papers with an unhurried motion, stacking them into a neater pile, angling the top sheet face down. "Boring stuff," he said easily. "Zoning business. You know how it is, always something with the county."

Denise had returned to her side of the desk and was rummaging through a file drawer, pulling out a manila folder with the bakery's name typed on the tab. "Here we are. Cancellation of vendor agreement, festival deposit refund request, and I'll need you to initial here and here." She slid the papers across the desk, along with a pen, and Maggie began scanning them, more out of habit than real attention, her mind still snagged on the red circled numbers on Walter's desk.

"I have to say," Denise went on, settling back into her chair, folding her hands on the desktop, "I hope you know that nobody on this committee believes for one second that you had anything to do with what happened to Harold. It's just an awful, awful coincidence, the whole thing happening in your kitchen. Awful timing, is what it is."

"Some people in town seem to believe otherwise," Maggie said, signing where indicated, not looking up.

"People love to talk. You know how Cedar Hollow is. Give it a week and they'll be talking about something else." Denise's voice was smooth, almost soothing, but there was something underneath it that Maggie couldn't quite place, a watchfulness that didn't match the sympathetic words. "How is the investigation going, do you know? I imagine Bill's told you more than he's told the rest of us."

"He hasn't told me much of anything," Maggie said. "I'm apparently more of a person of interest than someone he confides in."

"Well, that's ridiculous," Denise said, a shade too quickly. "I'll say something to him. We can't have him wasting time looking sideways at you when there's a real killer out there somewhere."

Maggie looked up at that. "Do you have any idea who it might be? You worked with Harold for years. Did he seem worried about anything lately? Diane mentioned he'd been slipping out at night to meetings he wouldn't explain."

Something moved behind Denise's eyes, there and gone so fast that Maggie would have missed it if she hadn't been watching for exactly that kind of flicker. "Harold was always fussing over some committee detail or other," Denise said, her voice perfectly even. "The man took Founder's Day more seriously than his own doctor's appointments, God rest him. If he was out at night, it was probably some vendor crisis or a permit issue. He never learned to let anything wait until morning."

It was, Maggie thought, a very complete answer to a question she hadn't asked in that much detail. She hadn't mentioned nighttime specifically, only that Diane had noticed him leaving.

Her eyes drifted, almost involuntarily, toward the green ledger book sitting at Denise's elbow, its cover worn pale at the corners from handling, a rubber band wrapped twice around it to keep loose papers from sliding free. She hadn't meant to stare. But something about the careful, deliberate way it sat there, separate from the rest of the desk clutter, close enough for Denise to reach without looking, snagged her attention the way an out of place object always does.

Denise's hand moved before Maggie had even fully registered where she was looking. In one smooth motion the ledger snapped shut, the sound sharp and final in the quiet office, and Denise slid it into the open drawer at her knee and pushed the drawer closed with her hip.

"Sorry," Denise said, with a small, embarrassed laugh that didn't reach her eyes. "Committee finances are an ongoing headache, you would not believe how far behind we are on some of these accounts, half the vendors from three festivals ago still haven't been reconciled properly. It's mortifying, honestly, the state of it. I keep meaning to hire a real bookkeeper instead of doing it all myself, but you know how these small town budgets are."

"I wasn't trying to pry," Maggie said, which was mostly true.

"Oh, I know, I know, don't mind me, I'm just sensitive about it." Denise waved a hand, the bracelets on her wrist clicking together. "It's embarrassing, is all. Old debts nobody wants to talk about. Nothing exciting, I promise you, just tedious old numbers."

But her voice had gone up half a register, just slightly, the pitch of someone talking a beat too fast to fill a silence, and her hand, Maggie noticed, had not left the closed drawer, resting flat against the metal front of it as if to keep it from springing back open on its own.

At the other desk, Walter Finch had gone very still over his surveyor's maps, his mechanical pencil motionless between his fingers, and though he did not look up, Maggie had the distinct impression that he was listening to every word of the exchange with far more attention than the blueprints in front of him.

"Well," Maggie said, sliding the signed papers back across the desk, "I appreciate you getting this squared away so quickly."

"Of course, of course. Anything for Rose's girl." Denise's smile came back on, full wattage, the earlier flicker of something colder folded away as neatly as the ledger had been. She stood, extending her hand again, and Maggie shook it, feeling the same cool, firm grip as before, though this time she noticed a faint tremor in it, barely perceptible, quickly controlled. "You let me know if there's anything the committee can do. We take care of our own in this town."

"I'll remember that," Maggie said.

She gathered her copy of the paperwork and turned toward the door, and as she did, her eyes caught Walter Finch's for just a moment. He held her gaze a beat too long, something unreadable in his expression, not quite a warning and not quite an apology, before he dropped his eyes back to his maps and picked up his pencil again.

The bell jangled behind her as she stepped back out onto Elm Street, and she stood on the sidewalk for a moment in the thin autumn sunlight, replaying the sound of that ledger snapping shut, the too smooth answer about nighttime meetings Denise shouldn't have known were nighttime at all, and the way Walter Finch's hand had gone still over a parcel map labeled FESTIVAL GROUNDS.

She had gone in for a cancellation form. She walked out with the growing, uneasy certainty that she had just watched two people work very hard to seem unbothered.
