# Inside the Proofing Room

"We should get back out there," Maggie said, and heard how thin her own voice sounded. "They'll be looking for me for the raffle."

"They won't miss you for five minutes." Denise's hand closed around Maggie's wrist, not hard, not yet, but with a grip that left no room for pulling away without a scene. "Walk with me. I want to talk to you somewhere quieter."

"Denise, I really need to get back."

"Maggie." The single syllable of her name came out flat and final, and underneath the pleasant mask Denise had worn for three years, for longer than that, Maggie caught a glimpse of something patient and merciless, the thing that had let a woman sit on committees and shake hands and run for office while carrying what she carried. "We're going to walk to your bakery. It's right there, isn't it, just past the fairgrounds. We're going to have a private conversation, and then you're going to go back to your raffle, and everything is going to be fine. Do you understand me?"

The pressure at Maggie's wrist told her a different story than the words did. She looked past Denise's shoulder, through the gap in the tent flap, for a flannel shirt, a balloon animal, a camera bag that wasn't really about photographs. She saw none of them. The crowd had shifted, thickened around the cake table, and whatever plainclothes officers Priya had stationed were somewhere in that crush, watching a tent flap that Maggie was now being walked directly away from.

She thought of screaming. She thought of what would happen if she screamed and Denise simply let go of her wrist, smiled, said something about Maggie's nerves being frayed from the stress of the last two weeks, and the whole town nodded along, because that was the story they already believed.

She let herself be walked.

The bakery sat two hundred yards past the last row of festival booths, its front windows still faintly striped with the shadow of tape marks from where the brown paper had been, its striped awning rolled up for the season. Denise steered her around to the back, to the delivery door Maggie had had rekeyed twice now, and produced, from her coat pocket, a key.

Maggie stared at it. "How do you have a key to my bakery?"

"Harold's spare." Denise said it like it explained everything, and in a way it did. "He kept one. For emergencies, he always said. I took it off his ring the night he died, before anyone thought to look." She turned it in the lock, and the door swung open onto the dim kitchen, flour dust still faintly visible on the steel counters, the proofing drawer standing closed now where it had once stood open with a dead man against it. "Inside."

"Denise, whatever this is, we can talk about it out there, in front of people, that's better for both of us—"

"Inside," Denise said again, and this time there was a small pistol in her other hand, low, close to her body, angled at nothing and everything at once. Maggie's whole body went cold and very, very still. "I'm not going to hurt you if you cooperate. I promise you that. I just need five minutes to think, and I can't think out there."

Maggie stepped into the kitchen. The door shut behind her with a click that sounded enormous, and she heard the deadbolt turn.

For a moment neither of them said anything. Denise stood with her back against the locked door, the gun lowered now but still in her hand, breathing hard through her nose like a woman working herself up to something she'd been circling for a long time.

"You know," Denise said finally. "I could see it. In your face, back there. You knew I wasn't supposed to know about that drawer."

"I don't know what you're talking about." It came out weak, and they both heard it.

"Don't." Denise's voice cracked, just slightly, the first honest sound Maggie had heard from her in two weeks of careful, managed warmth. "Don't do that. Not now. I am so tired, Maggie. I have been so tired for three years, and I looked at your face and I knew, I knew you'd already put it together, all of it, and I am done pretending I don't understand what that means."

Maggie's back found the edge of the stainless counter. She kept her eyes on Denise, on the gun, on the door six feet behind her that might as well have been six miles.

"Then tell me," Maggie said, low and steady, the way you'd talk to something wounded and cornered, because that was exactly what stood in front of her. "Tell me what happened."

Denise laughed, one short, broken sound. "You want a confession. Like on television." She shook her head, but she was talking now, the words coming loose like something long dammed up. "Harold was going to ruin everything. Twenty years I built this town's respect, Maggie. Twenty years of bake sales and ribbon cuttings and shaking every hand in this county, and I was three weeks from announcing for mayor, and that man decided, after three years of silence, that he suddenly had a conscience."

"The night at the Grange," Maggie said. "You met him there."

"He called me. Two nights before, he called me and said he couldn't sleep anymore, he was going to go to the state police, tell them everything, about the land reclassification, about the funds we moved through Walter's development, about your mother." Denise's voice thinned on the last word. "I met him at the Grange to talk him out of it. I brought coffee like it was any other committee meeting. He wouldn't budge. He said he owed it to Rose. He said he'd been a coward for three years and he wasn't going to die a coward."

"So you followed him here."

"He came here on his own, the fool, in the middle of the night, said he wanted to see the kitchen, said something about needing to look Rose's daughter's bakery in the eye before he turned himself in, some sentimental nonsense." Denise's jaw tightened. "I followed him. I tried one more time to talk sense into him, right there by that drawer of yours, and he told me he was calling the state police in the morning no matter what I said. So I hit him. With whatever was closest. I didn't plan it, if that matters to you, I didn't walk in here planning to kill him, but I am not sorry it stopped him."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere far off, muffled by walls and distance, the brass band kept playing, the same ordinary festival sounds rolling on without any idea what was happening two hundred yards away.

"And my mother," Maggie said. Her voice barely held. "You did the same thing to her."

Denise's eyes, when they came up, had nothing warm left in them at all. "Your mother wouldn't let it go. The reclassification, the numbers, she kept digging and digging, hiding it all in those ridiculous recipe cards like I wouldn't figure out what she was doing. I only meant to slow her down. Buy time before the vote. I never meant for her heart to actually stop, Maggie, you have to believe that much."

"I don't have to believe anything you say." Maggie's hands found the edge of the counter behind her and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "You murdered her."

"I gave her too much of her own medicine," Denise said, and the calm, reasonable way she said it was somehow worse than if she'd screamed it. "That's all. A doctor would call it an accident."

"A doctor didn't call it anything, because there was never an autopsy, because you made sure of that too."

Something in Denise's face shut all the way down then, the last thin membrane of the woman who baked casseroles for the sick and organized the Founder's Day raffle finally gone, and what was left underneath was flat and frightening. Her eyes went to the counter beside the proofing drawer, to the marble rolling pin sitting there from that morning's crust work, heavy, oak handled, and Maggie watched her decide.

"I'm sorry," Denise said, and didn't sound sorry at all. "I really am. But I can't let you walk out of here and say any of this."

She crossed the kitchen fast, faster than a woman her age had any business moving, and closed her hand around the rolling pin.

Maggie threw herself sideways along the counter, knocking a tray of proofing baskets to the floor with a clatter, scrambling for the back hallway, for anything. Denise swung, and the rolling pin came down where Maggie's head had been a half second before, splintering against the stainless counter edge with a crack like a gunshot.

"Maggie!" The voice came from outside, muffled through the door, Josie's voice, high and frightened, and then a second later, closer, Nate's, and the sound of a shoulder hitting the delivery door once, twice.

Denise raised the broken pin again, her face wild now, past reasoning, and Maggie got both hands up to block as the door gave way behind them both in a crash of splintering wood and Nate came through it low and fast with two uniformed state troopers right behind him, and the last thing Maggie saw before someone's arm swept her clear was Nate's hand closing around Denise Cobb's wrist and twisting the broken rolling pin out of it like it weighed nothing at all.
