# Dough and a Dead Man

The frost had come early to Cedar Hollow that year, silvering the maples along Elm Street a full week before the leaves were ready for it, and Maggie Doyle noticed it the way she noticed everything lately: with a small, private ache, because her mother would have said something about it. Rose used to stand at the bakery's back door with her coffee and declare the frost dates like a farmer reading almanacs, and Maggie had never once thought to write any of it down.

She parked her ancient Subaru in the alley behind the bakery at four fifteen in the morning, three days before Founder's Day, and killed the engine. The dashboard clock glowed orange in the dark. Somewhere down the block a dog barked twice and quit. The town square, two streets over, was already strung with the first lines of white bulb lights for the festival, looping between the gazebo posts like something waiting to be switched on.

Four hundred rolls. That was the order. Four hundred yeast rolls for the Founder's Day pancake breakfast, plus two hundred cider donuts, plus the six pull apart loaves the historical society wanted for their raffle table, and Maggie had exactly three days to prove to this town that Cedar Hollow Bakery hadn't died with her mother.

She let herself in through the back, the key sticking the way it always did in the cold, and flipped the lights.

The kitchen still smelled faintly of fresh paint under the flour and yeast, a smell she loved almost as much as she resented it, because every dollar of that paint had come out of the pastry career she'd left in Boston, the one she didn't let herself think about before six a.m. New subway tile behind the ovens. A rebuilt walk in cooler. The big steel proofing cabinet, secondhand but solid, that she'd talked the seller down to eleven hundred dollars for and then spent a weekend scrubbing until it gleamed. She'd signed the loan papers in June. She had told herself, standing in this kitchen at midnight more nights than she could count, that if she could just get through Founder's Day, if the town could taste what she could do, it would be enough.

She hung her jacket on the hook by the door, tied her apron, and reached for the light switch over the prep counter.

That was when she saw the proofing drawer was open.

Not the cooler, the proofer, the tall cabinet where she'd left four dozen rolls tucked under damp towels to rise overnight at exactly eighty two degrees, timed so they'd be ready to shape and bake the moment she walked in. The bottom drawer hung open on its track, and something had slumped against it, heavy enough to bow the thin steel front.

For one absurd second her only thought was that the dough had overproofed and collapsed, that some idiot mechanical failure had ruined six hours of work, and she actually took a step forward to check the thermostat before her eyes caught up with what she was looking at.

A man's shoulder. A gray wool coat, county fair name tag still clipped crooked to the lapel. Harold Pruitt's face was turned toward her, slack and gray in the fluorescent light, one hand fallen open on the tile with flour dusted white across the knuckles, and the ruined dough from the drawer had slid out over his shoulder in a long pale wave, like something trying to bury him where he sat.

Maggie did not scream. She would think about that later, turning it over, wondering what it said about her that the sound that came out of her was closer to a gasp cut off at the root, and then nothing at all, just her own breathing, too loud in the quiet kitchen.

She knew him. Everyone in Cedar Hollow knew Harold Pruitt, president of the Founder's Day Committee for going on nine years, the man who opened the festival every autumn from the gazebo steps with the same speech about the founding families and the same joke about the weather that got a little more tired every year. He had come into the bakery just last week, in fact, to ask about the pull apart loaves for the raffle table, and he'd complimented her mother's old recipe box sitting on the counter, said Rose always did make the best cinnamon rolls in three counties, God rest her.

He was not going to open the festival this year.

Maggie's hand found the counter edge before she understood she was reaching for it. She made herself look away from his face, toward the phone in her apron pocket, and her fingers were shaking badly enough that she had to try twice to unlock the screen.

"Nine one one, what's your emergency."

"There's a man." Her voice came out thin and strange, someone else's voice. "There's a man in my kitchen, I think he's dead, I think Harold Pruitt is dead, he's in my proofing drawer, oh God, he's in the drawer."

"Ma'am, I need you to tell me your location."

"Cedar Hollow Bakery. Main Street. The, the alley door's open, I just got here, I found him like this, I didn't touch anything, I swear I didn't touch anything."

"Okay. Stay on the line with me and step outside if you can. Is anyone else in the building?"

"I don't know. I don't think so." She was already backing toward the door, her eyes still on Harold's slumped shoulder, the dough spilling over it in that grotesque pale wave, and she thought absurdly, guiltily, of the four hundred rolls, of the three days she didn't have anymore.

The dispatcher kept talking, calm and practiced, and Maggie answered in pieces while she backed out into the alley and stood there in the cold with her apron still tied around her waist, watching her own breath cloud in front of her. The frost had silvered the dumpster lid. Somewhere a screen door banged. She realized she was crying only when she tasted salt.

The patrol car came fast, no siren yet, just the strobe of blue and red bouncing off the brick walls of the alley, and Maggie's stomach dropped in a different way entirely when she saw who got out of it.

Nate Coleman had not changed all that much in twelve years, not really, just filled out some through the shoulders and traded his old crooked smile for something more careful, more guarded, the kind of face a person built when they'd spent a decade dealing with car wrecks and domestic calls in a county too small to hide from any of it. He'd been the boy who used to sneak her out the back of this very bakery after closing so they could sit on the loading dock and split a stolen cruller, seventeen years old and certain the whole world was theirs. Now he was Deputy Coleman, hand already resting near his belt as he came up the alley, and his eyes found her face first, checked her over for injury, and only then flicked to the open door behind her.

"Maggie." His voice dropped, gentled, the way it used to when he was trying to talk her down off some teenage drama. "You're okay? You're not hurt?"

"I'm fine. It's Harold. Harold Pruitt, he's, Nate, he's in the proofer, I don't understand how he got in there, I locked up last night, I always lock up."

"Stay right here." He said it kindly but it was not a request. He drew his radio instead of his weapon and thumbed it as he moved past her toward the door, calling in a ten code she didn't recognize, requesting the chief, requesting the county coroner, requesting that Main Street be blocked at both ends of the block. Through the open door she watched him crouch beside Harold's body without touching it, watched his jaw tighten in the harsh kitchen light, watched him press two fingers briefly to the man's throat out of what looked like habit rather than hope.

He came back out a minute later, and something in his face had gone very still and very official, a mask sliding down over the boy she used to know.

"I need you to wait out front," he said. "Don't go back in. Not through this door, not through the front, not for anything, you understand? This whole kitchen is a scene now."

"My rolls are in there. The festival order, I have three days, Nate, I have four hundred rolls due Saturday morning and I can't just, the yeast will be shot by the time anybody lets me back in there."

The moment she said it she heard how it sounded, and from the flicker in his eyes she could tell he'd heard it too, some distant, cold part of him already filing it away the way a cop files everything away. She wanted to take it back. A man was dead ten feet from where she stood and she was talking about bread.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I don't know why I said that."

"It's shock. It does strange things." He said it gently enough, but he was already reaching for the roll of yellow tape clipped to his belt, and she understood, watching him string it across her own back door, across the doorway she had walked through every morning for three months believing this bakery was finally, finally going to be hers, that whatever came next was not going to be simple, and it was not going to be quick.

Down the block, a light came on in an upstairs window. Then another. Cedar Hollow was waking up, and by the time the sun cleared the ridge behind the church steeple, there would not be a soul in town who hadn't heard that Harold Pruitt was dead, and that they'd found him in Rose Doyle's daughter's kitchen.

Maggie stood on the sidewalk in the gray predawn cold, her apron still tied at her waist, and watched the tape go up strip by strip between the doorframes of the only thing she had left of her mother, and understood, with a dread that settled into her bones like the frost on the maples, that the festival was the least of what she had lost this morning.
