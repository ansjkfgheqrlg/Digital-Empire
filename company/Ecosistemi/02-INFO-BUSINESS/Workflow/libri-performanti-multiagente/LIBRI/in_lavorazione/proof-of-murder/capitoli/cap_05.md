# The Viewing

The Grange hall smelled like carnations and coffee urns, the specific funeral smell that Maggie had forgotten existed until she stepped through the double doors and it hit her like a wall. She had not wanted to come. Josie had told her not to come, actually, standing in the bakery doorway with flour still on her forearms and her voice pitched low and urgent. People are ugly right now, Maggie. Give it a week.

But Rose Doyle had raised her daughter to believe that you showed up for the dead, no matter what the living thought of you, and so Maggie had put on the navy dress she kept for exactly this purpose and walked the four blocks from the bakery to the Grange with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and her eyes fixed straight ahead.

It had not stopped the stares.

She felt them now, sitting in a metal folding chair near the back wall, a paper cup of bad coffee going cold in her hands. Conversations dipped when she passed. A cluster of women from the garden club, women who had bought her mother's sourdough every Saturday for twenty years, had actually turned their backs when she walked by the guest book, a wall of cardigans and permed hair angled away from her like she carried something contagious.

Ruth Ann Sawyer was working the room in a black dress that was slightly too festive to be appropriate, moving from cluster to cluster with a tray of the little lemon bars she made for every town function, and every so often her eyes found Maggie across the hall and held there a beat too long before sliding away.

Maggie had almost decided to slip out early, to sign the book and leave before the receiving line even opened, when someone lowered herself into the folding chair beside her with a soft grunt of effort.

"They put the good chairs up front for family," Diane Pruitt said, "and I can't stand the sight of them anymore. Mind if I sit back here with the criminals?"

Maggie's whole body went rigid. She turned, expecting to find bitterness in the widow's face, some sharp edge of accusation, and found instead a tired, soft-featured woman in her sixties with red-rimmed eyes and a wool cardigan buttoned wrong by one hole.

"Mrs. Pruitt, I am so sorry," Maggie said. "I should not have said criminals, I only meant, this is a hard seat to be in. I know people are saying things about me and I understand if you don't want me anywhere near you today. I can move."

"Sit still, child." Diane patted Maggie's knee with a papery hand. "I've known you since you were knee high to your mother's counter, stealing gingersnaps when you thought nobody was looking. You didn't kill my husband. I don't care what that stained little recipe card says or doesn't say. Harold liked you. Said you had Rose's hands for pastry, whatever that meant."

Something in Maggie's chest loosened just slightly, a knot she had not realized she was carrying. "Thank you," she managed. "That means more than you know."

They sat in silence for a moment, watching the receiving line shuffle forward, watching Chief Torres murmur something to a man in a suit who might have been from the county, watching Denise Cobb glide through the room in a black suit so well tailored it looked purchased for the occasion, touching elbows, murmuring condolences, working the crowd like a woman running for something. Which, Maggie remembered, she was.

"Can I ask you something," Maggie said carefully, "and you can tell me it's none of my business."

Diane made a small sound that might have been a laugh. "Nothing is anybody's business in this town, and everybody knows everything anyway. Ask."

"Was Harold, in the last few weeks, was he acting differently? Chief Torres keeps circling around something and won't say what."

Diane was quiet long enough that Maggie thought she had overstepped, had pushed too hard on a grieving woman two days after finding her husband dead in a bakery kitchen. Then Diane set her coffee cup down on the seat beside her with a small clink and folded her hands in her lap.

"He'd been strange for a month, maybe longer," she said. "Slipping out at night. I'd wake up around eleven, eleven thirty, and his side of the bed would be cold and the truck would be gone from the drive. Three, four times a week toward the end."

"Did he say where he was going?"

"Committee business, he'd say. Founder's Day business, can't be helped, don't wait up." Diane's mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "Thirty one years married to that man and I know the difference between committee business and a man who's hiding something. He'd come back smelling like he'd been standing outside somewhere cold, and he wouldn't look at me straight for a day or two after. I thought." She stopped.

"You thought what?"

"I'll say it, since he's dead and it doesn't matter what I thought anymore. I thought there was another woman." Diane's eyes were dry now, hard in a way they hadn't been a moment before. "God forgive me, I lay in that bed some nights and I built the whole story in my head. Some divorcee from the county, somebody at the hardware store, I don't know. I was ready to hire a man to follow him, if you can believe that. Then he turns up dead in your bakery with your mother's handwriting in his pocket and a hole in his skull, and every rotten thought I had about him feels like a sin I'll be answering for."

Maggie felt the hair rise on her arms. "Mrs. Pruitt, do you have any idea who he might have been meeting? Even a guess."

"I've turned it over and over. I keep coming back to the committee itself. He'd been odd about the committee for months, snappish if I asked how the books were going, which he never used to be, Harold loved talking numbers, bored me half to death with it for thirty years and then all of a sudden he'd change the subject if I brought it up." Diane looked over toward the front of the hall, where Denise Cobb was now embracing a councilman's wife, her dark head bent close, murmuring. "And I'll tell you something else, and you can make of it what you like. Two nights before he died, I heard him on the phone in the garage at nearly midnight. Low voice, the kind you use when you don't want to be heard. I only caught the tail end of it, coming down for water. He said, I can't carry this anymore, Denise. I have to make it right before the festival or I'm not doing it at all."

The name landed in Maggie's stomach like a stone dropped in a well.

"Denise Cobb," Maggie repeated softly.

"That's what I heard. Make of it what you like. I've turned it over to Chief Torres, for whatever good that'll do. He wrote it down in his little notebook and thanked me and I haven't heard a word since." Diane's mouth tightened. "Bill Torres has had his hand out to Denise Cobb since before he had the badge, everybody in this town knows it, they just don't say it at the Grange hall on a Tuesday."

Maggie glanced across the room again. Denise had moved on from the councilman's wife and was now standing near the guest book, in low conversation with Walter Finch, the developer's silver head bent close to hers, both of them wearing the practiced solemnity of people at a funeral who were actually discussing something else entirely. As Maggie watched, Denise's eyes flicked up, found Maggie's face across the crowded hall, and held there, cool and assessing, before she smiled thinly and turned back to Walter.

"Did Chief Torres ask you anything else? About what Harold might have been carrying, or working on?"

Diane shook her head slowly. "He asked me if Harold kept a diary. He didn't. He asked if Harold had money troubles, which he didn't, we're comfortable, thank the Lord. He didn't ask me a single question about Denise Cobb, though I told him her name plain as day." She looked at Maggie then, and something in her tired eyes sharpened. "You're going to look into this yourself, aren't you. I can see it on you. You've got your mother's stubborn jaw, always did."

"I don't have much choice," Maggie said. "Everyone in town already thinks I did it. If I don't find out who actually killed him, I'm not sure anybody else is going to bother looking past me."

"Rose wouldn't have let it go either," Diane said. "She was the only person on that committee who ever asked hard questions about where the money went. Made some people awfully uncomfortable, your mother did, God rest her." She reached over and took Maggie's hand, her grip surprisingly firm for such a small, tired woman. "You find out what my Harold was carrying around at midnight that he couldn't tell his own wife. I have a feeling it's the same thing that got him killed, and I have a feeling it has something to do with why your mother is in the ground three years before her time."

The words settled over Maggie like a physical weight. Across the hall, the funeral director was moving toward the front, signaling that it was time to close the casket for the procession, and the room began to shift and rustle, people rising, murmuring, gathering coats. Diane squeezed Maggie's hand once more and pushed herself up from the folding chair with a small groan.

"Thank you for sitting with me," she said. "Nobody else would come back here."

"Thank you for telling me the truth," Maggie said. "I mean that."

Diane paused, looking down at her, and for just a moment the grief slipped and something colder and more determined showed through. "Somebody in this town knows exactly what my husband was carrying around, Maggie Doyle. I intend to find out who, before Founder's Day comes and goes and everyone decides it's easier to let it lie. I get the feeling you intend the same thing."

She moved off toward the front of the hall, small and stooped in her wool cardigan, leaving Maggie alone in the folding chair with her cold coffee and the name Denise Cobb turning over and over in her mind like a stone in a tumbler, catching new light with every rotation.
