# What the Autopsy Says

Nate texted her a single line just after nine that night. Back door in twenty. Don't tell Josie yet, and Maggie had read it twice before she understood that the second part wasn't about secrecy so much as sparing Josie a night of pacing.

She let him in through the alley door, the one that opened onto the narrow gravel strip behind the bakery where the dumpster sat and where, in daylight, delivery trucks idled while dropping off flour and sugar in fifty pound sacks. At night it was just shadow and the hum of the walk in cooler, and Nate stood in it with his hands in his jacket pockets and his shoulders up around his ears against the cold, out of uniform, in jeans and a flannel that made him look, for a moment, exactly like the boy who used to climb through her bedroom window at seventeen to avoid her mother's questions.

"You look like you haven't slept," Maggie said, stepping back to let him in.

"I haven't, much." He came into the kitchen, glancing automatically at the proofing drawer, still wrapped in crime scene tape, still standing open a crack from where the techs had left it after their last pass. His jaw tightened at the sight of it, and he looked away. "I can't stay long. Torres doesn't know I'm here and I'd like to keep it that way."

"Coffee?"

"If you're making it anyway."

She was not making it anyway, but she filled the kettle and set it on the burner because it gave her hands something to do, and because the small domestic motion of it steadied her nerves for whatever he'd driven over in the dark to tell her.

"The county coroner finished the full report this afternoon," Nate said, leaning against the steel prep table, arms crossed. "It came in through the state system, which means Torres has seen it, and probably half the department by now, and it'll be public within a day or two whether he likes it or not. I figured you should hear it from me instead of from Ruth Ann's version of it at the diner tomorrow."

"That bad."

"It's not what anybody wanted to hear, if that's what you mean." He rubbed a hand over his face. "Initial read at the scene was blunt force trauma, consistent with a fall, hitting his head on the corner of the steel table or the proofing unit on the way down. That was the working theory Torres wanted, because a fall means an accident, and an accident means the case closes quiet and Founder's Day goes on without a murder hanging over it."

"But."

"But the coroner says the wound pattern doesn't fit a fall. The fracture's got a specific shape to it, a defined edge, consistent with a single deliberate blow from an object with a flat, narrow striking surface. Something like a pipe, or the flat side of a heavy tool. Not a table corner, not a proofing drawer edge, nothing round or wide like that would leave. And the angle's wrong for a man just toppling over. The coroner's report calls it, and I'm quoting here because I made him say it twice, inconsistent with accidental injury and indicative of an applied force from a second party."

Maggie's stomach dropped even though she had known, on some level, since the moment she'd seen Harold slumped against her proofing drawer with dough spilled across his shoulder like a shroud, that it had never really been an accident. Hearing it confirmed in flat clinical language still landed like a blow of its own.

"So it's official," she said. "Homicide."

"As of about four o'clock this afternoon, yes. The state's already been notified, which means they could roll in and take the case from Torres any day now, maybe by the end of the week if they move fast." The kettle began to whistle, and Nate reached over without being asked and pulled it off the burner, filling two mugs while Maggie stood frozen with her arms wrapped around herself. "I'm telling you because you need to understand what that means for you specifically. Right now you're the person who found the body in your own kitchen, with your mother's handwriting in the dead man's pocket. That's a strong circumstantial hook for anybody who wants to close this fast, and Torres wants to close this fast."

"He's already decided it's me," Maggie said quietly. "I could feel it in that dining room."

"I'm not going to pretend he isn't leaning that way. I am going to tell you that leaning that way and proving it are two very different things, and I intend to make sure the difference matters." Nate handed her a mug, his fingers brushing hers for a beat longer than the transfer required, and neither of them commented on it. "There's something else. Something the state lab found on the recipe card that I don't think Torres has fully understood the weight of yet, or if he has, he's sitting on it."

"Tell me."

"The card, the molasses spice cookie card, in your mother's handwriting. It was folded in Harold's coat pocket, like Torres told you. What he didn't tell you, and what I only found out because I was the one who bagged and tagged the evidence at the scene and I read the lab tech's supplementary notes before Torres pulled them, is that there's a second layer of writing on it."

Maggie frowned. "A second layer. What does that mean?"

"You know how when you write on a pad of paper, and there's a blank sheet underneath the one you're writing on, sometimes the pressure of the pen leaves a faint impression on the sheet below, even though nothing's actually written on it? Ghost writing, they call it, or indented writing. Forensic document techs can pull that up with the right light and technique, it's a pretty standard trick, they use electrostatic detection devices for the good stuff, but sometimes you can even coax it out with something as simple as raking light or graphite."

"Someone wrote something on top of the card," Maggie said slowly, the pieces assembling themselves in her mind even as she said it out loud. "Recently. Not my mother, three years ago. Someone put a fresh piece of paper on top of that recipe card and wrote on it, hard enough that the impression pressed through onto my mother's card underneath."

"That's exactly what the tech found. A pressed impression, recent, distinct from the original ink and pencil marks that were already on the card. The lab flagged it because the indentation pattern doesn't match anything Rose wrote. Different pressure, different stroke width, consistent with a different hand entirely, and consistent with paper contact within the last few weeks, not three years old like the rest of the card's markings."

Maggie set her mug down on the prep table, her hands not quite steady. "So somebody, recently, laid a piece of paper on top of my mother's recipe card and wrote something down. Which means somebody had that card, out of my recipe box, recently enough to use it as a writing surface. Which means it didn't just go missing the night Harold died."

"That's the read I'm getting too," Nate said. "It could have been out of your box for weeks. Longer. Somebody could have lifted it any time you had customers in the shop, any time Josie or you stepped into the back, any time the box was sitting out on the counter for one of your mother's old regulars to reminisce over. This isn't a crime of opportunity that happened at four in the morning in your kititchen. Somebody's been planning around that card for a while."

"Do they know what the impression says? Can they read it?"

"Not yet, not with certainty. The lab tech's notes said the impression was faint, partial, they could make out fragments but not a clean full sentence. He flagged a couple of words he was fairly confident about and marked the rest as illegible without further enhancement." Nate hesitated, glancing toward the taped off proofing drawer again, then back at her. "One of the words he was confident about was Grange. And there was a fragment that looked like it might read don't add up, though he wasn't certain of the exact phrasing around it."

Maggie went very still. "The Grange numbers don't add up."

"Could be. I'm not going to swear to exact wording off a partial forensic impression, that's not how this works and you know I could lose my badge just telling you this much before it's been formally disclosed. But that's the shape of what's there. Somebody, recently, wrote something about the Grange and about numbers not adding up, on a piece of paper resting directly on top of a recipe card that already had your mother's hidden financial notations penciled into the margins."

The kitchen felt suddenly very cold, colder than the autumn night leaking in around the alley door. Maggie thought of her mother's recipe box, the dates and figures hidden in pencil in the margins of a dozen cards, all from the month before she died. She thought of Harold slipping out at night for weeks, telling Diane it was committee business and refusing to explain further. She thought of the words Diane had overheard him say on the phone two nights before he died. I can't carry this anymore, Denise. I have to make it right before the festival.

"Harold had the card," Maggie said slowly. "He had it recently enough to write on top of it. Which means he was looking at whatever my mother encoded in it, maybe trying to understand it himself, or maybe using it to write out his own version of it somewhere else. A confession, maybe. Notes for a confession."

"That's where my head's gone too," Nate admitted. "I can't prove it yet. But it fits. Rose was tracking money in those cards before she died. Harold gets his hands on at least one of them, recently, and starts writing something down about the Grange numbers not adding up, something urgent enough that he's sneaking out at night for weeks working through it. And then somebody kills him and leaves the same card in his pocket like it doesn't matter, or like they want it found, or like they didn't even know what was hidden on it."

"Or like they wanted people to think of me," Maggie said. "Since it's in my mother's handwriting."

"That thought's occurred to me too." Nate's voice was grim. "I want you to understand something, Maggie, and I need you to actually hear it and not just nod along. I already bent a rule I shouldn't have bent, sitting on a piece of this for a day so you'd have time to look at those cards with Josie before Torres or the state came sniffing around your kitchen table. I did that because I believe in you and because I think whatever your mother found three years ago is the reason both she and Harold ended up dead, and I am not going to let this town decide you're guilty just because it's convenient. But I can't keep doing that indefinitely. The state's coming. When they get here, everything gets colder and more by the book, and I won't have room to give you a head start anymore."

"How much time do you think I have?"

"Days. Not weeks." He drained the last of his coffee and set the mug down, and for a moment his hand found hers on the prep table, warm despite the chill of the kitchen, familiar in a way that made her throat tighten unexpectedly. "Find out what your mother knew before Founder's Day gets here, Maggie. I think whoever killed Harold is scared of exactly that happening, and scared people make mistakes. But scared people also finish what they started, and I don't like how fast this is moving."

He left the way he'd come, back through the alley door into the dark, and Maggie stood alone in her kitchen for a long time afterward, staring at the taped off proofing drawer, thinking about ghost writing pressed into paper, about numbers that didn't add up, about a green ledger book snapping shut the instant her eyes had drifted toward it.
