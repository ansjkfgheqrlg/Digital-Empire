# The Fourth of November

She worked all Sunday.

Not upstairs. Downstairs, on the ground floor, in the ordinary stock, because a thought had arrived somewhere around six on Saturday evening and would not leave, and the thought was this: *he wrote in the ones he would not sell, so what did he do with the ones he would?*

Clara started at the door and worked clockwise, and she did it properly. She took each book off, opened the front endpaper, checked for the pencilled price, riffled the block with her thumb held loose so that the pages fanned, watched for the flicker of grey in a margin, and put it back. She could do about four hundred an hour, and she did eleven hundred before lunch and two thousand after it, and she found nothing at all.

Not one note in three thousand one hundred books.

By seven she had got as far as local history, which sat in the corner nearest the back curtain, five runs of it, the heaviest section in the shop. She was tired and her eyes were going and she nearly left it.

The third book in the second run had a note.

It was a soft covered thing, *The Parish Churches of North Dorset*, published in Blandford in 1971, priced at two pounds fifty in the small square hand, and on page fifty three, in the margin beside a paragraph about the churchyard yews of the Fenn valley, there was pencil.

Clara stood in the aisle and read it and put her free hand out to the shelf.

*4.xi.96, 23.10, G.A.*

She did not move for a while.

Then she carried the book to the counter, set it down under the lamp, photographed the page, and looked at it for another minute, and then she made herself do it correctly and went back and did the whole of local history, all five runs, six hundred and forty books, in two hours and ten minutes, and found three more.

Page 88 of a book about drove roads: *4.xi.96, 22.50, R.F. leaves shop.*

Page 12 of a hardback on the Dorset county constabulary: *4.xi.96, 23.32, G.A. returns, alone.*

And on the pastedown at the back of a booklet about lost wells, in pencil so faint that she found it by touch before she found it by eye:

*I did not write any of this down at the time. I have written it since from what I remember and I have not changed it after. E.P.*

Clara sat down on the floor of her own bookshop between two shelf runs with four books in her lap and put her head back against the wood.

They were not notes. They were a log.

Somebody had come into this shop, and somebody had left it, and somebody had come back, and Edwin Pryor had written the times, and then he had scattered the times through a section of local topography that nobody in this town would ever buy.

She got up. She went to the counter and opened her laptop and typed *Fenn Abbas 1996 churchyard* into a search box, and the connection in the shop was four megabits and it took eleven seconds, and Clara Vance watched a page load that changed the shape of everything.

It was the archive of the *Blackmore Vale Echo*, scanned, poorly, in 2011. Page five. Wednesday 6 November 1996.

**TOWN SHOCKED BY DEATH OF SHOP ASSISTANT, 22**

*The body of a young woman was found early on Tuesday morning in the churchyard of St Botolph's, Fenn Abbas. She has been named as Miss Ruth Feltham, 22, of Sheepwash Row, who worked at a bookshop in the town.*

*Miss Feltham was discovered shortly after 7am by a parishioner. Police said there were no suspicious circumstances and that a file would be prepared for the coroner. It is understood she had suffered a head injury consistent with a fall.*

*Miss Feltham's employer, Mr E. Pryor of The Midnight Ledger, described her as "quick, and kind, and a great deal cleverer than this town deserved". Her father, Mr Wallace Feltham, was too distressed to comment.*

*The vicar of St Botolph's, the Reverend A. Hallett, said the churchyard would remain open.*

Clara read it three times, and the third time she read the last line of the report, which was set in smaller type at the foot of the column and which she had skimmed twice because it read like filler.

*The Fenn Abbas Book Fair, which was due to take place on Saturday, has been postponed until the spring. The chairman of the organising committee, Mr Gerald Amory, said it was "not a week for a celebration".*

She sat with her hands off the keys.

Then she went back to the archive and searched forward, because a death that goes to a coroner comes back to a newspaper, and it did, on the twenty second of January 1997, on page eleven, in eight lines.

**INQUEST: FENN ABBAS DEATH WAS ACCIDENT**

*The Dorset coroner recorded a verdict of accidental death at the inquest into Miss Ruth Feltham, 22, of Fenn Abbas, who was found in St Botolph's churchyard in November. The court heard that Miss Feltham had suffered a head injury consistent with a fall against a stone kerb. No witnesses were called. The hearing lasted eleven minutes.*

Clara read *no witnesses were called* several times.

She had been in a coroner's court once, professionally, over a water damaged parish record, and she remembered the shape of the day: the usher, the list on the door, the family in the second row. Eleven minutes was not a hearing. Eleven minutes was a filing exercise with a person's name on it.

She looked for a follow up in the weeks after and there was none. Fenn Abbas appeared in the *Echo* four more times before the spring, in connection with a burst main, a planning objection, a fun run, and the rescheduled book fair, which had gone ahead in April and had raised nine hundred and forty pounds for the church roof.

G.A.

She made herself go slowly. She was a person who spent her life being told that a document said something, and finding that it did not; the whole of her professional value was that she did not run ahead of the page. So she wrote it out on paper, in pencil, in her own untidy hand.

**Established:** Edwin Pryor wrote times against initials for the night of 4 November 1996. He wrote them years afterwards, from memory, and said so.

**Established:** Ruth Feltham, 22, his employee, was found dead in the churchyard on the morning of the 5th. Verdict of accident, January 1997.

**Established:** Hettie Boon saw two people inside the churchyard gate at about 11.15pm on the 4th, and the gate lamp, which is on this building's supply and was lit every night for thirty one years, was off.

**Established:** Gerald Amory has initials G.A., was in Fenn Abbas in 1996, was chairman of the book fair, and on Friday told me he could not remember the year in which Edwin stopped selling from the upstairs room, unprompted, twice.

**Not established:** that G.A. means Gerald Amory. Fenn Abbas has four hundred people and Dorset has a lot of Georges. Also *A* could be a surname or a Christian name and I do not know which way round Edwin wrote.

**Not established:** anything at all about how Ruth Feltham died.

She underlined the last two lines twice, and then she wrote a sixth thing and looked at it for a long time.

**Question:** why did a man who wrote down the movements of a person on the night his employee died not go to the police?

The shop was very quiet. It was twenty past ten.

Clara went and stood at the front window with the lamp off behind her, and looked out at the churchyard gate, and the lantern on the corner of her building was burning as it had burned all day and as it had, according to Hettie Boon, burned every night for thirty one years except one.

*I said I would leave the side light on.*

She had that note in a spreadsheet upstairs with ten others and she had not understood a word of it on Friday.

"He turned it off," she said, to the window. "Somebody asked him to and he turned it off, and then she was in the dark."

And behind her, upstairs, above the shop floor, a page turned.

Clara did not jump. She found, standing at the glass with her arms folded, that something in her had gone past jumping some time on Saturday afternoon and had arrived somewhere colder and much steadier.

"I know," she said.

The turning went on, unhurried.

"I'm not going to be quick," Clara said. "There are six hundred books up there and I don't know your order yet and if I get it wrong I'll make a story instead of a case, and a story is no good to her. So you can keep at it all night if you like, but I'm going to do it properly, and it's going to take weeks."

The turning stopped.

Then, distinctly, from directly above her head, a book fell flat on the floorboards.

Clara went up.

The room was as she had left it, blinds level, shelves full. In the middle of the floor, alone, lay a book she had not touched: a road atlas of Great Britain, 1994 edition, open at the double page spread of the West Country, and lying across the fold, holding the pages down, was a second bus ticket.

WILTS AND DORSET. DORCHESTER TO FENN ABBAS. SINGLE. 04 NOV 96.

The outward ticket had been in the arc on Friday night. This was the return.

And on the map beneath it, in the small square hand, running along the line of the A road between the two towns, five words in pencil:

*he was not in Dorchester*
