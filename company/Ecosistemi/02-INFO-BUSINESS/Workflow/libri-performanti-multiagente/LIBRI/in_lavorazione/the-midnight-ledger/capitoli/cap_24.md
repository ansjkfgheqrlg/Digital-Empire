# The Midnight Ledger

The Midnight Ledger reopened on the first Saturday in March, at half past nine in the morning, with a new back door.

It had taken eleven weeks and rather more of the insurance money than Clara had expected, and a builder from Hurstock called Barry who had opinions about the 1740 floor joists and turned out to be right about all of them. The back room was a back room again: plastered, painted, with a stove that drew properly for the first time in forty years and a small window that had not existed before, because Barry had found the frame of one bricked up in 1911 and had put it back without being asked.

She did not put a bed in it.

She had taken a lease on the flat over Priya's in January, two doors down, four hundred and twenty pounds a month, with a bedroom that looked out over the back gardens and not at anything at all.

"You'll sleep," Priya had said, handing over the key. "That's the entire point of it. You'll sleep and you'll walk to work."

The shop had changed in three ways.

The first was that the upstairs front room was open, and everything in it was for sale.

That had taken a fortnight of argument with herself and one afternoon with Okafor, who had walked round the room with a clipboard and released four hundred and thirty one volumes from the schedule of exhibits, retaining a hundred and eighty as the ones that carried notes going to the trial. Those had gone to Winfrith in twelve crates and would come back, or would not, in about two years.

The rest Clara priced properly, at what they were worth, which was between one pound and four pounds fifty, and put on the ground floor with a card that said SECOND HAND, and they sold, because everything sells if it is priced honestly and somebody is standing behind it.

The oak shelves upstairs she kept. She had the blinds taken off and three windows cleaned for the first time in three decades, and the light came into that room in March like something being let out, and she put a table in it and two chairs and a kettle, and it became the place where she worked.

The second change was the sign in the window, which said BOOK REPAIR AND CONSERVATION, and which had brought in, by the first week of April, one seventeenth century parish chest from a church at Melbury that needed four months of work, two family bibles, a fire damaged photograph album from a woman in Yeovil, and an entire county archive's worth of nineteenth century tithe maps on a rolling contract that would keep her alive until 2028.

It turned out that there was not enough of her work in Bristol because she had been looking for it in Bristol. There was a great deal of it in Dorset, and it came to a crooked house on a lane because a crooked house on a lane is exactly where people expect that sort of thing to be.

The third change was the ledger.

She had bought it in January, in Sherborne, from a stationer who had four of them left in a drawer from a firm that stopped making them in 1994: three hundred leaves, printed feint, dark green cloth over boards, a red leather label. She had ruled a fourth column down the right hand side of every page by hand with a straight edge, because it seemed to her that a person should not inherit half a system.

She left that column empty. She intended to leave it empty for the rest of her life.

On the first page, on the first Saturday in March, she wrote the date, and under it:

*1 x large print romance, returned, no charge, H. Boon*

*4 x paperback crime, 6.00, Mrs Lacey*

*1 x county hist., 2 vols, 18.00, the man from Yeovil, who came back*

Priya brought coffee at eleven and stayed forty minutes, and by twelve o'clock there had been nine people in the shop, which was more than had been in it in any single day since 2009, and six of them were there to look at the woman from the papers and three of them bought something anyway.

Hettie Boon came at ten past nine, first, before the door was properly open, with her shopping trolley.

"I'll have my Friday books back," she said. "You've had them since November and I've been reading the paper instead and it's rubbish."

Clara had five put by for her under the counter, with a slip of paper on top, and she had written on it *hold for H. Boon, Friday*, and then had looked at it for a moment and had not changed it.

Hettie read the slip and put it in her handbag and did up the clasp, the way she had in October, and neither of them said anything about it.

"There's a thing I'll say once," Hettie said, at the door, with the trolley. "I told a policeman in 1996 and he wrote it in a book and it went nowhere, and I have had thirty years of thinking I should have shouted louder. And I've worked out this winter that that's the wrong way round, and it took a woman coming here from Bristol with a holdall to show me it. I said it. I said it four times. It was written down and it was thrown away, and that's a thing that was done to me, not a thing I failed to do."

"That's right," said Clara.

"Well," said Hettie Boon. "I'd have got there sooner if somebody had said so at the time," and she went off down the lane with her trolley bumping on the cobbles.

At the end of the day Clara went up to the front room and stood at the middle window while the light went.

The churchyard was doing what it did in March, which was mud and snowdrops and rooks. The gate lamp on the corner of the building was on. It had been on continuously since the fourth of November 1996, an electrician from Sherborne had eventually established, because at some point in the winter of that year somebody had bridged the switch inside the front door with a two inch length of wire, so that the lantern was wired directly to the supply and could not be turned off by anybody, ever, including by Edwin Pryor himself.

Clara had asked the electrician to leave it exactly as it was.

Forty five yards up the path, past the yew, the stone was there in the last of the light. It had been reset and the ground made good in January, and Wallace had paid for the reseeding, and in the spring the grass would come and in three years nobody would be able to tell.

Somebody had put flowers in the jam jar. Clara had not, and Wallace was in Weymouth for the week with his sister, and it might have been anyone in that town or none of them.

She stood there for a while.

Then she said, out loud, in an empty room above a shop, "It's done. It won't be tidy and it won't be enough and it's done, and I'll come up on Sunday and tell you the rest."

Nothing turned a page.

Nothing had, since the ninth of December, and she had stopped expecting it, and she found that what she felt about that was not disappointment but something closer to the way you feel when a friend gets home safely and does not ring.

She went down and cashed up and turned the shop lights off one by one from the back, the way you do.

At the front door she stopped, out of habit, with her hand near the brown Bakelite switch under the coat pegs, and did not touch it, because it did nothing and would never do anything again.

There were two coats on the pegs. One was hers.

The other was a woman's wool coat, grey, size ten, with a bus ticket and eleven pence in the left hand pocket, which had hung on the second peg of a bookshop in Church Lane for twenty nine years and four months, and which the Crown Prosecution Service had photographed and returned in February, and which Clara Vance had brought back and hung up in exactly the place it had always hung, and intended to leave there.

She put her own coat on over her jumper, and locked the door, and went down the lane past Priya's lit window towards the square, under a lamp that could not be switched off.

Behind her, on the counter, in the dark, the new ledger lay open at the first page with the day's takings in it, and one blank column, ruled by hand, going down the right hand side, waiting for nothing at all.
