# Eleven Weeks

The Reverend Tom Sayers came to the shop on a wet Wednesday at the beginning of December, without being asked, and stood in the doorway shaking water off his hat.

"I've been avoiding you for six weeks," he said. "I'd like to stop."

Clara put the kettle on.

He sat in the chair she had cleared for Okafor and held his mug with both hands and did not drink from it, and it took him a while to start.

"When you asked me in the churchyard whether the parish had a record," he said, "I told you the parish has a record of everything, and then I stopped, and you saw me stop, and I have thought about that every day since. I want to be honest about the order things happened in, because the order is the whole of it and it does not do me any credit.

"I did not know anything on that Saturday. I stopped because I remembered something and I did not want to say it out loud until I had checked it. That is the truth, and it is also exactly what a man says who has been sitting on something, so you will have to decide."

"Go on."

"I have been vicar here five years. Two years ago, in the January, I did an inventory of the parish chest for the archdeacon, because they make you, and it took me four Saturdays, and it is nine hundred years of paper in a box with no catalogue." He turned the mug in his hands. "The service register for 1996 is missing eleven weeks. The pages are not lost. They have been cut out with a blade against a straight edge, close to the gutter, from the seventh of September to the twenty fourth of November, and it is a very good job, and you would not see it unless you had the book flat at the right place and were counting."

Clara said, carefully, "What is a service register?"

"The least interesting book in England," said Tom Sayers. "A printed volume with a line for every service held. Date, service, time, officiant, number of communicants, amount of the collection, signature. It exists so the diocese knows the church is being used and so the treasurer can be checked."

"Who signs it?"

"The officiant signs for the service. But there is a column at the far right, and in this parish, by custom going back to the 1930s, it is initialled by whoever unlocked and locked the building that day." He looked up. "Which for the whole of that period was the sacristan."

Clara sat down on the arm of the other chair.

"Gerald Amory."

"Gerald Amory," said the vicar, "who is a churchwarden now, and was sacristan then, and who has raised eleven thousand pounds for that roof in four years, and who took my predecessor's funeral arrangements off my hands in my first month here when I did not know where anything was, and who I like." His mouth was tight. "I want that on the record too, because I have spent two years constructing reasons not to look at this, and every one of them was true."

"What did you do when you found it?"

"Nothing, for four months. Then in the May I asked him." Sayers put the mug down. "Not accusingly. I said, Gerald, there's a chunk out of the ninety six service register, do you know anything about it, was there a leak, did somebody take pages for a family history. And he said no, and he was surprised, and he was concerned, and he suggested it might have been the archdeacon's people in the eighties when they microfilmed things, and he offered to go through the chest with me at the weekend."

"Did he?"

"He came on the Saturday and we spent five hours on it and he was excellent," said the vicar. "He knew where everything was. He found me two faculties I had been looking for since March."

"And you thought that settled it."

"I thought there was nothing to settle. Miss Vance, until the middle of October I had never in my life had a reason to connect a gap in a service register with anything at all. Registers get damaged. It is the most boring possible fact." He rubbed his face. "And then a woman I had met once in my own churchyard started going through Edwin Pryor's shop, and a detective sergeant came to see me about a death in 1996, and I went home and sat in the kitchen and did the arithmetic on the dates, and I have been ill about it ever since."

"What are the dates?"

"Seventh of September to the twenty fourth of November," said Tom Sayers. "Ruth Feltham died on the fourth of November. Whoever took those pages did not take the week she died. They took eleven weeks, with her in the middle of it."

The rain came harder against the front windows.

"Why would you do that," Clara said slowly, "if you only cared about one night."

"That is the thing I could not get past in the kitchen." He leaned forward. "If you cut out one week, then anybody who ever opens that book knows exactly which week mattered. You have drawn an arrow. If you cut out a term, it looks like damage."

"It looks like damage," Clara repeated.

"And it worked," said the vicar, "because it looked like damage to me for two years, and I am not a stupid man, and it looked like damage to a churchwarden who spent five hours in a vestry helping me look for an explanation."

Clara went behind the counter and got a pad.

"What was in those eleven weeks?" she said. "Not that night. Generally. What does a service register have in it between September and November?"

"Harvest. All Souls, which is the second, and in this parish it is very well attended, because it is the one where the names are read. Remembrance Sunday, which in 1996 was the tenth. Two weddings, probably, and I know it is two because the fees book says so, and nobody touched the fees book."

"What else?"

"Every Sunday. Every Wednesday said service. Any weekday funeral." He spread his hands. "And the initials of the man who unlocked and locked, every single time, for eleven weeks."

Clara stopped writing.

"That's it," she said.

"Sorry?"

"He didn't take the pages to hide one night. He took them to destroy the pattern." She was writing fast now. "Think about what that column is. It is eleven weeks of one man's movements, in his own hand, dated, timed by the service, in a book kept by somebody else and signed off by a treasurer. It is the single best record of Gerald Amory's whereabouts in the autumn of 1996 that exists anywhere in the world, and he was the only person with a key to it."

"But if he was in Dorchester on the fourth, the register helps him."

"It helps him for the fourth," said Clara. "What about the third? The twenty seventh of October? Every Wednesday evening at half past six in a locked building, for eleven weeks?" She looked up. "Ruth Feltham was seeing a man for at least eight months and never once brought him to the pub, or her father's house, or anywhere in a town of four hundred people where somebody would see them. Where do you meet somebody in a place like this, where nobody will ever walk in on you?"

Tom Sayers had gone very white.

"Somewhere you have the only key to," he said.

Neither of them spoke for a while. A car went down the lane too fast, the way they always did.

"I have to tell the police," he said.

"You do."

"I have to tell the archdeacon and the diocesan registrar, and there is a process for a damaged register, and the moment I start it, it is in writing in three offices in Salisbury." He stood up. "And I will have to tell the parochial church council, because he is a churchwarden and I am not permitted to conduct an inquiry into a churchwarden on my own, and there are nine people on that council and four of them have known Gerald Amory since they were children."

"How long before somebody tells him?"

"In this town?" Tom Sayers put his hat on. "About a day."

He got to the door and stopped, and turned round, and for the first time since he had come in he looked younger than he was.

"Miss Vance. The inscription on that stone. *The light is on.*"

"Yes."

"I have been through the faculty papers this week, because I could not sleep and it was something to do." He took a folded photocopy out of his coat and put it on the counter. "The memorial was applied for in March 1997 by Mr Wallace Feltham. But the wording was submitted separately, in a letter, in the June, and the letter is in the file, and the chancellor's clerk queried it as unusual and asked for a source, and the applicant replied that it was a private matter between the deceased and the family, and the clerk let it go, because they usually do."

Clara turned the photocopy round.

It was four lines of typing above a signature, and the signature was a small square hand she could have picked out of a thousand.

*E. Pryor.*

"He paid for the stone," said the vicar. "Mr Feltham never knew. And what he asked to have cut on it, at his own expense, in June 1997, is a thing that only means anything if you know what he promised her on the night she died."

Clara looked at the four typed lines for a long time.

"He wrote her an apology," she said, "and he put it on a rock, and he stood in front of it for twenty nine years, and nobody in this town could read it."
