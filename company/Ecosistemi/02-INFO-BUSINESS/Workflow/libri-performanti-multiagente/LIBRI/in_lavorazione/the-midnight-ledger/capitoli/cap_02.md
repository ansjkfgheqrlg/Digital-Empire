# The Room Upstairs

Clara went up.

She would think about that later, and be surprised at herself, and conclude that she had gone up for the same reason she had come to Fenn Abbas at all, which was that the alternative was standing still.

The staircase was boxed in with panelling on both sides and it was steep, and there were books on nine of the treads, stacked against the wall side so that a person could still climb if that person kept to the right and did not mind turning sideways. She climbed with one hand flat on the panelling. Halfway up, the sound stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, between one page and the next, the way a person stops reading when they hear a stair creak.

At the top there was a small landing with a door on either side and a light cord hanging in the middle. Clara pulled it.

The door on the left was open onto a bathroom with a cracked basin. The door on the right was shut.

She opened it.

The room ran the full width of the building, front to back, and it had three windows on the churchyard side, and it was full of books, and Clara stood in the doorway with her hand still on the handle and understood within about four seconds that this was not more stock.

She could not have explained the difference to anyone who did not do her job. It was in the shelving, first: downstairs the shelves were the standard three quarter inch pine of every second hand shop in England, and up here they were oak, built in, fitted to the crooked walls by somebody who had scribed each upright to the plaster instead of packing the gap with card. It was in the spacing: the shelves downstairs were jammed, and up here every book stood upright with its neighbours and there was a finger of air at the end of each run. It was in the light: the windows had been fitted with roller blinds, all three drawn down to exactly the same height, which is what you do when you care about what daylight does to paper over thirty years.

And it was in the books themselves, which were not valuable. That was the thing that stopped her.

She went along the first run with her head tilted. Book club editions. Reprints. A run of Penguin crime from the seventies with sunned spines. A four volume county history that any dealer would price at fifteen pounds the set on a good day. Two shelves of Reader's Digest condensed novels, which are worth precisely nothing and which no bookseller alive keeps.

Nothing on that wall was worth more than a few pounds, and all of it had been shelved and blinded and cared for like a rare collection.

Clara took down the fourth book at random, a battered green hardback about canal boats, and opened the front endpaper out of habit.

No price.

She checked the next. No price. The next, the next, the one after that. She went along a whole run of thirty books opening front endpapers and not one of them had the small pencilled figure in the top corner that every book downstairs carried.

"You never put them out," she said.

Then she opened the canal boat book properly, and turned pages, and found the pencil on page eleven.

It was in the margin, level with the fourth line, in the small square hand. It said:

*she did not have a coat with her*

Clara read it, and turned the page, and found nothing else in the whole book. She checked the text on page eleven, twice, in case the note was a reader's quarrel with the author. Page eleven of the canal boat book was about lock gates. Nobody in it had a coat.

She put the book back and took another, at random, from a different shelf: a paperback about English churches. Nothing for sixty pages, and then, in the margin beside a photograph of a rood screen:

*13 minutes is not enough time*

She stood in a cold room above a shop she had inherited from a stranger, holding a paperback, and felt the hair go up on her forearms.

Downstairs the shop bell rang.

Clara came down the stairs fast enough to be embarrassed about it and found a woman standing just inside the door with a tray in her hands, looking around the shop with the frank open curiosity of somebody who has wanted to get in for months.

"You're the niece," the woman said.

"I'm not anybody's niece."

"You're somebody's something, because Edwin Pryor didn't leave that shop to a stranger." The woman put the tray down on the counter, moved the dried cup aside without any ceremony at all, and put out her hand. "Priya Raval. I've got the coffee place two doors down, and I've brought you a flat white and a cheese toastie, and before you say anything, it's not kindness, it's business. Church Lane has two premises on it and one of them has been shut since March and my takings are down eleven per cent. I want you open."

"I'm not opening. I'm selling."

"Right," said Priya, entirely unbothered, and sat down on the arm of one of the book covered chairs. "Everybody says that."

Clara took the coffee because she had not eaten since Westbury. It was extremely good coffee, which in a town this size she had not expected, and she said so, and Priya said that was the eleven per cent talking and then went quiet for a moment, looking at the shelves.

"He'd have liked this," she said. "Somebody standing in here drinking a coffee. Nobody stood in here for years."

"Did he not have customers?"

"He had Hettie Boon on a Friday and a man from Yeovil twice a year and that was about the shape of it." Priya turned the cup around in her hands. "That's not fair. He had customers in the nineties. My mum used to come in. He was busy, back then, and then he wasn't, and it was slow enough that you couldn't say when it happened. And then Amory did the coffee and the events and the window and that was that."

"The green shop on the square."

"Gerald Amory. He'll be in to see you," Priya said. "Not today. He'll leave it a day so it doesn't look like he's been waiting."

Clara set the coffee down. "Can I ask you something. I've been upstairs."

Priya looked up, and something in her face changed. It was not fear, exactly. It was the look of somebody being asked about a local peculiarity by an outsider and deciding, in real time, how much to say.

"The front room," she said. "Blinds down."

"You've seen it."

"Once. He had a chest infection three winters ago and I brought his shopping up and he was in there and he came out onto the landing so fast to stop me at the door that he had to hold the wall." Priya paused. "He wasn't nasty about it. He just said, not that room, love, and then he stood there until I went down."

"What's in it?"

"Books."

"There are books everywhere. Those ones aren't for sale."

"No," Priya said. "They're not."

"Why?"

"That's the thing." She set her cup down on the tray with a small click. "You know how a place gets a thing that everybody knows and nobody's ever actually asked about? That's the upstairs room at the Ledger. Ask anybody in this town. Edwin Pryor would sell you anything on the ground floor and he would not sell you a single book from the first floor front, not for any money, not to anybody, not once, in thirty one years. People tried. There was a dealer came down from Bath in about 2009 and offered him for the lot, sight unseen, four thousand pounds cash, and Edwin walked him to the door."

"Four thousand." Clara thought of the Reader's Digest condensed novels. "Priya, there is nothing up there worth four thousand pounds. There's nothing up there worth four hundred. It's reprints and book club and a lot of paperbacks with faded spines. I've valued collections for a living for eleven years. If you took every book out of that room and sold it properly you'd clear maybe eight hundred quid and it would take you two months."

Priya looked at her for a long moment.

"Well," she said. "That's worse, isn't it."

They sat with that.

"He changed," Priya said eventually. "That's what my mum says, and Hettie says it too, and Hettie's eighty eight and remembers everything. He was one thing and then he was another thing and it happened all at once, and after that he kept the shop and he was polite to everybody and he never once went to the pub again and he built shelves upstairs for books that aren't worth anything."

"When?"

"Ninety six, my mum reckons. The winter."

The word went through Clara like cold water down a wrist, and she could not have said why, and she was careful not to let it show, and she was not sure she managed.

Priya stood up and collected the tray. At the door she stopped.

"I'm not going to say anything daft to you," she said. "I've lived here thirty years and I don't believe in anything. But I'll tell you what I told the environmental health woman in March, which is that when I came in here that morning with Edwin lying in the back room, every window in this shop was shut and every book in the upstairs front room was on the floor."

Clara did not move.

"All of them?"

"No," said Priya. "That's the bit I don't like. Eleven of them."

The bell rang as the door shut behind her.

Clara stood in the middle of her aisle for a while, and then she went back up the stairs with her coffee going cold in her hand, and she stood in the doorway of the upstairs front room and looked at eight hundred pounds' worth of worthless books, shelved like treasure, blinded against the sun, every one of them without a price and, she was now fairly sure, every one of them with something written in it.

"All right," she said to the room. "What are you?"

The room did not answer. Outside the middle window, forty feet away across the cobbles, the nearest headstone in St Botolph's churchyard stood at exactly the height of the sill and faced the glass, the way it had faced the glass for two hundred and eighty years, and Clara Vance, who was not going to be here more than a week, went down to make up the bed in the back room because there was no hotel in Fenn Abbas and the last train had gone at six.
