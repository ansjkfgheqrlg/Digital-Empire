# What Gerald Amory Knew

The vicar was wrong about one thing. It took two days, not one.

Gerald Amory came on the Friday evening at ten past six, in the dark, when the lane was empty and Priya had been shut for half an hour, and he did not knock. Clara had put a proper lock on the door in November, as instructed, and she had not turned it, and the bell rang and there he was with rain on his shoulders and his hat in his hand.

"May I come in?"

"You're in."

"I am." He shut the door behind him and stood on the mat. "I'll go if you tell me to. I'd like you to know that before anything else, because I am aware of what this looks like."

Clara had thought about this for six weeks and had rehearsed four different versions of herself, and every one of them went out of her head at once, and what she said was, "Sit down, then."

He sat.

He looked older than he had in October. Not tired: reduced, the way a man is reduced when he has stopped taking up the amount of room he is used to taking up.

"Tom Sayers has gone to the police," he said. "And to the archdeacon, and to the council, and Marion Lacey rang me on Wednesday night to tell me, which she should not have done and which I am not going to pretend I did not appreciate." He turned the hat over. "So I know what is being said. I do not know what you have got, and I have not come to find out, and if you tell me anything I will assume it is because you want to and not because I have been clever."

"Why have you come?"

"Because on Monday I am going to be interviewed under caution at Bournemouth," said Gerald Amory, "and after that I will not be permitted to say a word to you for possibly two years, and there is one thing I would like you to have heard from me and not from a solicitor reading a prepared statement."

Clara stood behind the counter, which put four feet of oak between them, and was aware that she had put it there.

"Go on."

"I knew Ruth Feltham," he said.

He said it plainly and then stopped, and Clara understood that he had come a long way to get that sentence out and that it had cost him something on the way.

"I knew her from the January of 1996 until the November. Not to say hello to, which is what I told a police constable in a written statement in November 1996, and which was a lie, and I have known it was a lie every day since, and there is no version of the next two years in which that does not destroy me." He looked at his hands. "I was thirty three. She was twenty two. I was engaged to be married in the April to a woman whose father owned four shops. My own father was alive and he was a difficult man and the business was his."

"You were seeing her."

"Yes."

"For eight months."

"Ten. From about the second week of January." He did not look up. "In the vestry, mostly, because I had the key, which is the single most shameful sentence I have ever said out loud and it is going to be read to a jury."

Clara found that her hands had gone cold.

"And when she died?"

"I said nothing," said Amory. "I have said nothing for thirty years. I was in this shop that night and I have said nothing, and I let a coroner call it an accident, and I went to the funeral and I carried nothing, and I stood at the back."

The clock over the counter ticked.

"You were in this shop that night," Clara said.

"I was in this shop that night."

"Say it properly. Where were you at eleven o'clock on the fourth of November 1996?"

Gerald Amory looked up at her, and Clara saw, for the first time since he had walked in, something that was not sorrow.

"That," he said, "is the question I am going to be asked on Monday under caution with a solicitor beside me, and it is the only question in the world that matters, and I am not going to answer it in a bookshop to a woman who has a police officer's telephone number. Not because of what the answer is. Because of what I would be doing to it."

"That's convenient."

"It is completely convenient," he agreed, "and it is also the correct thing to do, and both of those are true, and you have been dealing with a dead man's paper for two months so you know better than most people that a true thing and a convenient thing are allowed to be the same thing." He stood up. "I will tell you what I came to tell you, and then I will go.

"I did not kill her. I have said that in this room and I will say it under caution and I will say it in a witness box. I was there, I lied about being there, I have lied for thirty years, and I am going to be convicted in the newspapers on Tuesday morning regardless of anything a court ever decides, and I have earned all of that.

"But I did not kill her, and there is one person on this earth who knew that for certain, and he is dead, and he spent thirty years watching me and writing it down."

Clara did not move.

"You knew," she said.

"About the room upstairs?" Amory almost smiled. "Miss Vance, I asked him in 1998 what it was and he told me it was a private library and threatened to stop selling me his overflow. A man does not do that about condensed novels. I have known there was something in this building since I was thirty five and I have never once tried to get at it, and you may believe that or not."

"You offered me fifty thousand over the market to clear it in nine days."

"Yes," he said. "I did."

"Why?"

"Because I have spent thirty years wanting to know what was in that room and being unable to ask," said Gerald Amory, "and in October a woman turned up who was going to sell the whole lot to a house clearance man in Sherborne, and for about four days I could have bought it, and I would have taken it home, and I would have sat up and read every one of them." His voice had gone rough. "And do you know what I would have done then? I have thought about that a great deal and I do not know. I would like to say I would have taken it to the police. I am sixty three and I have never once done the difficult thing in my life at the moment when it was difficult, and I am not going to stand in your shop and tell you I would have started."

He put his hat on.

"He was a better man than me by such a distance," he said, "and he was in love with her, and everybody in this town knew that except her."

"What?"

Amory stopped with his hand on the door.

"Edwin," he said. "He was forty nine and she was twenty two and it was not anything, and he never said a word to her and he never would have, and he was the kindest man I have ever met and it was completely obvious to every adult in Fenn Abbas and completely invisible to her." He shook his head slowly. "She used to laugh at him. Not cruelly. She'd tell me. *Edwin's put another book by for me.* And I would sit there and let her, because it suited me that she thought the person who was watching her was watching her out of kindness."

He opened the door onto the wet lane.

"Miss Vance. One last thing and then I am finished.

"If it goes the way it is going to go, and they charge me with lying to a police officer in 1996 and with whatever else the file will bear, then that is the end of Amory and Sons, which has been on that square since my grandfather, and my daughter will read about her father in the Echo, and I would rather be dead. And I have thought about all of that for two days and I find that I am, in a way I cannot explain to you, relieved."

Clara said, "Then answer the question."

"On Monday," said Gerald Amory.

He was three steps down the lane when she said it, and she said it because it came up out of her before she could stop it, and afterwards she could not decide whether she was glad.

"Did you know she had a daughter?"

He stopped in the rain.

He stood there with his back to her for a good five seconds, and then he turned round, and his face had come apart in a way that no rehearsal produces.

"She had a *what*," said Gerald Amory.
