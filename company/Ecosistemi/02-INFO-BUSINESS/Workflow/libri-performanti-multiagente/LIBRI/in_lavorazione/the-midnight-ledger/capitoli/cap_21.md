# The Fire

He answered everything.

Okafor rang on the Monday evening and said it in four words and then said nothing for long enough that Clara asked if she was still there.

"Six hours," she said. "Two breaks. He confirmed the relationship, he confirmed the vestry, he confirmed that he cut the pages out of the service register in the December of 1996 and burned them in his own grate, he confirmed that he lied in his statement, and he confirmed that he was in Fenn Abbas on the night of the fourth of November and that he came back on the last bus."

"He admitted the bus."

"He admitted the bus before I put the ticket to him, which I had been saving for two months," said Okafor, "and I want you to understand what that means, because it is the single most professional thing I have seen a suspect do in eighteen years. He has given me every fact I could prove and he has given them to me first, and each one of them now belongs to him instead of to me."

"And the churchyard?"

"He says he met her at ten past eleven by the yew. He says she told him she was pregnant. He says they argued for about fifteen minutes, quietly, because neither of them wanted to be heard. He says he told her he could not marry her and that he would pay, and she told him she did not want paying, and she walked away up the path towards the top gate, and he stood there, and then he went home along the top road."

"And she was alive."

"He says she was alive and walking when he last saw her, and that he learned she was dead at seven the following morning when he unlocked the church and saw her from the path." Okafor's voice was very even. "And, Clara, there is nothing whatever in this case that contradicts a word of it."

Clara stood in the middle of her shop with the phone against her ear.

"The eighteen minutes."

"He says he sat down on the wall and could not make his legs work and did not have a mobile telephone, because it was 1996 and he did not, and that he then walked to the vicarage and used theirs, and the vicarage log confirms a call at seven eighteen." A pause. "He is not lying about anything I can catch him on, and he has stopped lying about everything I can catch him on, and those are not the same thing and I cannot tell them apart."

"Her bag."

"He says he never saw a bag."

"Her coat is on my peg in a note written by a dead man."

"Her coat," said Okafor, "is the only thing in this entire case that Gerald Amory got visibly upset about, and he got upset about it because he did not know it was here, and if he had taken a bag off a peg in this shop at half past eleven at night he would have seen a coat hanging next to it, and he did not put a foot wrong for six hours until I said the word coat." She exhaled. "He has been released under investigation. It will be in the Echo on Thursday and on the local news by Friday, because a churchwarden and a bookseller from a family firm is a story, and the moment it is out I lose the two facts I was keeping."

"So what happens?"

"The exhumation licence came through this afternoon," said Nadia Okafor. "The faculty from the chancellor came through this morning, because Tom Sayers drove it to Salisbury himself and sat outside the registry until somebody signed it. We open the grave on the eighteenth."

The Echo ran it on the Thursday under a photograph of the churchyard gate.

Fenn Abbas took it the way a small town takes anything, which is to say completely and in every direction at once. Priya's takings went up forty per cent in three days. Somebody put a brick through the window of Amory and Sons on the Friday night and somebody else, on the Saturday, left forty seven signatures on a card of support on the counter of the same shop, and both of those things were done by people who had known Gerald Amory all their lives.

Clara kept the Ledger shut and worked upstairs.

She had one thing left. In two months she had matched three hundred and ninety eight of the four hundred and two notes to a line in a ledger strip. Four were unmatched, and three of those were simply damaged where the blade had gone through a figure, and the fourth was not damaged at all.

It was the last entry in volume eleven. The fourth of March 2026, the day before he died, written in a hand that had finally started to go, in the hand ruled column on the right.

*387.*

There was no book at page three hundred and eighty seven with a note in it. She had checked her spreadsheet eleven times. She had gone up and pulled every book in the room with more than three hundred and eighty seven pages, sixty one of them, and opened every one at that page, and found nothing.

She was doing it for the third time on the Saturday night when Priya started hammering on the front door.

Later, Clara could put the whole of it in order, because Okafor made her, twice.

She came down the stairs and the shop was full of a smell like a garage. Priya was at the glass with both hands, shouting something Clara could not hear, and pointing not at the front but along the side, towards the back of the building where the alley ran up to the churchyard wall.

Clara went through the curtain into the back room and the smell was very strong, and there was liquid coming under the back door in a slow tongue across the flags, and it had already reached the leg of the bed.

She did the two things she had rehearsed in her head every night for a month without ever once believing she would do them, which were to pick up the extinguisher from beside the stove and to not open the back door.

Then the door went up.

She never saw a flame arrive. There was a sound like a sheet being shaken and the bottom half of the door was alight and the fire went along the tongue of liquid across the floor towards the bed and the bed had a blanket on it that had been folded at the foot of it since March.

Clara emptied the extinguisher on the floor between the door and the bed and it went out, and came back, and she was out of extinguisher.

She got the door of the back room shut behind her and shoved the curtain rail down against it and went to the front and got the new lock open and Priya came in past her with a red cylinder in both arms, twenty nine years old, in a coat over pyjamas, having run two doors up a wet lane carrying nine kilos of dry powder.

Between them it took eleven minutes.

The fire brigade came from Sherborne at eleven minutes past midnight and by then there was nothing to do but open the windows and stand in the lane. The back room was gone: the door, the frame, the flags scorched black, the bed, the blanket, the chair, the paperback that had been open at page ninety since March.

The stairs did not catch. The shop did not catch. Upstairs, six hundred and eleven books stood in their runs with a finger of air at the end of each one and the blinds down, and the smell got into them and would be in them for years, and not one of them was harmed.

Clara sat on the churchyard wall at half past one in the morning with a blanket round her and watched a fire officer walk in and out of her own front door, and she was extremely calm and she knew that this was shock and she let it happen.

Priya sat next to her.

"You could have not come," Clara said.

"Don't."

"You could have rung them and stood in your window."

"Clara, I have been standing in my window watching that building since March," said Priya Raval, "and I did not go in when the light was on all night and I did not go in when he stopped opening on a Tuesday, and I have had seven months of thinking about what happens if you stand in a window. So no, actually, I could not have."

At two o'clock a fire officer came over and crouched in front of them.

"Petrol, poured under the door. There's a container in the alley and it's been left there, not thrown, and my colleague has photographed where it is and nobody is touching it." He looked at Clara. "Is there anything upstairs I should know about?"

"Yes," said Clara. "There's evidence in an unsolved death from 1996 and you need to ring DS Nadia Okafor at Dorset before you ring anybody else."

The fire officer went away and made a telephone call.

And Clara Vance sat on a wall in the dark, looking at the smoke going up past her own crooked windows towards a church tower, and thought about a man of sixty three saying *I would rather be dead* in her shop with his hat in his hands, and found, to her own great surprise, that she did not believe it had been him for one second.
