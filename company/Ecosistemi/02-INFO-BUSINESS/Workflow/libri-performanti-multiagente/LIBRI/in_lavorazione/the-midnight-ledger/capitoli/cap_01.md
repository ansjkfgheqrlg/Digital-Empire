# A Key in an Envelope

The letter had come on a Tuesday, in a brown envelope with a Dorchester postmark, and Clara Vance had read it three times standing up in the corridor of a flat she could no longer afford.

It was four lines long. *Dear Ms Vance. We act in the estate of the late Mr Edwin Pryor, of The Midnight Ledger, Church Lane, Fenn Abbas. Under the terms of his will the property and its contents pass to you absolutely. Please contact this office at your convenience.*

There was a key taped to the inside of the folded sheet. Not a modern key. A long iron thing with a hollow shaft and a bit like a small flag, the sort that belongs to a lock made before anyone thought locks needed improving.

She had rung the solicitor the same afternoon and asked the only question that mattered, which was whether they had the right Clara Vance, and a woman called Miss Deare had confirmed her date of birth, her middle name, the address of a flat she had lived in nine years ago in Bristol, and the name of the primary school she had attended in Taunton before that. All correct. All the sort of thing a person could not guess.

"And Mr Pryor," Clara had said. "Did he leave any indication of, I don't know. Why."

"He did not."

"Did he leave anything else at all? A letter?"

"The property and its contents," Miss Deare said, in the voice of a woman who had already read the file twice and found it as unsatisfying as everyone else did. "That is the whole of it, Ms Vance. I asked him myself, when he came in to sign. He said the contents were the letter."

Six days later Clara took the last train that stopped at Fenn Abbas and arrived on a Thursday afternoon in the middle of October with one holdall, a laptop bag, and a plan she had worked out somewhere around Westbury, which was: look at it, price it, sell it, go home.

She had a job to go back to. That was not true. She had two months left on a contract at a conservation studio, and after that there was nothing pencilled in, and the studio had been careful with her in the particular way of people who already know they will not be renewing you. She was thirty four and she restored books for a living and she was extremely good at it and there was not enough of it, which was the whole of her working life summarised in one sentence.

So: look at it, price it, sell it.

The station at Fenn Abbas was a single platform with a shelter, and the road up from it ran between hedges for a quarter of a mile before the first houses. It had rained earlier. The air smelled of wet hawthorn and, faintly, of woodsmoke, and the light was already going, that dead flat October light that makes four in the afternoon look like six.

The town when she reached it was smaller than she had allowed for. A market square with a war memorial and a chemist and a bank that had been turned into a coffee place. A pub with a board out advertising a quiz. On the far side of the square, a bookshop.

Not hers. She knew it was not hers before she read the sign, because it was the wrong kind of shop entirely: a wide double frontage painted a careful dark green, a window with fifteen books in it arranged on stands and nothing else, gold lettering, an interior she could see through the glass with lamps on and a woman at a counter and a coffee machine going. AMORY AND SONS. EST. 1961.

It looked like the sort of bookshop people photograph.

Clara walked past it and found Church Lane on the third try, because it did not go off the square, it went off a side road that went off the square, and it was not so much a lane as a slot between two buildings that widened after twenty yards into a cobbled way running along the wall of the churchyard.

And there it was.

The Midnight Ledger occupied the last house on the lane, and the house was crooked. Not picturesquely crooked. Genuinely out of true, the whole upper floor leaning perhaps four inches towards the church, the window frames adjusted over two and a half centuries to keep pace with the lean so that every one of them was a slightly different parallelogram. Three storeys, the top one dark. Brick that had gone the colour of strong tea. A shopfront with small panes, and above it, on a hanging board that had not been repainted in a long time, the name in letters that had once been gold.

Every window on the front of that house looked out at the churchyard of St Botolph, which came right up to the far side of the cobbles and rose slightly, so that the nearer gravestones stood at about the height of the shop windows and looked in.

Clara stood on the cobbles for a while with her holdall on her shoulder.

She had prepared herself for a dusty shop. She had not prepared herself for the feeling that arrived in the middle of her chest and sat there, which was not fear and was not grief, and which she eventually identified, standing in a lane in a town she had never been to, as the feeling of arriving somewhere.

"Ridiculous," she said, out loud, and got the key out.

The lock took the key without complaint and turned with a sound like a small bone setting. The door swept an arc through the dust on the floor, and a bell over it rang once, and Clara Vance walked into her inheritance.

It was dark and it smelled of paper.

She found a switch by feel, and three of the four bulbs came on, and she saw the shop properly for the first time.

It was not a room with books in it. It was a room made of books. Shelving ran floor to ceiling on every wall and on both sides of four freestanding stacks that divided the floor into aisles barely wide enough for one person, and every shelf was full, and on top of the full shelves there were books laid flat in the gap below the shelf above, and at the foot of each stack there were books in columns, and on the two chairs there were books, and under the two chairs there were books. A ladder on a rail. A counter of dark wood with a brass till that could not possibly still work. A door at the back with a curtain over it. A staircase going up, boxed in, with books on nine of the eleven visible treads.

Clara had valued private collections before. She stood in the middle of the floor and did the thing she did automatically, running a count along one shelf, multiplying by shelves, by stacks, by walls.

Eight thousand. Give or take five hundred.

"Oh, Edwin," she said. "What did you do."

She walked the aisles slowly. General stock, the kind you find anywhere: orange spined paperbacks, book club hardbacks, a whole section of local history, a shelf of Ordnance Survey maps of counties nobody in Dorset needed. Everything was priced in pencil on the top corner of the front endpaper, in a small square hand that never varied. Everything was in reasonable order. Everything was clean. The dust was on the floor and the sills and the counter, seven months of it, but not on the books, and Clara understood from that alone that until March somebody had come in here every single day and looked after eight thousand books that nobody was buying.

She reached the counter.

There was a cup on it.

A plain white cup, a third full of something that had dried to a brown ring with a darker crust at the edge, standing on a wooden counter beside a pencil, a rubber, and a stack of five books with a slip of paper on top. The slip said, in the small square hand, *hold for H. Boon, Friday.*

Clara looked at the cup for a long time.

The solicitor had said that Edwin Pryor died at home in March. Clara had assumed that meant a house somewhere in the town. Standing at the counter with her hand not quite touching the cup, she revised the assumption, and found that she had known for about ten minutes, since the moment she saw the state of the books, and that she had simply not said it to herself yet.

He had died here. He had put his cup down, and gone through the curtain at the back, and not come out.

She was aware, standing there, that the correct response was to be unsettled, and that she was not. What she felt, in fact, was a kind of steadiness, as if some part of her that had been braced for months had finally put its weight down.

She made herself do the practical things. She found the fuse box under the stairs and confirmed the power was on. She found a sink in the back room, and a kettle, and a small cold cupboard with nothing in it but a jar of instant coffee and two tins of soup with a best before date of the previous year. She found a single bed made up neatly against the wall of the back room, with a folded blanket at the foot of it and a paperback face down on a chair beside it, still open at page ninety.

Eighty one years old, and he had slept in the back room of his own shop. There were two floors above her head and he had slept down here beside the stove.

She looked at the paperback for a moment and did not close it.

Back in the shop the light had gone entirely outside, and the windows had turned into black mirrors with gravestones standing behind them where the reflections were thin. Clara filled the kettle and set it going for something to do, and while it boiled she went back to the counter, because there was one thing she had noticed and put aside.

The five books held for H. Boon were ordinary. Two crime paperbacks, a gardening hardback, a Dick Francis, a large print romance. She turned to the front endpaper of the first, expecting the pencilled price.

There was a price. There was also, an inch below it, a line of pencil in the same small square hand that was not a price at all.

*not the third one, the third one is wrong*

Clara read it twice. Then she opened the second book, and found nothing, and the third, and found nothing, and the fourth, and found, in the same place, in the same hand:

*ask her about the lamp*

Behind her, at the back of the shop, something moved.

She turned fast. Nothing. The curtain over the back door hung still. The aisles were empty in both directions.

Then, from the top of the boxed staircase, from the floor above where she had not yet been and where no light was on, came a sound she knew as well as she knew her own name, because it was the sound of her trade and her whole adult life.

A page turning.

Then another.

Then a long pause, the exact length of a person reading a page, and then another.

Clara stood at the counter of a bookshop she had owned for six days, in a town she had reached four hours ago, and listened to somebody upstairs read.
