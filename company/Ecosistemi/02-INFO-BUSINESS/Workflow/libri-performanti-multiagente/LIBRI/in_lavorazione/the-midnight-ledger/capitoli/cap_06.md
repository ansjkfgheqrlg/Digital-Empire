# Who Edwin Pryor Was

Clara went and got the five books.

She carried them down the lane in the rain under her jacket and put them on the table in front of Hettie Boon, and Hettie took the slip off the top and read *hold for H. Boon, Friday* in the small square hand, and put it in her handbag without a word and did up the clasp.

"Right," she said. "What do I owe you."

"Nothing."

"Don't be daft."

"Mrs Boon, I've inherited eight thousand books and I can't sell any of them fast enough to matter. Take the five."

Hettie considered this from several angles, visibly, and then accepted it with a small nod that Clara understood to be a considerable concession.

"Miss Boon," she said. "Never married. Nearly did, in 1961, and he went to Canada and I have never once regretted it." She spread the five books out on the table and put her hand flat on the Dick Francis. "He always knew. Thirty years and he never once put me a book by on a Friday that I'd already had, and I read four a week."

"Can I ask you something odd?"

"You can ask me something odd."

Clara opened the first of the five to the front endpaper and turned it round.

*not the third one, the third one is wrong*

Hettie looked at it for a long moment. Then she took her glasses off, cleaned them on a napkin with great deliberation, put them back on and looked again.

"That's Edwin's hand," she said.

"Does it mean anything to you?"

"No." She turned the book slightly, as though a different angle might help. "It's not about the book, is it. This is a Barbara Vine and there isn't a third one, it's a standalone."

"There's another." Clara opened the fourth book. *ask her about the lamp.*

And Hettie Boon went white.

It happened quickly and completely and Priya was round the counter with a glass of water before Clara had finished registering it, and there was a small commotion, and Hettie waved them both off with real irritation and drank the water anyway.

"I'm eighty eight," she said. "You'd think I'd have got past being surprised."

"Miss Boon, what lamp?"

Hettie put the glass down.

"There's a lamp at the churchyard gate," she said. "There's been one there since before the war. It's on the wall of the Ledger, on the corner, and it lights the gate and the first bit of the path, and it's the only light down that end of the lane."

"I've seen it. It was on last night."

"It's on a switch." Hettie looked up. "Not a timer. Not the council. It's on the shop's supply and it's always been on the shop's supply, and there's a switch for it inside the front door of your building, on the left, low down, under the coats. The vicar pays your electric for it, or he's supposed to, four pound a quarter, and I doubt anybody's collected since about 2004."

Clara had seen that switch. It was a round brown Bakelite thing under a row of pegs, and she had assumed it was dead.

"Edwin put that light on every night of his life," said Hettie. "Every night. In thirty one years I never once walked past that gate in the dark and found it off, and I have walked past that gate in the dark about four thousand times, because I live in Sheepwash Row and there is no other way home."

"Why does that matter?"

"Because it wasn't on," Hettie said, "on the fourth of November, 1996."

Priya sat down.

"I want to be careful," Hettie said. She said it slowly, and Clara realised that she was not being careful for her own sake. "I am eighty eight years old and I have been telling this story to myself for thirty years, and a story you tell yourself for thirty years is not evidence, it's a story. So I will tell you what I know I saw, and then I will tell you what I think, and you will keep them in separate pockets, please."

"I will."

"What I saw." Hettie folded her hands. "I came back from my sister's at Hurstock on the last bus, which put me on the square at ten past eleven. I walked down Church Lane. The gate lamp was off. I noticed because I had to put my hand out for the wall. And there were two people standing inside the gate, up the path a bit, by the yew."

"Could you see them?"

"No. I could hear them and I could see two shapes against the sky, and one was taller. They were not shouting. That's the thing I have always remembered. They were talking quietly and quickly, both at once, the way people do when they are having the same argument for the fourth time."

"Man and a woman?"

"I think so and I would not swear to it." Hettie's mouth tightened. "I went home. I was sixty years old, it was gone eleven, it was cold, and it was the churchyard, and people did stand in the churchyard, courting couples used it, everybody used it. I went home and I put the kettle on and I did not think about it again until Wednesday."

"What happened on Wednesday?"

"They found Ruth Feltham on the path by the yew on Tuesday morning," said Hettie Boon, "and it was in the Echo on the Wednesday, and I went to the police station at Sherborne on the Thursday and I told a young constable exactly what I have just told you."

Clara did not move.

"And?"

"He wrote it down." Hettie looked at her hands. "He wrote it in a notebook, not on a form. He was very pleasant. He said someone would be in touch. And nobody was ever in touch, and in January the coroner said she had fallen and struck her head on the kerb of the Fenn tomb and that was an accident, and I read that in the Echo too, sitting where I am sitting now, in this room when it was Vokins the grocer."

"You didn't go back?"

"I did go back." Hettie said it without heat. "Twice. In February and in about the April. The second time a sergeant told me the file was closed and that a lamp being off wasn't evidence of anything and that two shapes in the dark at a distance of thirty yards, at night, at my age, wasn't either, and he was perfectly polite about it, and he was not entirely wrong." She lifted her chin. "I was sixty. He said at my age. I have thought about that for thirty years too."

Priya said, very quietly, "Het, you never said."

"I said it four times in six months in 1996 and it went nowhere, and then I stopped, because I had got a reputation in the queue at the butcher's and I did not care for it." She looked at Clara. "And that is what I saw. Now I will tell you what I think, in the other pocket. I think somebody turned that lamp off. I do not think it failed, because it was on again the next night and nobody mended it, and I know nobody mended it because there was nobody in this town to mend it except Edwin and Edwin was in Dorchester all day Tuesday, and I know that because he shut the shop and put a card in the door and I have never seen him do it before or since."

Clara's coffee had gone cold in front of her. She could feel her own pulse in the tips of her fingers.

"Miss Boon," she said. "Did Edwin know Ruth Feltham?"

Hettie looked at her with something like pity.

"He employed her," she said. "Eleven months. She was behind that counter three days a week from the January to the November, and she was twenty two and she was quick and she was rude to people in a way they liked, and she doubled his takings. Everybody in this town knew Ruth Feltham. Ask anybody over fifty."

"Nobody has mentioned her to me."

"No," said Hettie. "They wouldn't."

The rain had stopped outside. A van went down the lane too fast.

"And after," Clara said. "Edwin, after."

"He shut the shop for eight days." Hettie's voice had gone thin. "That was the only time. Then he opened it again and he ran it for thirty more years and he never took anybody on behind that counter again, not a Saturday girl, not a soul. And he stopped going anywhere. He was in the choir and he left the choir. He was on the fair committee and he came off it. He would talk to you all day about a book and he would not talk to you about anything else ever again, and he built those shelves upstairs that spring, because I heard him doing it and I asked him what he was building and he said shelves, Hettie, and shut the door."

Clara said, "Who else knew her? Who was she with?"

"That's what nobody could tell you," said Hettie. "She had somebody. A girl of twenty two who is that pleased with herself all summer has got somebody. She never brought him in here and she never brought him to the pub and her father never met him, and after she died the police asked and asked and not one person in Fenn Abbas could give them a name, which at the time everybody thought was very sad."

"And now?"

Hettie Boon picked up her five books and put them in her shopping trolley one at a time.

"Now," she said, "I am eighty eight, and I have had thirty years to notice that in a town this size there is only one reason nobody can give you a name."
