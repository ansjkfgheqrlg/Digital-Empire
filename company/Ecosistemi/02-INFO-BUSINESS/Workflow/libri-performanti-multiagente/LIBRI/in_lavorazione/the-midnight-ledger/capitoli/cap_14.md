# A Thing With Boards

She waited until nine and rang Okafor and said, "I need you here today and I need you to bring the analyst, and I need somebody to watch me do this on camera from start to finish."

"Why?"

"Because in about two hours I am going to take a scalpel to a piece of evidence," said Clara, "and I would like a police officer standing next to me while I do it."

Okafor arrived at eleven with a woman called Danni Ellis who did two days a week and had a tripod, and Clara had spent the two hours doing what she should have been doing since the day she arrived, which was looking at six hundred and eleven books as objects rather than as texts.

She had them laid out on the shop counter in the order she had found them, twenty two of them.

"Explain it to me as though I'm stupid," said Okafor, "which for the next ten minutes I am."

"A hardback book has boards." Clara held one up. "Two pieces of card, the thickness of a beer mat, covered in cloth or paper, hinged to the spine. That's what makes it a hardback. The board is not solid, it is layers, pressed and glued, and it is the one part of a book that nobody ever looks at, because there is nothing to look at."

"Go on."

"If you know how to do it, you can lift the pastedown, which is the sheet of paper stuck to the inside of the board, and you can split the board itself, and you can lay a folded sheet of paper inside it, and you can glue it back and press it overnight and put the pastedown down again over the top." She turned the book over in her hands. "It's a real technique. It's how people got documents out of occupied countries. It's also how forgers hide provenance. I have taken things out of boards eleven times in my career and I have never once put anything in."

"And you can spot it."

"That's the point I have been an idiot about for three weeks." Clara set the book down. "You cannot spot it by looking. You can spot it by *weight*, and by thickness at the fore edge, and by the sound it makes when you tap the board with a fingernail. I have handled seven hundred books in this building and I have been reading all of them and weighing none of them, because I came here as a person looking for pencil."

She lined up the twenty two.

"These are the ones. Six hundred and eleven volumes upstairs, five hundred and four of them are hardbacks, and twenty two of those have boards that are between one and a half and two millimetres over. All twenty two are in the two hundred and nine that have no note in them, and I could not understand for a week why he would keep two hundred and nine books with nothing in them at all."

"Camouflage," said Danni Ellis, from behind the camera.

"Camouflage. He needed a population of blank ones or the twenty two would have been the only books in the room without a note." Clara looked up. "Twenty two boards. Ten ledgers is twenty boards. Two spare."

Okafor was very still.

"Do it," she said.

It took four hours.

Clara worked at the counter under a lamp with the camera running and Danni Ellis calling out times, and she did it the way she would have done it for a museum, which was slowly. Lift the pastedown with a hot spatula and a wet brush and infinite patience. Ease the split. Withdraw. Photograph in situ before, during and after. Number the board, number the extraction, seal each sheaf in a polythene sleeve that Okafor signed across the flap.

Volume I came out of the boards of a 1968 book club edition of *The Cruel Sea*.

It was not a ledger. Clara had said ten ledgers and she had been wrong about that too, and she was glad, because a ledger would have been four hundred leaves and would not have gone into anything. What Edwin Pryor had put in the boards was the ruled right hand column and nothing else: he had gone through each of the first ten ledgers with a straight edge and a scalpel of his own and cut out the hand ruled strip from every page that carried a number, and thrown the rest away.

Ten volumes reduced to two hundred and six narrow strips of paper, in order, folded in fours, hidden in twenty boards across a room of six hundred books.

Each strip carried a date, a number, and, in the description column that Clara had assumed was lost, whatever fraction of the entry had fallen inside the cut.

*4.xi.96 . . . . . . . . 53*

*4.xi.96 . . . . . . . . 219*

*4.xi.96 . . . . . . . 88*

*5.xi.96 . . . . . . . . 12*

*5.xi.96 . . . . . . . nil pm*

There were thirty two entries for the fourth and fifth of November 1996.

Clara laid them on the counter in a row and stood back and put both hands flat on the wood.

"That's it," she said. "That is the whole of it. Thirty two numbers. Each number is a page. Each page is in a book upstairs, and I have all four hundred and two of them photographed and logged and I know exactly which book each one is in."

"How long?"

"To read it in his order?" She was already reaching for the laptop. "An hour."

"No," said Okafor.

Clara stopped.

"Not today, and not by you, and I am sorry." Okafor put a hand flat on the ledger strips, not roughly. "Danni, stop recording. Miss Vance, sit down a moment.

"You have just done something on camera that I will be able to put in front of a court, and I want you to understand what you have got, because it is not what you think. Until four hours ago, what we had was a room full of pencil that any competent barrister would tell a jury was written by the woman standing in front of me last month. As of this afternoon, you have handed me twenty two books whose boards were split and reglued and pressed, and pressed boards take days, and the glue is animal glue that has been in there for years, and that is testable and datable and it is the first thing in this entire building that you could not possibly have faked in a fortnight."

Clara sat down. Her legs, she discovered, had been going to do it anyway.

"So the notes are real."

"So the *concealment* is real, which is better," said Okafor. "It means a man built an index and hid it, and that means the thing indexed mattered to him, and that gets me a great deal further with a senior officer at half past eight on a Monday morning than four hundred pencil notes ever would." She squared the sleeves. "Now. Reading it. You have logged four hundred and two notes and you found them and you photographed them and that is your work and nobody is taking it off you. But if you sit down tonight and put thirty two of them in an order and write out a story of what happened on the fourth of November 1996, then for the rest of this case the story belongs to you, and you are a witness with an unexplained connection to the deceased's employer, and I will not be able to use a word of it."

"Then who reads it?"

"Danni reads it. Cold, from your log, from the strips, without your spreadsheet and without ever having spoken to Hettie Boon, in an office in Winfrith, tomorrow, with a supervisor's signature at the bottom." Okafor stood. "And then, and only then, you and I sit down and compare what she got with what you would have got, and if they match, Miss Vance, we have got something."

Clara wanted to argue and could not find a single hole in it.

"There's a spare board," she said instead. "Twenty two boards, ten volumes, twenty strips of strips. Two of them held something else."

She had left them until last on purpose.

The first spare board was in a copy of *A Shropshire Lad* and it held a single sheet of writing paper, folded in four, blank on both sides except for one line at the top in the small square hand:

*If it is you reading this then I was right about one thing at least.*

The second spare board was in a book about ferns.

Clara opened it, and lifted the pastedown, and eased the split, and drew out a photograph.

It was colour, six by four, the emulsion gone slightly warm the way 1990s prints do. It showed a young woman of about twenty standing in the doorway of a shop that Clara had walked through eleven times a day for a month, squinting into low sun, laughing at whoever was holding the camera, with her hair blowing sideways.

On her hip, held with the entirely unconscious competence of somebody who does it every day, was a small child in a red coat.

Clara turned it over.

On the back, in pencil, in that hand:

*R. and C., June.*
