# Why Edwin Never Spoke

They arrested Wallace Feltham on the Sunday afternoon.

Okafor came and told her herself, standing in a shop that smelled of petrol and wet ash, and did not sit down.

"His prints are on the container and he did not wipe it and he did not hide it and he was at home when they went. He has made a full account in seven minutes and he has not asked for a solicitor and he keeps saying he does not want one." Her jaw was set. "He is seventy seven and he will be charged tomorrow and I have never in my life wanted less to do a thing that is entirely correct."

Clara had to sit down herself.

"Why?"

"Because you were wrong," said Okafor, "and I want to say that once and then never again, because on that wall on Saturday night you told a fire officer you did not believe it was Gerald Amory, and you were right about that and right for no reason, and if you had been wrong you would have said a thing to a witness that could have cost me the case. You are not a detective. You are a woman who reads paper very well, and those are different jobs, and the difference nearly bit you."

"Understood."

"Good." Okafor sat down after all. "He did not do it to stop the case. He is desperate for the case. He rang me twice last week to ask about the eighteenth."

"Then what?"

"He did it because of the back room," said Okafor.

Clara did not understand for a second, and then she did, all at once, and put her hand over her mouth.

"He told me this morning, in a voice like a man reading a shopping list," said Okafor. "He said: I have had thirty years of that man walking up my path on the fourth of November and standing at my gate, and I let him, because I thought he was being kind. And now I find he knew. He knew who the fellow was and he knew about the baby and he knew about the girl, and he had it written down, all of it, all that time, and he sat on my bench in the rain and he said nothing, and now he is dead and he has arranged for the whole town to be told and he does not have to be here for it."

"So he burned his bed."

"He walked past a shop with a hundred and fifty tons of paper in it," said Okafor, "and he went round the back, and he poured a five litre can of unleaded under the door of the one room where Edwin Pryor slept, and he put the can down neatly in the alley and he walked home. He did not know you were in the building. When I told him you were in the building he was sick."

She stood up again.

"He will be charged with arson being reckless as to whether life was endangered, and he will plead guilty, and in April, when the whole of the rest of this is in every newspaper in the country, a jury will not be needed for that bit, and I will stand up in a Crown Court and read a personal statement from Wallace Feltham that will take the roof off, and he will very probably be given a suspended sentence, and none of that is justice, it is just what happens."

At the door she stopped.

"He asked me to tell you one thing. He said: tell her I have never once in my life been angry at her, and that I am sorry about the room, and that I hope she is not frightened of me, because on the eighteenth I would like her to stand with me."

Clara found the note on the Monday.

She had gone back to *387* because it was the only thing left in the world she could do with her hands, and she had done it the way she should have done it in December, which was to stop looking for a book with three hundred and eighty seven pages and start looking at what had actually happened on the fourth of March 2026.

The last day of Edwin Pryor's life. A Wednesday. He had opened the shop, taken nothing, written *nil pm* in a ledger, put a number in the margin, and gone into the back room and died in the night.

And on his counter, when Clara Vance walked in seven months later, there had been a cup, and a pencil, and a rubber, and a stack of five books with a slip of paper on top, and the slip had said *hold for H. Boon, Friday.*

She had given them away on her fourth day in Fenn Abbas, over a table in a coffee shop, to an eighty eight year old woman who had put them in a shopping trolley one at a time.

Hettie Boon opened her door at Sheepwash Row in a housecoat.

"I've read four of them," she said, when Clara had finished. "I've not started the fifth because it's a fat one and I've been keeping it."

"Which one is it?"

"Large print. *The House at Harrow Bay.* Six hundred pages and every one of them daft." She was already going through to the front room. "It's on the arm of the chair."

Clara opened it at page 387 with Hettie standing next to her, and it was there, in the margin, level with the fourth line, in a hand that had gone shaky at last:

*C., the back board of this book. E.P., 4.iii.26.*

She did the board on Hettie's kitchen table at ten in the morning with a bread knife, badly, because she did not have a single tool with her and would not wait, and Hettie Boon stood over her with her arms folded and said nothing at all.

Inside was one sheet of lined A4 paper, folded in four, and it had been folded so long that it opened along the creases like a hinge.

It was not in Edwin's hand. It was round and quick and it slanted, and it had been written fast with a biro that skipped.

*Edwin,*

*I'm sorry to ask. I've asked everyone I can ask and that's you and that's the whole list, which tells you something about me.*

*Will you tell him I'll be there at eleven. He'll come if it's you, he thinks you're nobody. Sorry. You know what I mean.*

*I'm going to tell him about the baby. Not to make him do anything. I've stopped wanting him to do anything, which is the only good thing that's happened this year. I'm telling him because she's going to exist and she's not going to grow up with people whispering, and the way you stop people whispering is you say it out loud first. Dad will be all right. Dad's better than everyone thinks.*

*And I'm going to tell him about Clara.*

*I've never said her name to anyone in this town and I've just written it down and I've had to stop for a bit.*

*She's four in March. She's in Taunton. She's got people who are good to her and I signed the papers when I was seventeen because everyone I trusted said it and I have thought about it every day for four years and I am not going to undo it, because it isn't hers to be undone. But she exists, Edwin, and I've got a photograph of her on your step and if I'm going to stand in that churchyard and say one true thing out loud then I'm saying both of them or neither.*

*Here's the ask, and it's a big one, and you can say no.*

*If it goes wrong, don't tell them about her.*

*I don't mean the police. I mean anyone. His father is not a kind man and neither is he when he's frightened, and there are four shops and a name and I've watched what that family does about things that are inconvenient. She's safe because nobody knows she's there. If it ever comes out that Ruth Feltham had a girl in Taunton, then that girl has got the Amorys in her life for ever and she has done nothing to deserve them.*

*So: nobody. Not Dad, and I know what I'm asking. Not the police, if it comes to that. Not until she's grown and it can't be done to her.*

*I know you'll do it because you're the only person I've ever met who does what he says he'll do and doesn't make a thing of it.*

*Leave the side light on for me. I'll be back for my coat.*

*Ruth. 4th Nov.*

Clara read it standing at a kitchen table in a council house at the top of Sheepwash Row, and then she sat down on Hettie Boon's kitchen chair and read it again, and Hettie put the kettle on and did not speak for eleven minutes, which was, Clara thought afterwards, the greatest act of tact she had ever witnessed.

"He did what she said," Hettie said eventually.

"He did exactly what she said."

"For thirty years."

"He had this in his hand the whole time." Clara's voice was not working properly. "It's the only thing in the world that proves the meeting was arranged and who it was arranged with, and it says *tell him I'll be there at eleven*, and it is signed and dated in her hand, and any police officer in England would have had Gerald Amory in a room within a day."

"And it says her name," said Hettie.

"It says my name. Twice. And where I was." Clara put both hands flat on the table. "He couldn't hand it over without handing me over. So he built the whole of the rest of it out of pencil and shop books, thirty years of it, so that one day somebody could get to the end without ever needing this piece of paper, and then he waited."

"For what?"

"For me to be thirty four," said Clara.

She looked up.

"They reopened the case on the second of February. He changed his will on the eleventh. He was eighty one and he had been waiting for the police to come back for thirty years, and when they finally did, the promise had run out, because I was grown and it could not be done to me any more."
