# Seven Months

Okafor came on the Sunday and Clara told her all of it, in order, from the bell to the last thing said in the rain, and Okafor listened with her coat still on and did not write anything down until the end.

"He'll deny that conversation happened," she said.

"He won't, though. That's the thing. He came here to say it out loud to somebody who would remember it."

"I know," said Okafor, "and that is what worries me, and I would like you to sit with that for a minute before you decide he is a broken man who has come to confess. A man who has lied for thirty years and is about to be interviewed under caution went to see the principal civilian witness at night, alone, unrecorded, and told her a version of events, and made a point of not answering the only question that matters." She looked up. "That is either the most honest thing he has ever done or the most careful, and I have met people who could not tell you which it was themselves."

"He didn't know about me."

"No," said Okafor. "I watched your face while you told me that bit and I believe you, and I have been doing this a long time and I want you to understand what it costs me to say the next thing.

"It fits either way. If he did not know, then he had no reason to want her silenced and that is enormously in his favour. If he did know, then a girl of twenty two turning up in a churchyard at eleven at night with a four year old she has never mentioned to a man whose wedding is booked for April is a motive so obvious that a jury would need it explained once."

"But she didn't. That's the wrong way round. I was born in ninety two. He'd have known about me in the January if she was going to tell him at all."

"Unless the reason she was in that churchyard was to tell him," said Okafor, "for the first time, having not told him for ten months."

Clara sat down.

"Why would she wait ten months?"

"That," said Okafor, "is the third question I have written on my whiteboard, and I have had it there since October, and the first two are *where is her coat* and *where is her bag*." She finally took her coat off, which Clara had learned meant she was staying. "And on Thursday I got an answer to a question I had not written down at all, which I have come here to give you in person because it would be indecent to do it any other way."

She took an envelope out of her bag and did not open it.

"I told you in October that the pathologist's report is not in the file and not at the hospital. That is still true. What I did not tell you, because I did not know, is that a pathologist keeps a personal case book. Dr Muriel Enright did the post mortem on Ruth Feltham on the sixth of November 1996 at Dorchester. She retired in 2009 and died in 2021, and her executor gave forty years of case books to the Royal College because that is what you do with them, and I found them in a catalogue on a Tuesday afternoon in a way that would embarrass you if I described it."

"You have the report."

"I have her working notes, which are better, because a report is written for a coroner and a case book is written for herself." Okafor put the envelope on the counter. "I am not going to make you read it. I am going to tell you what is in it and then it is yours to read or burn.

"Cause of death is as recorded: a depressed fracture at the back of the skull, consistent with striking a kerb or a raised stone edge. Dr Enright's note says the injury is consistent with a fall and she does not resile from that. There is no other injury of note. There is nothing under her fingernails. There is no evidence of a struggle."

Clara let out a breath she had not known she was holding.

"So it was an accident."

"That is not what a pathologist means by *consistent with*," said Okafor gently. "A depressed fracture at the back of the skull is consistent with a fall and it is equally consistent with a shove, and Muriel Enright wrote that in her case book in exactly those words, and she wrote something else underneath it that never reached the coroner because nobody ever asked her a question that would let her say it. She wrote: *no history taken from anyone who saw her that evening. Cannot exclude.*"

"That's it?"

"That is not it." Okafor put two fingers on the envelope. "Clara. The post mortem records a pregnancy of approximately seven months."

The shop went very quiet.

Clara heard the rain, and the clock, and a car in the lane, and none of them seemed to have anything to do with her.

"Seven months," she said.

"Approximately twenty nine weeks. Dr Enright noted it, measured it, recorded it, and she wrote in her case book, and I am quoting: *told officer in attendance. He asked whether it was necessary to include. I said it was a finding. Nothing further.*"

"It's not in the newspaper."

"It is not in the newspaper, it is not in the four page file, it is not in the coroner's finding of accidental death, and it was not known to her father, who I sat with on Friday morning for two hours." Okafor's voice had gone flat and hard. "Wallace Feltham found out at the age of seventy seven, in his own front room, from me, on Friday, that he had lost a grandchild as well, and I will be carrying that for a while."

Clara put both hands flat on the counter.

"Twenty nine weeks," she said. "In November. So she was pregnant in the June, and everybody in that shop and that town saw her every day, and in the notes there is one that says *she was frightened of him in June and not in October and that is the wrong way round*, and Edwin could not work out why she stopped being frightened and I can, because at some point between June and October she decided."

"Decided what?"

"To keep it. And to say so." Clara looked up. "She wasn't frightened in October because she'd stopped negotiating. You're not frightened once you've decided. You're frightened while you're still hoping the other person will make it come out all right."

Okafor said nothing for a moment.

"There's a thing that goes with it," Clara said. "The note about the coat. *Two coats on the peg and only one of them went out.* I've had it as evidence for two months and I've never once read it as a fact about her."

"Meaning?"

"It was minus two that night. She left her coat because she was coming back." Clara's voice had gone unsteady. "You don't go out in your jumper at eleven at night in November to end something. You do it because you've got twenty words to say and then you're going to walk back down a path and put the kettle on. She wasn't going out there afraid. She was going out there for five minutes."

"That's interpretation."

"It's arithmetic about a coat," said Clara. "She was seven months pregnant and it was freezing and she left it on the peg. Whatever she thought was going to happen in that churchyard, she thought it was going to be quick and she thought she was coming home."

Okafor wrote three words on her pad and underlined one of them.

"You will not say any of that in a witness box," she said. "You will say what you found and where you found it. But for what it is worth, that is the first sentence anybody has said to me about this case in ten months that sounded like a person instead of a file."

She picked up her coat.

"Amory is interviewed at nine tomorrow. He will be asked about the vestry, the register, the ticket, the statement of 1996, the finding of the body, and the eighteen minutes. He will not be asked about a pregnancy or about you, because I am not putting those in the first interview and I will tell you exactly why: they are the only two facts in this case that he cannot have prepared for, and I am keeping them."

"And if he answers everything?"

"Then he answers everything," said Okafor, "and I will have got a great deal further than I was in February, and Ruth Feltham will have a file with more than four pages in it, and some of this town will find out what it was actually living next to, and none of that requires anybody to be convicted of anything."

She stopped at the door.

"One more. I have applied for an exhumation."

Clara stared at her.

"Not to reopen a grave for the sake of it. Because a body buried in 1996 in Dorset clay with a recorded pregnancy has, potentially, foetal material, and foetal material has paternal DNA, and there is no other route to that fact on this earth." Okafor buttoned her coat. "It goes to the Home Office and then to the diocese, because it is consecrated ground, and it needs a licence and a faculty and the consent of the nearest relative, and the nearest relative is a man of seventy seven who has already been told one thing this week that he should not have had to hear."

"Will you ask him?"

"I asked him on Friday," said Nadia Okafor. "He said yes before I finished the sentence, and then he asked me whether he was allowed to be there, and I said yes, and he said, then I'll be there, because nobody was there the first time."
