# Eleven Books

The bookmark was a bus ticket.

Clara took it out with the flat of her thumbnail, the way she would have taken anything out of anything, and laid it on the floorboard beside the book, and photographed it before she turned it over.

WILTS AND DORSET. FENN ABBAS TO DORCHESTER. SINGLE. It had been through a machine that printed the date in dot matrix, and the date had faded to almost nothing, and the almost nothing said 04 NOV 96.

She sat back on her heels.

Outside the window the sky over St Botolph's had gone the colour of a wet pavement. It was ten past six in the morning. Clara Vance had been the owner of The Midnight Ledger for eight days and had been inside it for seventeen hours, and she was sitting on the floor of a locked room holding a bus ticket that was older than most of the pages she had ever repaired.

She did what she always did when a thing got large. She made it small and she wrote it down.

By eight she had the eleven books logged in a fresh sheet: author, title, publisher, year, shelf position where she could work it out from the gaps, page opened to, and the note. She photographed each open page. She numbered the books one to eleven going along the arc, and she gave the outlier number twelve in her own head and then corrected it to eleven with an asterisk, because she was already worrying about the difference between what she had found and what she had decided.

The notes on the eleven read:

*she came in at ten past and she was not alone*

*two coats on the peg and only one of them went out*

*he was in the shop until twenty to and then he was not*

*the top road takes 22 minutes at a walk, I have done it*

*W. rang three times on the Tuesday*

*I gave her the key because she asked me for the key*

*I said I would leave the side light on*

*she was frightened of him in June and not in October and that is the wrong way round*

*the register would say and there is no register*

*after this there is nothing more to write down*

*I am not going to be believed and that is not the reason*

Clara read the last two several times.

She got up, and her knees hurt, and she went along the shelves looking for the gaps where the eleven had come from, and this was where the morning stopped being an inventory and became something else.

There were no gaps.

She went round the room twice. Every run of oak shelving was full and even and upright, with a finger of air at the end where the finger of air had always been. Nowhere in six hundred books was there a slot two inches wide waiting for a volume to go back into it, and nowhere was there a run that had slumped sideways the way a run slumps when you take a book out of the middle of it.

Which meant that the eleven had not come off these shelves. Which meant that they had come from somewhere else in the building, or that something had closed the shelves up behind them, and Clara stood in the middle of the floor and made herself hold both of those possibilities without choosing, because choosing was how you got things wrong.

Then she noticed the dust.

The floorboards in that room had a fine grey film on them, seven months thick, undisturbed except where she had been walking since last night, and her own prints were perfectly clear in it, and the eleven books sat in the film with a clean margin of board around each one.

No drag marks. No scuffs. Nothing had slid.

And no other footprints at all.

Clara went downstairs and made coffee in the back room with hands that were not quite steady and were steady enough, and drank it standing up, and then she opened the door onto Church Lane to let the morning in.

Priya was outside her own shop two doors down, wrestling a chalkboard.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Eleven books," said Clara.

Priya let go of the chalkboard. It fell over.

They went and sat in the coffee place before it opened, in the window, with the machine warming up behind them and the lane going about its business outside, which at half past eight in Fenn Abbas consisted of a van and a woman with a dog.

Clara told it plainly, in order, with the photographs on the table between them. The turning she had heard on Thursday evening, an hour after she arrived, before she had ever been up those stairs. The turning again at ten past three last night. The closed door she had left open. The arc. The dust. The absence of gaps in the shelving. The bus ticket.

Priya listened all the way through without saying anything, which Clara had not expected and was grateful for.

"The same number," she said at the end.

"The same number."

"Eleven in March and eleven last night."

"Different books," Clara said. "That's the thing I keep going round. I asked you yesterday and you said you didn't look at what they were, you looked at Edwin. But you'd have seen if they were in an arc."

"They were in a line," said Priya.

Clara stopped with her cup halfway.

"Say that again."

"A line." Priya was looking at the table. "I've not thought about it in six months and I've thought about it every week since March, if that makes sense. I came up because the shop was shut on a Tuesday and Edwin had never once shut on a Tuesday, and I had the spare key, and I found him in the back and I rang the ambulance and then while I was waiting I went up, because I could hear the wind and I thought a window. And they were on the floor in the front room, in a line, along the boards, going towards the middle window. Not a heap. A line. I remember thinking somebody's been sorting."

"Going towards the middle window."

"Yes."

Clara put the phone on the table and turned it round and showed her the photograph from the door, and Priya looked at the arc of ten books and the eleventh sitting outside the curve, and traced the shape with one finger without touching the screen, and stopped where the finger ran off the edge of the arc.

"That's the same direction," she said.

"It's the same direction."

They sat with that while the coffee machine came up to pressure and hissed.

"I want to say something and I want you to let me say all of it," Priya said. "I don't believe in ghosts. My gran believed in about nine things and I believe in none of them and I have never once in this town said anything different, because you say a thing like that here and it goes round in a day. So I am not saying that."

"All right."

"But Edwin Pryor was in that room every night." Priya turned her cup. "Every night, Clara. I close at five and I do the till and I'm out at half five, and from about November through to March you can see the upstairs front window from my back step, and there was a light on in that room every night for the six years I've had the shop, and it went off between one and two in the morning. An eighty year old man. Every night."

"Doing what?"

"I assumed reading."

"He was writing," said Clara.

"In the margins."

"In the margins of six hundred books that he wouldn't sell and that aren't worth anything, in pencil, on the left hand page, one note per book." Clara spread her hands flat on the table. "Priya, I have handled a lot of damaged documents. When somebody breaks a thing up and hides the pieces in different places, they are doing one of two things. Either they are trying to make sure nobody can ever put it together."

"Or?"

"Or they are trying to make sure that it survives whoever comes looking for it." She looked out of the window at the lane. "You can burn a notebook. You'd have to burn a building."

Priya went and got the milk out of the fridge and did not use it and put it back.

"The ticket," she said. "The fourth of November ninety six."

"Does that date mean anything to you?"

"No," said Priya. "But I'll tell you who it'll mean something to, and she's in here at ten past nine every day of her life for a pot of tea and a slice, and she's been coming to this lane since 1958."

The bell over the coffee shop door went at eleven minutes past nine.

"Morning, Het," said Priya.

"You've got the girl from the Ledger sitting in your window," said Hettie Boon, who was eighty eight years old and four feet eleven inches tall and had come in out of the wet with a shopping trolley and an expression of frank and total interest, "and you haven't offered her anything to eat. Sit down, love, you look like a fortnight of rain. I'm Hettie. I've got five books on hold at your shop and I've had them on hold since March and I'd like them, please, because I've been reading the same Dick Francis since the funeral and I'm sick to death of horses."
