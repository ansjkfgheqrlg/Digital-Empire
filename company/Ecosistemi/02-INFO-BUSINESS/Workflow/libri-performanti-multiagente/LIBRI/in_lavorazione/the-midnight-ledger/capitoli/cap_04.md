# The Second Night

There was no hotel in Fenn Abbas. There was a pub with two rooms, both taken by a wedding party from Shaftesbury, and a bed and breakfast four miles out at Hurstock that answered the phone and quoted eighty five pounds, and Clara had looked at her banking app on Thursday evening and then at the neatly made single bed in the back room of her own building and made the sensible decision.

Thursday night she had slept in her coat on top of the blankets, badly, and nothing at all had happened, and she had woken at seven convinced that a house makes any noise you ask it to make.

On Friday, after Amory left, she did it properly. She washed the sheets in the sink because there was no machine, and hung them over the stove, and they were dry by nine, and she remade the bed with the blanket folded at the foot exactly where it had been because it seemed rude to do otherwise. She put the paperback that was open at page ninety on the shelf by the stove, still open, still face down. She had the second tin of soup.

Then she sat at the counter with her laptop and worked, because working was the thing she did when she did not want to think, and she was extremely good at it.

By eleven she had a proper spreadsheet. Ground floor: fourteen shelf runs, an estimated seven thousand four hundred volumes, trade value somewhere between two and three thousand pounds if a dealer took the lot and cleared it himself, which was the only way anybody ever bought eight thousand books. First floor front: six hundred and change, value negligible, notes required.

She looked at the last line for a while. *Notes required.*

At half past eleven she made a second spreadsheet, and gave it three columns, and named them BOOK, PAGE, TEXT.

She had eight rows in it by midnight. She had gone up with a pencil and a notepad and worked along the shelf nearest the door, and she had opened forty one books to find eight notes, and the notes were these:

*she did not have a coat with her*

*13 minutes is not enough time*

*not the third one, the third one is wrong*

*ask her about the lamp*

*he says he walked back along the top road*

*the second time it was raining as well*

*W. does not know and must not*

*nobody checked the register*

Clara read them down the screen half a dozen times.

They were not marginalia. Marginalia argue with the book. These did not touch the book: the coat note was in a volume about canal boats, the thirteen minutes note was beside a photograph of a rood screen, the lamp note was in a Dick Francis. She checked whether they might be tracking pages, or chapters, or dates of publication, and they were not doing any of those things either. The only pattern she could find was that all eight were on the left hand page, and that the pencil was the same grade throughout, and that the hand did not change over what she was fairly sure was a great many years, because in two of them the graphite had oxidised to a silvery grey and in two others it was still black.

They were also, all eight, fragments of one thing.

She could not have defended that in an argument. But she had spent eleven years handling documents that had been broken up, and a person who does that work develops a sense for it, the way a person who mends pots can tell from one sherd whether it belongs to a bowl. The eight notes had the same voice, the same tense, the same interest. They were about a person, or people. Somebody had walked back along the top road and said so. Somebody had not had a coat. Somebody called W. did not know something.

Nobody checked the register.

At half past twelve she gave up, turned off the laptop, went into the back room and lay down in Edwin Pryor's bed with her clothes on and the light off, and listened to the building.

Amory had been right about one thing at least. The house talked. There was a tick from the stove as it cooled, at intervals of about four minutes, then five, then seven. There was a settling somewhere above the ceiling that came twice and did not come again. Wind got into the lane and pushed at the shop door and the bell moved without ringing, which was worse than ringing.

She fell asleep some time after one, and woke at ten past three, all at once, completely.

The shop was silent.

She lay still. She had been woken by something and she waited to be woken by it again, the way you do, and the stove ticked, and the wind did not, and nothing else happened for a length of time she afterwards estimated at two minutes and which felt considerably longer.

Then, upstairs, a page turned.

It was not the building. Clara had by then spent nine hours in the building and had catalogued its noises with the same attention she gave to everything. This came from directly above the shop floor, it was dry and light and specific, and it was followed by another after an interval, and another, and the intervals were not regular the way a mechanical thing is regular. They were the intervals of somebody reading who is going slowly, and then faster, and then stopping to look at something.

Clara got up.

She would ask herself about this too, later. What she remembered was that she was frightened in her body, hands cold, heart going hard and high in her chest, and not at all frightened in her mind, and that the two things ran alongside each other all the way across the shop floor without ever meeting.

She did not turn on the shop light. There was enough coming through the front windows from the one lamp at the churchyard gate to show her the aisles in grey. She went to the bottom of the stairs and put her hand on the panelling.

The turning stopped.

"All right," Clara said, to the dark at the top of the stairs. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I'm coming up."

She went up sideways past the books on the treads, and at the top she pulled the light cord and the landing bulb came on, and the door to the front room was shut, and she was completely certain she had left it open.

She opened it and reached round the frame for the switch.

The room was empty. The three blinds were down, level as they always were. The oak shelves stood along the crooked walls.

And on the floor, in a rough arc that started near the door and curved away towards the middle window, lay eleven books, face up, open.

Clara stood in the doorway with her hand still on the light switch and did not go in for quite a long time.

They had not fallen. That was the first thing and it was not a matter of opinion, it was a matter of physics, and she stood there and made herself go through it properly because the alternative was to feel things. Books that fall off a shelf land in a heap under that shelf. They land closed, or open at the natural break of the binding, and they land spine down more often than not because the spine is the heavy edge. These were spread across four feet of floor in a curve, none of them within two feet of a shelf, all of them open, all of them face up, and the nearest one was lying across the join of two floorboards with its pages perfectly flat, which is a thing a book does when it is put down and not when it arrives.

There was no draught. The blinds hung dead still.

"Eleven," Clara said.

Priya's voice, from yesterday, in the doorway with the tray in her hands. *Every window in this shop was shut and every book in the upstairs front room was on the floor. No. That's the bit I don't like. Eleven of them.*

Clara crouched at the edge of the arc, not touching, and looked at the nearest book, and it was open at pages one hundred and eight and one hundred and nine, and in the margin of the left hand page, level with the second line, in small square pencil, it said:

*she came in at ten past and she was not alone*

She looked up at the arc of them, eleven books lying open on the floorboards of a room whose shelves nobody had been allowed to touch for thirty one years, and she understood two things in the same second.

The first was that she was not going to be catching a train in nine days.

The second was that the shape the books made on the floor was not random either, and that she should photograph it before she moved anything, and she went down for her phone and came back up and did exactly that, four shots from the door and one from each corner, because whatever else was happening here, the woman it was happening to had spent eleven years recording the state of things before she touched them.

It was only when she was looking at the photographs afterwards, sitting on the bottom stair with the shop grey around her and the sky over the churchyard just starting to go from black to something else, that she noticed the arc had a gap in it.

Ten books made a curve. The eleventh sat a foot outside it, on its own, at the point where the curve would have carried on if it had carried on.

It was the only one of the eleven with a bookmark in it.
