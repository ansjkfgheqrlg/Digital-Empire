# The Constable

The house went quiet and stayed quiet.

Clara noticed it the way you notice a fridge you have lived with for years after somebody unplugs it. She slept eight hours on the Thursday night and nine on the Friday, and nothing turned a page above her head, and no book was on the floor in the morning, and on the Saturday she found herself standing in the doorway of the upstairs front room at eleven at night saying, "You can, if you want to," to a room full of books, and feeling extremely stupid, and going back down.

She told Priya about it in the coffee shop on the Sunday.

"Well," said Priya. "It got what it wanted."

"That's an awful way to put it."

"Is it? He spent thirty years trying to get one fact to one person and he couldn't do it while he was alive, and now it's done." Priya wiped the table. "I'd shut up too."

"He didn't just want me to know who my mother was."

"No," Priya agreed. "But you can't do the rest without knowing that first, can you. He couldn't hand you the second half of it while you were still packing up to sell."

Okafor rang on the Monday morning.

"Are you sitting down."

"I'm on a ladder."

"Get off the ladder." A pause, and paper moving. "I have found the constable."

Clara got off the ladder.

"His name is Raymond Godsall. He was twenty three in 1996, he did nine years and then went to the prison service, and he lives in Weymouth and he has a caravan and a dog and he answered his own front door on Saturday morning and said, before I had got my warrant card all the way out, *is this about the girl in the churchyard*."

"He remembered."

"Miss Vance, he had been waiting thirty years." Okafor's voice was flat in a way that Clara had learned meant the opposite of calm. "He remembered the name Hettie Boon. He remembered Sheepwash Row. He remembered that she was sixty and that she had walked to Sherborne from the bus and that she had waited fifty minutes in the front office, and he remembered writing it in his pocket book, which is what a probationer does, and he remembered handing it up, and I believe every word of it."

"So where is it?"

"Pocket books from 1996 were returned to the force and destroyed on a six year cycle. It is gone and it was always going to be gone and that is not sinister, that is a filing policy." Okafor took a breath. "What I asked him next was why it never went anywhere. And I want to read you what he said, because I wrote it down and I have thought about nothing else since.

"He said: *I gave it to the sergeant and the sergeant said the file was going to the coroner as an accident, and that a statement like that would confuse it, and that the lady was sixty and it was dark and she'd have us digging up the whole town. And I said should I not type it up anyway, and he said, son, if you type it up somebody has to disprove it.*"

Clara sat down on the second stair.

"That's not a cover up," Okafor said. "That is the single most ordinary sentence in policing and I have heard versions of it in this decade. A man with forty jobs on decided which one this was in the first six hours and then defended the decision, and everything after that was tidying. It is worse than a cover up, in a way, because there is nobody to charge for it."

"Is the sergeant alive?"

"He died in 2011. Before you ask: I have his personnel file and there is nothing in it. He was, by every account, a perfectly decent officer who was running a division on nothing."

"So we've lost it."

"We have lost the statement," said Okafor, "and we have gained something I did not go there for, and this is the reason I am ringing you instead of writing it up first.

"Godsall was the first officer at the scene on the morning of the fifth. He was on early turn at Sherborne, the call came in at seven twenty, he was there by seven fifty. And I asked him to walk me through his arrival, and he said: *I parked on the lane and there was a man at the churchyard gate waiting for me, and he took me up the path.*"

Clara did not move.

"The file," Okafor said, "records that the deceased was found by *a parishioner*. Two words. No name, no statement, no contact details, no account of the discovery. I have read those four pages eleven times and I have flagged that omission in every review note I have written since February, and it is the single worst thing in the file, and I could not do anything about it because there was nobody to ask."

"Who was at the gate?"

"Godsall didn't know the name. It was his first month on the division." A beat. "He described him. Thirties, tall, well spoken, in a waxed jacket, and he had the church keys in his hand, and he said he had come to unlock at seven and had seen her from the path. And he stayed with Godsall by the body until the sergeant arrived, and he was, in Godsall's words, *very calm and very helpful, and he told me not to walk on the grass because of the frost*."

Clara found that her hand had gone to her mouth again.

"Gerald Amory was sacristan of St Botolph's from 1994," said Okafor. "I checked it on Saturday afternoon with the diocesan office and he held the office until 2016 and he unlocked that church at seven o'clock every morning for twenty two years."

"He found her."

"Somebody found her, and the file does not say who, and a retired prison officer in Weymouth has just described a man who had the keys." Okafor's voice sharpened. "Now hear the rest of it, because this is the part that matters and it is not the part that feels good.

"If Gerald Amory found her, that is not evidence of anything at all. Sacristans find things. He unlocked that church every morning for twenty two years and on one of those mornings there was a dead woman on the path, and if he had never gone near this case again nobody would ever have thought twice about it. Being the person who finds a body is the most innocent thing in the world and it is also the most useful, and both of those are true at once and a jury has to hold both.

"What it does is this. It means that from seven o'clock on the morning of the fifth of November 1996 until the sergeant arrived, a man who by our account had been in that churchyard at a quarter past eleven the night before was standing on the scene, alone at first, in charge of it, telling a twenty three year old probationer where to walk."

"Thirteen minutes is not enough time," Clara said.

"Say that again."

"It's one of the notes. It's one of the first eleven, from the floor, the first night." Clara was already moving, through the shop, up the stairs, with the phone against her ear. "*13 minutes is not enough time.* I've had it in a spreadsheet for six weeks and I read it as being about the walk up the top road and it doesn't fit, because that's twenty two minutes, and Edwin says so twice, and I could never make thirteen mean anything."

She got the printout off the floor of the front room and found the line.

"Seven thirty seven," she said. "If Godsall got the call at seven twenty and arrived at seven fifty, and Amory rang it in at, what, seven o'clock, quarter past."

"Seven eighteen," said Okafor. "It's the one time in the whole file I trust, because it came off a control room log."

"Then Amory was alone with her for eighteen minutes before he called, and thirty two minutes after." Clara's voice was not steady. "And Edwin wrote *13 minutes is not enough time* in the margin of a book about English churches, and hid it in a room nobody could get into, and I want to know what he thought thirteen minutes was not enough time to do."

There was a silence on the line.

"I'll tell you what I would want thirteen minutes for," said Nadia Okafor at last, "if I were a frightened man standing on a frosty path at seven in the morning looking at somebody I had left there in the dark. I would want to walk the ground and pick up anything of mine that was on it."

"Her bag," said Clara.

"There is no bag in the file," said Okafor. "There is no property list at all. There is no coat, either, and it was minus two that night, and I have had *where is her coat* written on a whiteboard in Winfrith since March."

"Her coat was on the peg," Clara said. "In my shop. That's note number three."
