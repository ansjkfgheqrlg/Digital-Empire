# The Offer

He came at eleven the next morning, which was exactly the day's delay Priya had predicted, and Clara found that she had been listening for the bell since nine.

She had spent the morning being sensible. She had wiped down the counter, washed the cup and set it upside down on the drainer, opened the two windows that would open, and started a spreadsheet on her laptop with columns for section, shelf run, estimated units and estimated trade value. She had got as far as the local history and put in a figure that was probably wrong, and she had not gone upstairs, and the not going upstairs had taken up rather more of her attention than the spreadsheet.

The bell rang. A man came in, and took his hat off, which nobody had done in a shop Clara was standing in for at least a decade.

"Miss Vance. Gerald Amory. I'd have come yesterday but I thought you'd want a night to look at it."

He was in his sixties, broad, well kept, in a green quilted jacket over a shirt and a knitted tie, and his handshake was dry and brief and he did not hold on. He had the particular ease of a man who is known everywhere within four miles of where he is standing.

"You're the shop on the square," Clara said.

"Third generation, God help me." He looked around the room, and he did it properly, taking his time, in a way she recognised because it was how she looked at rooms. "He kept it well, didn't he. People assume a shop like this is a tip and it never was. Edwin was a good bookseller in a town that stopped wanting one."

"You knew him."

"Forty years." Amory turned back to her. "Not well. That's not modesty, it's a fact. I don't think anybody knew Edwin well. But I bought his overflow twice a year for twenty five years, and I carried his coffin in March, and there were six of us and two were from the undertaker."

That landed somewhere Clara had not braced.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'm at a bit of a disadvantage. I never met him."

"No," Amory said. "I know."

There was a small pause, one beat too long, and Clara heard it and filed it and did not know why.

"So," he said, brisk again. "You'll want to know what I want, because I'm not here about the coffin. I'd like to buy it."

"The shop."

"The freehold, the fixtures and the stock. All of it, as it stands, cleared by me. You wouldn't lift a book." He said it without any salesman's warmth, which was, Clara thought later, the cleverest thing he did all morning. "I'll give you a number and you'll want to check it and you should. Ready?"

"Go on."

"Two hundred and forty thousand."

Clara kept her face still, which took some doing. She had looked at the Dorset property pages on her phone at seven that morning, sitting on Edwin Pryor's bed with the blanket round her shoulders, and a crooked three storey unmodernised commercial premises on a back lane in a town like this was worth, on a good day, with a following wind, something in the region of a hundred and eighty.

"That's over the market," she said.

"It is." He seemed pleased that she knew. "About fifty over. I'll tell you why, because you'll work it out anyway and then you'll wonder what else I'm not saying. Church Lane is dying. Two premises, one shut. Priya Raval's place hangs on because she's good, and if this stays boarded she'll go in two years, and then that's a lane with nothing on it, and the town has three empty units on the square already. I'm the chairman of the book fair and I've been on the chamber for eleven years and I have to look at that every day. I want it lived in. I'd put a manager in and run it as an annexe, antiquarian and county history, open Thursday to Sunday. That's worth fifty grand of my money and it's worth it to me and not to anybody else, which is why you won't get this number twice."

It was, Clara thought, an extremely good speech.

"And the stock?"

"Cleared and dispersed. Some of it into my shop, most of it to the trade, the rest to the charity people in Sherborne. It's the part that would take you six weeks and it takes me a phone call."

"All of it?"

"All of it."

"Including upstairs."

Gerald Amory's face did not change, and his hands did not move, and he did not look at the ceiling.

"Including upstairs," he said.

Clara let it sit.

"I went up last night," she said. "There's nothing up there. Book club editions. Condensed novels. Not one of them priced. Do you know why he did that?"

"No."

"You bought his overflow for twenty five years. You never asked?"

"I asked him twice." Amory smiled slightly. "The second time was in about 1998 and he told me it was a private library and that if I asked again he would stop selling me his overflow. Which he would have done, and I'd have lost a good line, so I didn't ask again. That's the whole of my knowledge of the upstairs room at the Ledger, and I'd guess it's more than anybody else has got."

"1998."

"About then."

"Not 1996?"

She had not planned to say it. It came out flat, an ordinary date in an ordinary sentence, and she watched him.

He blinked once.

"Could have been," he said. "It's thirty years, Miss Vance. I couldn't tell you what month I asked, let alone what year, and if I gave you one you shouldn't believe me."

Which was, she thought, a perfectly reasonable thing for a person to say. It was also the only sentence he had spoken since he came in that was not precise.

"There's a condition," he said. "There always is. I need it done by the end of next week. Not because I'm pressuring you. Because I've got a bridging facility that expires on the thirtieth and if I lose it I'm buying nothing until the spring, and I'd rather tell you that now than have you find out in six weeks that I've gone cold."

"Nine days."

"Nine days to say yes. The lawyers can take as long as they like after that." He put his hat back on. "You'll want to talk to a solicitor and you'll want to talk to a valuer, and I'd recommend Vokins in Sherborne for the second one because he'll tell you I'm over the odds, which is what you need to hear from somebody who isn't me. Think about it in the cold. Two hundred and forty, cleared, and you're on a train."

He went to the door, and then, with his hand on it, he turned back, and for the first time all morning he looked at the ceiling.

"Miss Vance. A thing you should know, since nobody else will tell you." He said it kindly. "It's the churchyard. Three windows on the front and every one of them looks at it, and there's a lane between and nothing else, and this house has been standing in that view since 1740. People sleep badly in here. Edwin slept in the back room for the last twenty years of his life and he'd tell you it was the stove and it wasn't the stove. You'll hear the building. It's a crooked house on a slope with a hundred and fifty tons of paper in it and paper moves with the damp, and at three in the morning that sounds like all sorts."

"Pages turning," Clara said.

"Among other things," said Gerald Amory. "Nine days," and the bell rang and he was gone.

Clara stood where she was for a full minute.

Then she went to the door and looked down the lane, and he was thirty yards along it, walking towards the square, and he stopped level with the churchyard gate. He did not go in. He stood with his back to the wall for perhaps four seconds, looking at nothing, the way a man stands when he is about to make a phone call and has not taken his phone out yet.

Then he walked on.

Priya came out of the coffee place at half past twelve with two toasties and no invitation.

"Well?"

"Two hundred and forty."

Priya sat down heavily on the arm of the chair. "That's over."

"About fifty over. He said so himself before I could."

"That's Gerald." She said it without warmth. "He'll tell you the true thing that helps him. He's not a liar, that's the trouble with him. You can check every single thing he says and it all comes back right and you still walk out of there having agreed to something."

"He wants the stock cleared. All of it."

"Course he does. Who wants eight thousand books."

"Priya." Clara put her toastie down. "He never once looked up. He was in here twenty minutes talking about the stock and he never looked at the ceiling, not when he said upstairs, not when I said it. And then he did it deliberately on the way out, and made a whole speech about the building settling and how people sleep badly in here."

"So?"

"So people look up at ceilings. It's what people do." Clara turned her cup around on the counter. "And when I asked him if it was 1996 that Edwin stopped selling from that room, he told me he couldn't possibly remember the year, and then he told me not to believe him if he gave me one."

Priya had gone quite still.

"Why did you say 1996?"

"Because you did. Yesterday. You said your mum reckons ninety six, the winter." Clara looked up. "Priya, what happened here in the winter of 1996?"

And Priya Raval, who had been born in 1997, said, "I don't know," and it was the first thing anybody in Fenn Abbas had said to Clara that was completely and obviously true and completely and obviously incomplete, because she said it while looking straight out of the window at the churchyard.
