# Thirty-Two Lines

Danni Ellis did the cold read on the Wednesday and it took her nine hours, and on the Thursday afternoon Okafor came back to Fenn Abbas with two printouts in a folder and put them face down on the counter.

"Yours first," she said. "Out loud, from memory, no notes. What happened that night."

"You want me to guess."

"I want you to do exactly what I told you not to do a week ago, now that it can't contaminate anything, because if you and Danni built the same night out of the same thirty two lines separately then that is worth something and if you didn't I need to know before I take it any further."

Clara put her hands in her pockets.

"She worked Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays," she said. "The fourth was a Monday. She was in the shop that day.

"At some point in the autumn she asked Edwin to arrange something for her, and he did, and he arranged it for that night, in the churchyard, at eleven, because there is a note that says *because I told her he would come*, and there is one that says *I said I would leave the side light on*, and there is one that says *he took the lamp glass out and put it back and I did not think anything of it at the time*.

"So Edwin promised her the gate lamp would be on. Somebody took the glass out of that lantern in the shop, in front of him, and did something to it, and put it back, and at eleven o'clock that night the lamp was off, and Hettie Boon walked down the lane with her hand out for the wall.

"Ruth left the shop at ten to eleven and left her coat on the peg, because there is a note about two coats and one of them going out, and because she expected to be five minutes. He came at ten past. Hettie heard two people talking quickly and quietly by the yew at about a quarter past.

"And at twenty two minutes to midnight a man came back into that shop on his own, and Edwin Pryor wrote the time down thirteen years later in the margin of a book about the county constabulary."

She stopped.

"And that," she said, "is everything I can honestly get out of it, and it is not enough for anything, because I cannot tell you what happened between eleven ten and eleven thirty two, and neither could Edwin, because he was in here."

Okafor turned the first printout over.

Danni Ellis had built the same night. Not the same words: she had it as a numbered chronology with confidence ratings and she had marked three of Clara's steps as inference rather than statement, and she had one detail Clara had not caught, because it was in a note Clara had read forty times without seeing it.

*item 19 (bk 244, p.88): "he asked me for paper and an envelope and he could not find a pen." Assessed: places subject inside premises, post-event, in a state of agitation, requiring writing materials. Note: no envelope or letter recovered.*

"He came back for paper," Clara said.

"He came back for paper at twenty two minutes to midnight," said Okafor, "having, on his own later account, gone home along the top road."

"His account."

Okafor turned over the second printout, and this one was not from Danni Ellis. It was a photocopy of a photocopy, four pages, and the typeface was a 1996 police typewriter.

"That is the whole file," she said. "I have shown it to you now because you have given me your version and it can't be unsaid. Read the third page."

Clara read.

*STATEMENT OF WITNESS. Gerald Charles AMORY, b. 14.3.63, bookseller, of Amory & Sons, Market Square, Fenn Abbas.*

*I knew Ruth Feltham to say hello to as she worked at another bookshop in the town. On Monday 4 November I travelled to Dorchester in the afternoon for a meeting of the book fair committee at the Antelope. The meeting broke up at about 10.15pm. I stayed in Dorchester at the home of Mr P. Yeo, a committee member, as I have done before, and returned to Fenn Abbas by bus on the Tuesday morning arriving about 9.30am, when I learned what had happened.*

Clara read it twice and then a third time, and put it down on the counter with great care, because her hands wanted to do something else with it.

"He was in the shop," she said. "At half past eleven at night, in Fenn Abbas, asking for an envelope."

"That is what a pencil note in a book says," said Okafor, "written by a man who is dead, thirteen years after the event, hidden in a board. And that is worth precisely nothing on its own, and I want you to hold on to that, because the next ten seconds are the most dangerous part of this entire case."

"There's the ticket."

"There is the ticket," Okafor agreed. "Say it properly."

Clara went behind the counter and brought out the two polythene sleeves and laid them side by side on the wood, and looked at them for a moment before she spoke, because she had known since Sunday what they were and she had wanted to be wrong.

"Two bus tickets. Wilts and Dorset. Machine dated the fourth of November 1996. One out, Fenn Abbas to Dorchester. One back, Dorchester to Fenn Abbas."

"And?"

"And if he stayed the night at Mr Yeo's house in Dorchester, as he told a police officer in writing in November 1996, then there is no return ticket for the fourth of November, because he did not come back on the fourth of November." Clara looked up. "It exists. It's in my shop. It was in the boards of a book with a note against it that says *he was not in Dorchester*."

Nadia Okafor stood with her hands on the counter and looked at the two small pieces of card for a long time.

"Right," she said quietly. "Now I have got something."

Then she straightened up and became a police officer again, quite visibly, like a person putting a coat back on.

"Which I will now spend six weeks trying to break, because that is the job. Tickets are transferable. Anybody can carry anybody's ticket, and a good barrister will suggest that Edwin Pryor, who hated Gerald Amory for thirty years for reasons we cannot establish, put a bus ticket in a book. So: the ticket goes to the lab for whatever they can get off it after thirty years, which is probably nothing. Mr Yeo, if alive, gets a visit. The Antelope's function book, if it survives, gets found. And the whole of this comes down, in the end, to whether anybody can put Gerald Amory in this town between eleven and midnight on that night by any route that does not run through a dead man's handwriting."

"Hettie," said Clara.

"Hettie Boon saw two shapes at thirty yards in the dark and cannot say if they were men or women, and told a constable in 1996, and it is not in this file." Okafor tapped the four pages. "There is no statement from her. There is no note of a statement. There is no record of a visit from a member of the public. That is the second thing I want to look at and it is going to take me somewhere I would rather not go."

She gathered the sleeves.

"And the third thing," she said, "is the photograph, which I have not mentioned all afternoon and which you have not asked about once, which I have found extremely interesting."

Clara did not say anything.

"I had it looked at," said Okafor. "It's a June 1994 print, which we know from the processing code on the back edge, so the child is somewhere between eighteen months and two and a half. The woman is Ruth Feltham; her father has confirmed that from a copy without being told where it came from, which I have on record. And the child is not on any file anywhere, Miss Vance, because Ruth Feltham did not have a child, and everybody in this town would tell you so, and her father told my colleague so in 1996 and again three weeks ago."

The shop was very quiet.

"When's your birthday?" said Okafor.

"The ninth of March 1992."

"And where were you born?"

"Taunton," Clara said. "I think. It's what's on my short certificate. I was adopted at four and I have never once looked, because my parents were good to me and it never seemed to matter, and because every time I nearly did I found something else to do instead."

She heard herself say it and heard how it sounded.

"I have spent five weeks," she said slowly, "reading a dead man's handwriting in a house I inherited for no reason, and I never once ordered a copy of my own birth certificate."

"No," said Nadia Okafor, not unkindly. "You didn't. Why do you suppose that is?"
