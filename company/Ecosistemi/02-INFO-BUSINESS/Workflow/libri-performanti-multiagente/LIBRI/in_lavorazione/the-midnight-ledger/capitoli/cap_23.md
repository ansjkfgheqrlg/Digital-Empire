# The Eighteenth

They opened the grave at six in the morning on a Thursday in the middle of December, behind screens, in the dark, with four lights on stands and a tent over the plot and a police officer at each gate.

Clara stood on the path with Wallace Feltham on one side of her and Nadia Okafor on the other, and Tom Sayers stood at the head of the plot in a surplice in the cold and said the words that the Church of England provides for this, which are not many and are not comforting and are the only ones there are.

Wallace was on police bail with a condition not to enter The Midnight Ledger, and no condition at all about the churchyard, because Okafor had gone to a superintendent on the Tuesday and said, in writing, that she would not stand between a man of seventy seven and his daughter for the sake of tidiness.

He held Clara's arm the whole time, quite hard.

"You've got her hands," he said, once.

It took four hours. At half past ten a woman from the forensic archaeology unit came out from behind the screen with a sealed box and walked it to a van, and that was the whole of it, and there was nothing to see, which Okafor had promised him and which turned out to be true.

What Clara had not been warned about was the town.

By eight o'clock there were perhaps forty people standing in the lane below the churchyard wall, in the dark, in the cold, not talking much. Nobody had organised it. Priya had opened at six and had given away, by her own later count, a hundred and nine cups of tea, and had refused to take money from anyone, and had shut at eleven having lost about two hundred pounds and being entirely unrepentant about it.

Hettie Boon stood at the gate for the whole four hours with a stick and would not sit on the chair somebody fetched her.

"I was here in ninety six," she said, when Clara went down. "Nobody was here in ninety six. That's the bit I've never got over. Twenty two years old and it was a Tuesday and everybody went to work."

At the end of it Tom Sayers stood at the gate and shook the hand of every single person who was still there, forty one hands, and did not say anything to any of them, and Clara watched a man do the only part of his job that could not be done by paperwork.

The results came back on the ninth of January.

Foetal remains recovered. Sufficient material for a profile. A full paternal profile, obtained by subtraction, complete and unambiguous, and it matched the sample Gerald Amory had provided voluntarily in November when he was arrested, in a police station in Bournemouth, having been advised by his solicitor that refusing would be worse.

"It proves he is the father," said Okafor, on the phone, at eight in the morning. "That is all it proves and I want to say that before you say anything, because it is going to be reported as though it proves he killed her and it does not, and if you say the wrong sentence to the wrong reporter in the next fortnight you will be helping him."

"What does it do, then?"

"It kills the last version of the story where he is a man who barely knew her." Okafor's voice was steady and Clara could hear that it was costing her something. "As of nine o'clock this morning, Gerald Amory is a man who was in a sexual relationship with the deceased, who lied about it in a signed statement to police, who was in the churchyard with her at the material time, who destroyed a parish record of his own movements, who found her body and was alone with it for eighteen minutes, and who is the father of the child she was carrying. Every single one of those is now a proved fact and not an allegation."

"And the letter."

"And the letter." A pause. "The letter is the only document in this case written by Ruth Feltham. It establishes that she asked Edwin Pryor to arrange a meeting with a specific man at a specific time on a specific night, and it establishes what she was going to say to him, and it is in her hand, and it is dated, and the handwriting has been examined by two people who have never met each other."

"Is it enough?"

"It went to the Crown Prosecution Service on Monday," said Nadia Okafor. "I am not permitted to tell you what I think, and I am going to tell you what I think, because you have earned it and because I am so tired.

"They will not charge murder. There is no evidence of intention and there never will be, because the only two people on that path are one dead and one saying nothing.

"They will charge manslaughter, and they will charge perverting the course of justice for the register and for the statement, and the second one will stick like glue and the first one is a coin. And a jury will hear that a man of thirty three met a pregnant twenty two year old in a dark churchyard at his own request, that she died there, and that he then spent thirty years being a churchwarden, and juries are not machines."

They charged him on the twenty second of January.

Clara did not see it. She had gone back to Bristol for four days to give up a flat she had not slept in since October, and she was standing in an empty room with a bin bag in her hand when Priya rang and read her the front page of the Echo down the telephone.

She read the whole thing out, including the photograph caption, and then she said, "Are you there?"

"I'm here."

"Say something."

"There's nothing to say," said Clara. "It's the twenty second of January and Ruth Feltham has been dead for twenty nine years and two months and a piece of paper has changed and that's the whole of it, and it doesn't bring anybody back, and everybody knows that, and we did it anyway."

"Clara."

"I know." She sat down on the floor of an empty flat in Bristol with a bin bag beside her. "I know. I'm glad. I just can't feel it yet."

There was one more thing, and it came in February, and it did not come from the police.

Miss Deare rang from Dorchester, in the voice of a woman who had been waiting nine months to make a telephone call.

"You asked me in October whether we had acted for Mr Pryor in anything other than his will," she said. "I said I would look. I have looked, at some length, and I have taken advice, and I am now able to tell you that in February 2026 Mr Pryor also lodged a sealed envelope with this firm, with an instruction that it be opened only upon a specified event."

Clara stood very still in the middle of her shop.

"What event?"

"The instruction reads: *upon any person being charged in connection with the death of Ruth Anne Feltham.*" A rustle of paper. "That condition was satisfied on the twenty second of January. I have opened it in the presence of a colleague and I am obliged to tell you that its contents are addressed to you and that a copy has already gone to Dorset Police, because the instruction required that too."

"What is it?"

"It is one page," said Miss Deare, "and I am going to read it to you, and then I am going to put it in the post, and Miss Vance, I have been a solicitor for thirty one years."

*To whoever got to the end.*

*If you are reading this then somebody has been charged and it is over, and I am not there, and both of those are correct and were always going to be.*

*I want to put one thing down plainly because the rest of what I have left you is evidence and evidence is not the same as an account.*

*I sent her out there. She asked me to arrange it and I arranged it and I was pleased to be asked. I turned the side light on for her at half past ten and it was off at eleven and I did not go and look, because he had taken the glass out that afternoon in front of me and I thought he was being helpful, and I have had thirty years to decide whether I believed that at the time and the answer is that I did, entirely, and that this is worse.*

*I did not go to the police because she asked me not to and because of the child. That is the truth and it is also a thing I have hidden behind, and both are true at once. A braver man would have found a way to do both. I found a way to do neither for a very long time and then I found this way, which is slow and stupid and which took me the rest of my life, and it is the best I could manage.*

*I have not been unhappy. People will say I was. I had a shop and a town and a Friday customer and thirty years of a job that needed doing, and there are men who get less.*

*Do not keep the room shut. That was mine and there is no reason for it to be anybody else's. Sell the books. They were only ever paper.*

*The light is on. It always was, except once.*

*E. Pryor.*
