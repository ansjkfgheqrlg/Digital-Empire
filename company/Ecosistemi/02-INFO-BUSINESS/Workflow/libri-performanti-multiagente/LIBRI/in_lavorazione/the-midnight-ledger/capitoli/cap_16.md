# Wallace

Wallace Feltham lived at the top of Sheepwash Row in a council house he had bought in 1988, with a front garden of gravel and a wooden bench by the door that had been sat on so often the arms were pale.

Clara went on the Saturday, on foot, with a copy of the photograph in an envelope and no plan at all.

He opened the door on the chain, and then off it, and looked at her.

"You'll be the bookshop," he said.

"Clara Vance."

"I know what you're called. Everybody in this town knows what everybody's called by Tuesday." He was seventy seven and built like a man who had done something physical for forty years and had lately begun to fold up, and he had a newspaper folded to the crossword under one arm. "I'm not talking about my daughter."

"That's all right."

"It isn't all right, it's what I've said to nine people since 1996 and two of them were policemen and one of them was a woman off the television." He did not shut the door. "You've come about her, though."

"Yes."

"Everybody does eventually." He looked past her, down the row. "You'd best come in before Mrs Wickham gets her binoculars out."

The front room was clean and too warm and had a gas fire going in October, and there were no photographs anywhere at all, which Clara noticed within four seconds and did not comment on.

"Tea?"

"Please."

He made it in the kitchen and brought it in and sat down opposite her, and there was a silence of perhaps thirty seconds which Clara did not fill, because she had learned by then that in this town filling silences got you nothing.

"Go on then," he said.

"Mr Feltham, I'm not police and I'm not press. I inherited The Midnight Ledger from Edwin Pryor and I never met him and I don't know why he left it to me, and I've spent five weeks going through what's in it, and there's an active police review of your daughter's death that a detective sergeant will tell you about herself, and she'd rather I hadn't come."

"But you came."

"I came because a woman called Nadia Okafor showed you a photograph three weeks ago and asked if that was Ruth, and she didn't show you the back of it, and she didn't show you the child properly, and I think that was the right thing for a police officer to do and I think it was rotten." Clara put the envelope on the table. "That's a copy. It's yours. It came out of the boards of a book in my shop."

Wallace Feltham did not touch it for a while.

Then he took it out, and held it at arm's length, and got a pair of glasses off the mantelpiece and held it closer, and Clara watched a man's face come apart very slowly and put itself back together with a great deal of practice.

"June," he said. "That's the summer she was nineteen."

"Yes."

"That's outside Edwin's door. That's the step." He turned it over and read the pencil and turned it back. "I've not got a picture of her from after she was seventeen. Her mother had them all and her mother went in 2004 and I did a thing I regret, which was that I couldn't be in the house with them."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be, it was my doing." He was still looking at it. "Who's the little one?"

And there it was, in the middle of a hot front room with a gas fire going, and Clara found that she had no technique for it at all.

"I think it might be me," she said.

Wallace Feltham looked up.

He looked at her for a long time, at her face, and Clara made herself sit still and let him, and she watched him go through it: the photograph, and her, and the photograph, and her, and then something behind both of them that had nothing to do with a likeness, because a man does not identify a granddaughter from a face at thirty four years' distance. He was checking something else. She could see him counting.

"Nineteen ninety two," he said.

"March."

"She went to her aunt's at Yeovil in the November of ninety one." He said it without any expression at all. "She was seventeen. She was there till the June. Jean said she'd gone for the college."

"Mr Feltham."

"There wasn't a college at Yeovil," said Wallace Feltham, "and I knew that at the time, and I never said, and Jean never said, and Ruth came back in the June and she was different and I put it down to being eighteen." He set the photograph down on the arm of the chair with enormous care, as though it might go off. "And in nineteen ninety four she is standing on Edwin Pryor's step with a baby on her hip in broad daylight in the middle of this town, and I never saw it. I was on the roads then. I was away four nights in five."

"Would Jean have known?"

"Jean knew," he said. "Jean knew, and Jean is twenty two years dead, and I have just this minute worked out what she meant by a thing she said to me in the hospice, and I would rather I hadn't."

He got up and went to the window and stood with his back to the room.

"You'll want to know about the man," he said.

"Yes."

"There wasn't one. That's what I told the police in ninety six and it was the truth as I had it." His voice had gone hard. "She had somebody from about the spring of that year. Anybody with eyes could see it. She'd stop in the middle of doing a thing and smile at the floor. But she never brought him here and she never named him and when I asked her in the September she said, Dad, I'll tell you at Christmas, and she laughed, and I have had thirty years of that laugh."

"Was she frightened of him?"

Wallace turned round.

"Why do you ask that?"

"Because there's a note in a book in my shop that says she was frightened of him in June and not in October, and that this was the wrong way round."

He came back and sat down heavily.

"Edwin wrote that."

"Yes."

"Then Edwin knew who he was." He said it flatly. "Thirty years. He carried my daughter's coffin, girl. He was in this room after the inquest with his hat in his hands and he sat where you're sitting and he said he was sorry and I said what have you got to be sorry for."

"He never told you anything?"

"He came every year." Wallace looked at his hands. "Every year on the fourth of November, at seven in the evening, for twenty nine years, and he'd bring nothing and say nothing and sit there for half an hour and go. I stopped answering the door in about 2009 and he sat on that bench outside for half an hour instead, and then he'd go, and he did that for the next sixteen years, in all weathers, and I watched him do it out of that window."

Clara could not speak for a moment.

"Last November?"

"Last November he came and he did not sit down," said Wallace. "He stood at that gate and he said one thing through the door and then he went. He said: Wallace, I'm going to be able to put it right and I am sorry it has taken me so long and I am sorry it will be after I'm gone."

The gas fire hissed.

"I thought he was going daft," said Wallace Feltham. "He was eighty. I thought, that's the start of it. And I went in and did the crossword."

Clara ordered her full birth certificate that night from the shop counter at ten past nine, on the government website, with her card, for eleven pounds, which is the amount it costs to find out.

It came on the Thursday in an envelope with a window in it, along with a gas bill addressed to a dead man.

She opened it standing at the counter, in the middle of the shop, with the front door still open behind her.

*Registration district: Yeovil. When and where born: Ninth March 1992, Yeovil District Hospital. Name: Clara Anne. Sex: Girl.*

*Name and surname of mother: Ruth Anne FELTHAM.*

*Occupation of mother: Shop assistant.*

*Name and surname of father:* and then a ruled line, drawn edge to edge, through an empty box.

Clara stood there and read her own mother's name on a piece of paper for the first time in her life, in a shop that her mother had worked in, forty feet from the ground her mother was buried in, and heard, above her head, from the room where six hundred books stood in their runs with a finger of air at the end of each one, a sound like a very long breath going out.

Then the whole upstairs went quiet in a way it had not been quiet in five weeks, and stayed quiet, and Clara understood that it had stopped.
