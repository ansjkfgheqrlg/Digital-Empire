# Lost Hours

She searched the building for four days.

It was not a large building and she was methodical, and by the end of the first day she had established that there were no ledgers in it, which she refused to accept, so she spent three more days establishing it again.

She emptied the back room. She took the bed apart. She lifted the loose board under the stove that turned out to have three 1988 pennies and a mouse nest in it. She went through the cellar, which was four feet high and eleven feet square and held a mangle, and she went through it on her hands and knees with a torch.

She did the top floor, which she had not been into at all. Two attic rooms with sloping ceilings, one full of empty cardboard boxes flattened and stacked, the other with a bedstead and no mattress and a wardrobe containing seven identical grey cardigans on wire hangers. She looked at the cardigans for rather longer than the search required.

She did the shop, run by run, taking every book off every shelf, which took two days and which she had already done once looking for pencil.

She did the upstairs front room the same way and found nothing but what she had already logged.

And on the Thursday evening she stood in the middle of the shop floor at half past six with dust in her hair and both hands filthy and understood that the ten ledgers were not in this building and had not been in it for some time.

She rang Okafor and told her.

"Ten of them," Okafor said. "Physically, what are we looking for?"

"Green cloth over board, quarter leather, about fourteen inches by nine by two, three pounds each. Ten of them together is a box you can just about lift."

"So not something you post."

"No."

"And not something you burn quickly either," said Okafor. "Paper that size is a nuisance to destroy, which I know because people try. Right. I'll ask the question you're not asking. If he wanted them kept, they're with somebody, the way volume eleven was with Miss Raval. If he wanted them gone, they went in a skip in about 2011 and we are finished."

"He gave one away in 2024 and told her to hand it to whoever came." Clara wiped her forehead with her wrist and made it worse. "He wasn't destroying it. That's the one thing I'd stake anything on. He built a machine that needs two halves and he only gave one half away and he did that fourteen months before he died."

"Then he thought the other half was safe."

"Yes."

"Which means he thought it was somewhere nobody would look," said Okafor, "and, Miss Vance, you have now looked everywhere. Come at it tomorrow. You've been in that building for three weeks and you sound like somebody who has been in a building for three weeks."

Clara said something dismissive and put the phone down and went back up to the front room, because there was a thing she had not tried, which was reading the four hundred and two notes again in the light of the ledger she did have.

That was at about seven.

She was sitting on the floor with her back against the third run and the printout across her knees, and she had got as far as the notes in the second hundred, and she was reading a fragment that said *he took the lamp glass out and put it back and I did not think anything of it at the time*, and she was aware that the room had got dark and that she should put the light on and that in a minute she would.

Then she was standing in the churchyard.

It was not like waking. That was the part she could never get anybody to understand afterwards, including herself. Waking has an edge to it. This had no edge at all: she was reading, and then she was standing on wet grass forty yards inside the gate of St Botolph's with her hands at her sides, and the two states were joined without a seam, the way two shots are joined in a film.

She was in her jumper and her jeans and she had no coat and no shoes and no socks.

Her feet were entirely numb. That was the first fact she got hold of, and she got hold of it because it meant time had passed, and she needed to know how much, and she stood absolutely still and made herself work it out from the ground up like a problem instead of looking at her phone, because she did not have her phone.

The grass was frozen. It had not been frozen at seven.

The moon was over the church tower and it had been behind the limes when she had come up on the Saturday at about this angle, which meant nothing, because that was three weeks ago.

She turned round.

There was a light on in the upstairs front room of The Midnight Ledger, and the middle blind was up, and from where she stood she could see the ceiling of that room and the top eighteen inches of the third run of shelves, and nothing else, because the window was above her eye line.

And she was standing at the foot of Ruth Feltham's grave.

Not near it. At it, square on, at the foot of the plot, in the position a person takes when they have come to speak to somebody, with the toes of both bare feet against the edge of the chippings.

Clara made a sound she was not proud of, and got out of there.

She went down the path fast and hit the gate with her hip and got the shop door open and shut it behind her and put her back against it, and stood in her own dark hallway breathing, and after a while she put the light on and looked at the clock over the counter.

Twenty past four in the morning.

Nine hours and twenty minutes.

She sat down on the floor of the hallway with her feet in her hands and cried for a bit, out of shock and cold and because her feet, coming back, hurt very badly, and then she stopped, and got up, and put the kettle on, and did the only useful thing available, which was to write it down.

She wrote it down at twenty five to five in the morning with her handwriting all over the place, and she wrote it the way Okafor had taught her, observation only, no interpretation, times and positions and physical states.

*Last memory: seated, third run, printout, reading note 187. Light not yet on. Estimated 19.00, no clock seen.*

*Next memory: standing, churchyard, at foot of grave, square to it. Barefoot, no coat. Feet numb, grass frozen. Blind up in upstairs front, light on. 04.20 on return.*

*Missing: 9 hrs 20 min approx.*

*Note: I do not raise blinds. I have raised that blind once, on the 24th, and lowered it the same night. It was down at 19.00 today, I am certain, because I remember thinking the room had got dark and that I should put the light on.*

She stopped writing.

Then she got up and went upstairs, holding the banister, and stood in the doorway of the front room.

The light was on. The middle blind was up.

The printout was on the floor by the third run where she had been sitting, with two pages folded back.

And on the floorboards, laid out in the clear space in the middle of the room between her sitting place and the middle window, were nine books, arranged end to end in a line pointing at the glass, each one open, each one face up.

Nine, not eleven.

Clara stood in the doorway for a long moment. Then she went and got her phone off the counter and came back and photographed the line from the door, from both corners and from above, and only then did she crouch at the first book and read what was in the margin.

She did not need to read the second one, or any of the rest, because the first one told her where the ten ledgers were, and it told her in six words, and she should have worked it out for herself eleven days ago.

*a book is a thing with boards*
