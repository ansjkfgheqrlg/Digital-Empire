# The System

She finished the room on the following Tuesday, at ten past one in the morning, and sat down on the floor with her back against the oak and looked at the last book in her hands for a while before she wrote it up.

Six hundred and eleven volumes. Four hundred and two notes.

Two hundred and nine books with nothing in them at all, which had taken her four days to accept as a fact rather than a failure of searching, because she had gone back over eighty of them a second time and found nothing the second time either.

Every note was in pencil, in the same hand. Every note was on a left hand page. There was never more than one to a book.

Clara stood up, put the last book back with a finger of air at the end of the run, turned off the light, and went downstairs and slept for nine hours, which she had not done in eleven days.

Then she started on the order.

She had a spreadsheet with four hundred and two rows and eleven columns, and photographs of every one, and she had DS Okafor's rule written on a card and stuck to the wall above the counter: *observation and interpretation never in the same file.* So she opened a second document and called it ORDER, and at the top of it she wrote the only thing she was sure of.

*He built this so that it survived him and so that somebody could put it back together. Therefore the order exists and it is findable. Therefore I have not found it yet, which is a different thing.*

She tried alphabetical by author. The notes made no sense in that order, and she had not expected them to, but you start with the cheap tests.

She tried alphabetical by title. Nothing.

She tried publication date, which took a day because a hundred and forty of the books had no date and she had to establish them from printing codes, and which produced a sequence that ran from 1902 to 1994 and read like a bag of loose sentences shaken out on a table.

She tried page number. Four hundred and two notes on pages ranging from four to six hundred and one, in an order that put *she came in at ten past and she was not alone* between a note about a bus fare and a note that said only *no*.

She tried shelf position, which she had recorded obsessively: run, shelf, position from left. This one she believed in for most of a day, because it is the obvious system, because it is how a man standing in a room would naturally lay a thing out, left to right and top to bottom. She built it and read it through and it was so nearly coherent in three places that she went back and rebuilt it twice, and it was not coherent, and by the third pass she understood that she was doing the thing she had spent eleven years warning students about, which is finding a pattern by wanting one.

She tried the position of the note on the page. She tried the grade of the pencil, sorting silver oxidised graphite from black, which gave her a rough chronology of writing and not of events. She tried mapping every note that contained a time onto a clock and every note that contained a person onto that clock, and got a night with four people in it and nine gaps.

On the Friday, Amory rang.

"It's the twenty fourth," he said pleasantly. "I said nine days and I meant it and I'm not going to pretend the phone call is about anything else."

Clara stood at the counter with the ORDER document open in front of her and four hundred and two rows of somebody else's mind behind her eyes.

"No," she said. "Thank you. Genuinely, thank you, it was a fair offer and it was over the odds and I'm not being clever with you. I'm not selling."

There was a pause on the line of exactly the length of a man recalculating.

"May I ask what you're going to do with it?"

"I don't know yet."

"Miss Vance." Still perfectly pleasant. "You've got no stock worth anything, no trade, no local goodwill because nobody in this town has bought a book on that lane since about 2011, and a building that will need a roof inside five years. I'm not saying that to frighten you. I'm saying it because in eight months you will be trying to sell it and I will not be buying, and I would rather you heard that now."

"I understand."

"Do you." He said it lightly. "You know, I've been in this trade since I was nineteen, and the thing nobody tells you is that a shop like that isn't a business, it's a room where somebody kept themselves. Edwin kept himself in there for thirty years and it did him no good at all, and I watched it happen and I did not do anything about it, and I have thought about that a good deal since March." A breath. "Don't get kept in there. That's the whole of my advice and it's free."

He rang off.

Clara stood with the phone in her hand and found, to her considerable irritation, that she was moved.

That night she did the thing she had been avoiding, which was to sit on the floor of the upstairs room with the printout of all four hundred and two notes in her lap and read them straight through, in the arbitrary order in which she had found them, from beginning to end, out loud.

It took two hours and ten minutes.

It was the worst thing she had ever read.

Not because of what was in it. Because of what it was. Four hundred and two fragments of a man talking to nobody for thirty years, the same six or seven facts turned over and over, the same night approached from a hundred directions, the same three or four questions asked and asked and asked.

*she did not have a coat with her*

*I have gone up the top road again and it is 22 minutes*

*I have gone up the top road again and it is 22 minutes and I am 61 now and it would have been 19 then*

*if she went out at ten to and he came at ten past then she waited twenty minutes in the cold*

*she waited twenty minutes*

*why did she wait*

*because I told her he would come*

Clara stopped reading.

She read the last one again, and then she went through her spreadsheet to find where it had come from, and it had come from page 219 of a book about the ferns of the British Isles, on the second shelf of the third run, and there was nothing near it and nothing after it.

*because I told her he would come.*

She put the printout down and sat with her hands over her mouth for a while.

Then she got up, and went to the middle window, and rolled the blind up for the first time since she had come into that room.

The lamp on the corner of the building lit the gate and the first ten feet of the path. Beyond that the churchyard went up into the dark, and the yew was a black shape against a sky the colour of dishwater, and beyond the yew, exactly where she knew it was, invisible now, was the stone.

"I can't do it," Clara said. "I've tried nine ways and it isn't any of them, and I'm going to get it wrong and make a story out of it, and then a barrister stands up and it's finished, and she stays an accident for ever." Her voice was not steady. "You have to tell me the order. I can't get there on my own. I'm not being lazy, I'm telling you the truth, I have run out."

The room was silent.

The blind hung up. The books stood in their runs with their finger of air.

Nothing moved and nothing fell and nothing turned over, and after four or five minutes Clara laughed at herself, wetly, and wiped her face with the back of her wrist, and said, "Fine," and went to pull the blind down.

Her hand stopped an inch from the cord.

There was a light on in Priya's coffee shop, two doors down, at twenty to midnight on a Friday, and Priya Raval was sitting alone at the table in the window with a cup in front of her, not drinking it, looking out at the lane.

She had been there, Clara realised, for some time.

And on the table in front of her, squared up in front of her folded hands, was a book. Not a paperback. A tall narrow ledger bound in dark cloth, the shape and proportion of every shop account book made in England between 1880 and 1970, and Clara had handled two hundred of them and knew the shape of one at forty feet through two panes of glass in the dark.

Priya looked up at the lit window of the upstairs front room.

She saw Clara.

She did not wave, and she did not look away, and she sat quite still with her hands on the cover of the ledger, and after a moment she nodded once, the way people do when the thing they have been putting off has finally arrived and there is a kind of relief in it.
