# Eighteen Months of Budget

They went down and Clara put the kettle on because it was the only thing she could think of to do with her hands.

"Say it," she said.

"I'm deciding how much to say."

"Then decide out loud, because I've had five days of people in this town deciding how much to say and I'm at the end of it."

Okafor almost smiled.

"Edwin Pryor changed his will on the eleventh of February," she said. "Nine days after the case review was opened, and five weeks before he died. Before that, everything he had went to the Book Trade Benevolent Society, and had done since 2003. On the eleventh of February he instructed a solicitor in Dorchester to leave the freehold and contents of this building to a woman in Bristol he had never, as far as anybody can establish, met, spoken to, or written to in his life."

The kettle came up loud and Clara turned it off at the wall.

"How would he even know I exist?"

"That is a very good question and I do not have the answer to it, and it is the reason I did not phone you," said Okafor. "It is also the reason a defence barrister would enjoy you. Now sit down, because I'm going to tell you what I've actually got, and it will take four minutes, and afterwards you'll understand my position better than you do now."

Clara sat.

"February 2026. Dorset opened a cold case review unit, because every force is doing one, because forensic recovery has moved on and because it looks good in the annual report. We were given eighteen months, two officers and a civilian analyst two days a week, and a list of forty one unresolved deaths going back to 1971. My job is to look at all forty one and pick the ones where modern work could realistically produce a charge."

"And Ruth Feltham was on the list."

"Ruth Feltham was not on the list," said Okafor. "The list is unsolved homicides and open verdicts. Ruth Feltham is a recorded accident with a coroner's finding, and no accident is on anybody's list, ever, or the list would be nine thousand names long."

"Then why?"

"Because I read the summaries of everything from that division for that decade as background, and there is a file on the Feltham death, and it is four pages long." Okafor held up four fingers. "Four. A twenty two year old woman is found dead at seven in the morning in a churchyard in a town of four hundred people, and the file is four pages. I have a road traffic fatality from the same division from the same month and it runs to sixty one."

"That could be a filing failure."

"It could be, and that was my first assumption, and I spent six weeks on it. There is no second file. There is no property book entry. There is no scene log. There are two photographs and both are of the path, neither of the body. There is a pathologist's summary of eleven lines with a cause of death and no report attached, and the report is not at the hospital either, and I have asked twice."

She let that sit.

"Now. None of that is evidence of anything except a bad week in an under resourced division in 1996, and I want to be extremely clear about that, because it would be very easy for you to hear what I've just said as *the police covered it up*, and I do not think that, and I have no reason to think it."

"But you drove here."

"But I drove here," Okafor agreed, "three times before today, and sat in the car on the square, and did not come in, because I have got no new evidence, no witness, no forensic opportunity and no exhibits, and I cannot spend eighteen months and a quarter of a million pounds of public money on a case that a coroner closed in January 1997 because a detective sergeant has a feeling about a page count."

Clara said, "How long have you got?"

"The unit closes on the thirty first of March. There is no continuation funding, I have asked, twice, in writing." Okafor's voice did not change at all, which was somehow worse. "On the first of April the forty one files go back in the archive and the Feltham file goes back to being a recorded accident, and the only person who has looked at it seriously in thirty years will be doing something else. That is not a sad story, it's a timetable, and it is the reason I am sitting in a bookshop having a conversation I ought not to be having."

The shop was quiet.

"So," Clara said. "You need me."

"I need what's in that room and I need it to be worth something, and those are different problems." Okafor leaned forward. "Let's do the second one first. What would make it worth something is if the notes contain a fact that was never public. Not an opinion. Not *he was not in Dorchester*. A fact: a time, a place, an object, a thing that only somebody who was there could know, which we can then check against something independent. That is the entire test, and everything else in this building is decoration."

"There are three hundred and nine of them."

"Then one of them might be a fact." Okafor stood and buttoned her coat. "Now the first problem, which is you. I can't stop you working in your own house. I could seize the room, and I have thought about it every minute since I walked in, and I'm not going to, because you're right that I'd get six hundred books and no key, and because if I seize it and it turns out to be nothing I have spent a fifth of my remaining time on a bookshop."

"So?"

"So we do it properly, which is slower and duller and which you will not enjoy." She counted it off. "Everything you have already touched, you write up now: what you did, in what order, and every photograph gets exported with its original timestamps and hashed and given to me on a disc, today. From now on, you photograph the book closed, then the spine, then the open page, then the note, in that order, every time. You do not remove a note from a book. You do not clean anything. You do not order anything. Where you have an interpretation, you write it in a separate document with your name on it and you never, ever write it in the same file as the observation."

"That's how I catalogue a collection anyway."

"Good. Then you'll be quick." Okafor paused at the door. "And one more. I am going to arrange for you to be fingerprinted and DNA sampled for elimination, voluntarily, this week, and you are going to say yes, because the day this becomes a case is the day somebody asks why the woman who found it all never was."

"And the room? At night?"

Okafor turned round.

Clara had not planned to say it. It had come out on its own, and now it was in the air, and she stood in her own shop and made herself go through with it.

"There's something else I have to tell you and you're going to like it a great deal less than the notes. I have not touched three of the books I've logged. They were on the floor when I came down in the morning, in an arc, open, and there were no gaps in the shelves they should have come from, and there were no marks in the dust, and there are seven months of dust on that floor and the only footprints in it are mine." She kept her voice flat, which took effort. "The same thing happened in March, the morning Edwin was found. Priya Raval saw it. Eleven books in a line, pointing at the middle window, and from the middle window you can see Ruth Feltham's grave and nothing else."

She waited.

DS Okafor stood with her hand on the door for what felt like a very long time.

"Right," she said at last.

"Right?"

"You told me. You could have not told me, and you'd have been a great deal more use to me tomorrow, and you told me anyway." She opened the door. "I have been doing this for eighteen years, Miss Vance, and I have had two people in that time volunteer a thing that made them look mad when they didn't have to. Both of them were telling the truth about everything else."

"And the books?"

"I don't believe in ghosts and I am not going to start," said Nadia Okafor. "But I have written *no innocent explanation identified* in a great many files, and it has never once meant *stop looking*. Photograph it. Timestamp it. And Miss Vance, get a lock for the front door that isn't two hundred years old, because if it is not a ghost then somebody has a key to this building, and that is worse."
