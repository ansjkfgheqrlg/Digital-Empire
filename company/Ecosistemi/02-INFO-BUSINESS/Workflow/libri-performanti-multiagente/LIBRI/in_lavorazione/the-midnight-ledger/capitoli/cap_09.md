# The Policewoman

On Monday morning Clara did three things before nine o'clock.

She sent an email to the Dorset coroner's office requesting the inquest record for Ruth Anne Feltham, died November 1996, giving her name and her address as the proprietor of the premises at which the deceased had been employed, which was true and which she was aware was doing a certain amount of work.

She sent a second to Dorset Police at Winfrith, asking whether any file relating to the death remained open and how a member of the public might view it.

And she rang Miss Deare at the solicitors in Dorchester and asked whether the firm had acted for Edwin Pryor in anything other than his will, and Miss Deare said that she was not able to discuss the affairs of a client with a third party, and Clara said that she was not a third party, she was the sole beneficiary and the executor's principal, and there was a pause of about four seconds and then Miss Deare said that she would look, in a voice that suggested she had been waiting six months for somebody to ask her something intelligent.

Then Tom Sayers rang and cancelled the vestry.

"I am so sorry. Genuinely. Something's come up with the diocese and I've got to be in Salisbury and I'll be there all week."

"Next Monday?"

"Let me look at it and come back to you," said the vicar, and Clara, who had spent the whole of Sunday learning to hear the shape of a sentence, heard a man who was not going to come back to her.

She said thank you and put the phone down and wrote on her pad: *Sayers, cancelled, Mon 20th. He offered on Sat without being asked.*

On Tuesday the coroner's office replied with a form and a fee of fifteen pounds and a note saying that inquest records over thirty years old had been transferred to the county archive and that the file for that period was, and here somebody had typed a sentence and then partly deleted it, *not currently available for production.*

On Wednesday, at twenty past ten, a woman walked into the shop and did not look at a single book.

Clara noticed that first, before the coat or the face or anything else. Every human being who had come through that door since Thursday had looked at the books, because that is what a room made of books does to a person. This woman came in, glanced once at the ceiling, once at the back curtain, once at the staircase, and then put her attention on Clara and left it there.

"Clara Vance?"

"Yes."

"Detective Sergeant Nadia Okafor. Major crime review team, Dorset." She held up a warrant card at a distance where it could actually be read, and waited until Clara had actually read it, which Clara appreciated more than she expected to. "I'd like fifteen minutes."

"Would you like to sit down?"

"There are books on both chairs."

"There are books on everything. Give me a second."

Clara moved a stack of Reader's Digest condensed novels off the nearer chair, and DS Okafor sat in it with the air of a woman who has sat in worse, and did not take out a notebook, which Clara also noticed.

She was in her early forties, in a dark coat over a suit, with her hair scraped back hard and a face that gave away exactly as much as she wanted it to and had clearly had a great deal of practice.

"On Monday morning you emailed two organisations about the death of Ruth Feltham," Okafor said. "The coroner's office and my force. Would you like to tell me why?"

"Am I in trouble?"

"That depends entirely on what you say next, and I'd rather you knew that going in than found it out afterwards." Okafor's tone was level. "I'll go first, because it's quicker. The death of Ruth Feltham is the subject of a live case review. It has been since February. That is not public and I would prefer it stayed that way, and I am telling you because within about four days you were going to find out anyway and I would rather it came from me than from somebody in the queue at the Co-op."

Clara sat down on the arm of the other chair.

"February," she said. "Edwin Pryor died in March."

"Yes."

"Did he know? That it was reopened?"

"I have no idea," said Okafor, "and I would very much like to, and that is one of the several reasons I am sitting in this shop." She let that stand for a moment. "Your turn. Why did you write those emails?"

Clara had spent two days deciding what she would say if this happened, and every version had involved starting with the paranormal, and she had not been able to make any of them sound like anything but a woman who has been alone in a house too long.

So she started with the paper.

She got up and fetched the four local history books and laid them open on the floor between them, in order, and let DS Okafor read four pencil notes in a small square hand.

*4.xi.96, 22.50, R.F. leaves shop.*

*4.xi.96, 23.10, G.A.*

*4.xi.96, 23.32, G.A. returns, alone.*

*I did not write any of this down at the time. I have written it since from what I remember and I have not changed it after. E.P.*

Okafor read them twice. Then she sat back, and for the first time since she had come through the door her attention went somewhere else entirely, and Clara watched a woman doing arithmetic she had done many times before and did not enjoy.

"Where were these," she said.

"On the shelves. Local history, ground floor, second run, spread across six hundred and forty books. I found four in two hours and ten minutes and I was looking. Nobody who wasn't looking would ever find one."

"And upstairs?"

Clara's head came up.

"Miss Vance, there is a room on the first floor of this building that Edwin Pryor would not sell out of and would not let anybody into, and that has been common knowledge in this town for thirty years, and I have been working on this case since February, so please do not look at me as though I have read your mind." Okafor stood up. "Show me."

They went up. Clara went first and turned the lights on and stood aside, and DS Nadia Okafor walked into the front room and stopped two paces in, exactly where Clara had stopped, and stood there for a good while.

"How many?"

"Six hundred and eleven. I've counted twice."

"And they've all got writing in them."

"I don't know yet. I've opened four hundred and eighty three and found notes in three hundred and nine, and I'm doing about a hundred an hour when I do it properly, which means photograph, log, page number, position, replace." Clara nodded at the shelves. "It'll take me another week to finish the room and then I'll have three hundred and something fragments of something and no idea what order they go in."

Okafor turned round slowly, taking in the oak, the blinds, the finger of air at the end of each run.

"Right," she said. "Now I'm going to say the unpleasant part and I'd like you to hear all of it before you react.

"What is in this room is, potentially, evidence in an unresolved death. It is also, potentially, six hundred books with nothing in them but the ramblings of a lonely man who got old in a strange way, and I have seen more of the second than the first in eighteen years. I can't tell which it is yet and neither can you.

"But here is what I can tell you. If this ever gets in front of a jury, the first thing a defence barrister will do is stand up and establish that for nine days before the police saw any of it, a private individual with no training, who is not independent, who has a financial interest in this building, and who has an unexplained personal connection to the deceased's employer, was in this room on her own, opening and closing and rearranging and photographing. And the second thing he will do is ask you whether you can prove you did not write any of it yourself."

Clara felt that land in the pit of her stomach.

"I could have," she said slowly. "Couldn't I."

"You could have written all three hundred and nine of them in a fortnight with a soft pencil, and you're a book conservator, so you'd know exactly how to make the graphite look old." Okafor said it without any apology at all. "I looked you up on Monday afternoon. Eleven years. Two years at the Bodleian on a contract. You are, professionally, the single least credible person in England to have found this."

The room was quiet. Down in the lane a car went past.

"So what do you want me to do?" Clara said.

"I want you to stop."

"No."

"Miss Vance."

"No," said Clara, "and I'll tell you exactly why, and then you can decide what to do about it. Because Edwin Pryor spent thirty years hiding this so well that a police force with a warrant would take a month to find it, and the only reason anybody knows it exists is that I opened four hundred books in three days for no reason a sane person would recognise. If you seal this room, you get six hundred books and no key. I'm the only person alive who has any chance of working out what order they go in."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment.

"You said an unexplained personal connection," Clara added. "To Edwin. What did you mean?"

And DS Okafor, who had been in control of every second of the conversation up to that point, said, "You don't know," and it was not a question, and something in her face had changed.
