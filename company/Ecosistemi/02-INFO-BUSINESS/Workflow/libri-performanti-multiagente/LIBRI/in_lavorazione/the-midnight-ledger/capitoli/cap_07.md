# The Churchyard

The switch was where Hettie said it was.

Clara found it under the coat pegs inside the front door, a round brown Bakelite dolly switch on a wooden pattress, wired in cotton covered flex that ran up the wall and out through the frame above the door and along the front of the building to the bracket on the corner. She had walked past it eleven times and taken it for a dead light for a stockroom.

She put her thumb on it and turned it down and up again, and it made a fat mechanical click, and out in the grey afternoon nothing visible happened, because the lamp over the churchyard gate was already alight.

Clara stood very still.

She went outside into the lane and looked up at it. A cast iron bracket, a glass lantern with four panes and one of them cracked and mended with tape gone brittle, and inside it a bulb burning steadily in broad daylight at half past two on a Saturday afternoon.

She went back in and turned the switch the other way, decisively, and went out.

It was still on.

She did that three more times, up, down, up, listening to the click each time and looking at the lamp each time, and on the fourth she stood in the doorway with her hand on the switch and looked along the wall at the flex, which ran where it had always run, into a lantern that had no other supply and had never had one, on a bracket bolted to her building.

"All right," she said.

Then she went and got her coat and crossed the lane and went into the churchyard, because it seemed to her, standing there, that being frightened of a thing at a distance of forty feet was a poor use of an afternoon.

St Botolph's sat above the lane on a shelf of ground that the centuries had raised, so that the churchyard wall on the lane side was four feet high on the outside and eighteen inches on the inside. The gate was iron, and it did not squeal, which surprised her. The path went in under a yew of enormous age, forked, and ran up to a porch on the south side.

It was not a frightening place. That was the first thing. Clara had prepared herself for something and got instead a large quiet garden in October: wet grass mown in strips, a bank of leaves against the north wall, four rooks arguing in the limes, and two hundred and some headstones going back to a slab by the porch so worn it might have said anything.

She walked. She read stones, because she always read stones, because they were documents and she could not help it.

The old ones were up by the church and half sunk. The Victorian ones ran in ranks down the middle. The twentieth century occupied the eastern third, where the ground was flatter, and there she found the granite and the black polished stone and the small tidy plots with chippings, and a bin, and a tap on a post.

She had gone perhaps ten yards into that section when her feet stopped.

That was how it happened, and she was precise about it afterwards, because being precise was the only tool she had. She did not see a stone and stop. She was walking, and her feet stopped, and then, a distinct moment later, her eyes went where her feet had already taken her.

RUTH ANNE FELTHAM
1974 to 1996
DAUGHTER OF WALLACE AND JEAN
*the light is on*

Clara read the last line four times.

It was cut smaller than the rest and set in italic and it sat alone at the foot of the stone, and it was not a verse, and it was not from anything. She got out her phone and photographed it and stood there with her thumbs going and could not think what to search for, and put the phone away.

The stone was clean.

She noticed it because she had spent twenty minutes reading a churchyard and had got her eye in. The stones around it had thirty years of lichen on the north face and green in the lettering, and this one had none. The chippings in the plot were even and free of leaves. There was a jam jar sunk into the grass at the head with an inch of water in it and no flowers, and the water was clear.

Somebody had been coming here for thirty years, and had stopped coming some time in the last seven months.

Clara sat down on the flat kerb of the tomb behind her, and after a while she realised she was crying, and this she could not explain at all.

It was not the grief of hearing a sad story about a stranger, and she knew the difference, because she had cried at strangers before. This came up through her from the ground. It arrived complete, without a thought attached, the way a smell arrives, and it was so specifically and physically like homesickness that Clara sat on a stranger's tomb in a churchyard in Dorset with her hands flat on the cold stone and said, out loud, "I have never been here in my life."

"You'd be surprised how often people say that."

She got up much too fast.

There was a man on the path in a waxed jacket over a clerical collar, holding a bucket and a pair of shears, and he had the decency to look mortified.

"I am so sorry. That was a dreadful thing to say and I said it because I say it about twice a year and it's usually charming." He put the bucket down. "Tom Sayers. I'm the vicar, God help this parish. And you're at the Ledger, which I know because there are four hundred people in this town and I have already been told twice today."

"Clara Vance."

"Would you like me to go away? Genuinely. Some people want a vicar and some people very much do not."

"Do you know whose grave this is?"

Tom Sayers looked at the stone, and his face did something quick and complicated.

"Ruth Feltham," he said. "Yes."

"Were you here?"

"In 1996 I was thirteen and living in Nottingham. I've been here five years." He came and stood a proper distance away with his hands in his pockets. "I know the grave the way you know a grave in your own churchyard. It's the one people stop at. Fourteen visitors a year, easily, and half of them from away, because a young woman dying in a churchyard is a story and stories travel."

"Who cuts the grass on it?"

"Sorry?"

"The plot's clean and everything round it isn't, and the water in the jar's fresh, or it was recently." Clara pointed. "That stone has no lichen on it and it's been there thirty years. Somebody's been scrubbing it. Who?"

The vicar looked at the stone properly, and then at the ones on either side of it, and Clara watched him see it.

"Edwin Pryor," he said slowly. "Not that I ever saw him do it. But he had a key to the tap house and the mower shed, from before my time, and I inherited a list from my predecessor of people who had keys and I never took his back because he was eighty and it seemed unkind." He rubbed his jaw. "I've not been up here on a weekday morning since about March. I'd assumed the volunteers."

"There's an inscription at the bottom. *The light is on.* Is that from something?"

"No," said Tom Sayers. "And I have wondered."

"Would the parish have a record? Of who ordered the stone, what was cut on it?"

"The parish has a record of everything," said the vicar, "which is the joy and the curse of the Church of England," and then he stopped, and something went out of his face, and Clara saw it go.

"Except?"

"Except nothing." He picked up the bucket. "The burial register would have the interment. The faculty for the memorial would be in the chest in the vestry with the rest of it. You'd be very welcome to look, though I'd want to be there, because it's a chest with about nine hundred years in it and no catalogue."

"When?"

"Monday. I've got two services tomorrow and a funeral visit tonight." He was already turning. "Miss Vance, may I say one thing that isn't my business. That house you've inherited looks at this churchyard out of every window on its front and there is no other building in Fenn Abbas that does, and it was built that way on purpose, because in 1740 you paid extra for a view of consecrated ground. It was thought to be a comfort."

He went up the path towards the porch.

Clara stayed where she was until the light started going, and then she walked back down to the gate, and stopped under the lamp on the corner of her own building.

It was on. It had been on all day.

She went inside and up the stairs to the front room and stood at the middle window, and from there, in the last of the afternoon, she could see straight down the churchyard path, past the fork, to the yew, and beyond the yew, forty five yards away and lit up like a small pale door in the grass, the stone that said RUTH ANNE FELTHAM.

The middle window. The window the eleven books had been pointing at.
