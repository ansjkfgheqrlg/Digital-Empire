# The Thing Priya Kept

The coffee shop was closed and the chairs were up on four of the six tables and Priya let her in without saying anything and locked the door behind her.

"Sit down."

"Priya."

"Sit down, because I've been sitting here since half nine working out how to say it and if you talk first I'll lose it."

Clara sat.

Priya put both hands flat on the ledger.

"I have had this for two years and one month," she said. "Edwin gave it to me in September 2024. He came in here on a Wednesday afternoon with it under his coat and he put it on that table and he said, Priya, I'd like you to keep this for me and not open it and not tell anybody you've got it, and if I die I'd like you to give it to whoever comes."

"Whoever comes."

"His words. Not *my family*. Not *the solicitor*. Whoever comes." Priya's jaw was tight. "And I said, Edwin, that is the single most sinister thing anybody has ever said to me, and he laughed, which he did about twice a year, and he said, no, it's the opposite, and then he went home."

"And you kept it."

"I put it in the flour store in a cake tin and I left it there for two years, and I did not open it, and then he died and *whoever comes* did not come, because nobody came at all, because the shop was just shut. For seven months." She looked up. "And then you turned up on a Thursday with a holdall and said you were selling."

"So you didn't give it to me."

"So I did not give it to you," Priya said, "and I would like you to understand that I have not slept properly since, and I'd also like you to understand why, and then you can be as angry as you want.

"He said give it to whoever comes. He did not say give it to whoever comes and is going to hand the whole building to Gerald Amory to clear inside a fortnight. And on that first Friday you told me Amory had offered you two hundred and forty thousand and that the stock was to be cleared, all of it, and I sat in here that night and I thought, if I hand her this she puts it in a box with everything else and it goes off in Gerald's van and that is the end of it for ever."

Clara found she could not be angry at all, which was annoying, because she had been building up to it for the length of the lane.

"And tonight?"

"Tonight I sat here and looked at your window for two hours." Priya turned the ledger round and pushed it across the table. "That light's been on till one in the morning every night for a fortnight. Same as him. Exactly the same as him. And I thought, she's not selling, and then I thought, she's not sleeping either, and then I thought about Edwin doing that for thirty years on his own." She sat back. "You asked me on the first day what happened here in 1996 and I said I didn't know, and that was true, and it was also the last honest thing I said to you. I'm sorry. It's yours. It was always yours."

Clara put her hand on the cover and did not open it.

"Have you looked?"

"Last Tuesday. For about four minutes and then I shut it because I felt sick." Priya got up and went behind the counter and started making something, with her back turned, which Clara understood was a kindness. "It's just a shop book. That's what makes it horrible."

"Say what you mean."

"I opened it expecting a diary." The milk started up, loud, and Priya let it run. "I don't know what I thought was going to be in there. A confession. Something. And it's *two paperbacks three pounds*, and *nil pm*, and *nil pm*, and *nil pm*, page after page, in beautiful handwriting, and the pages don't stop." She switched the machine off. "There's a stretch in about 2017 where he took nothing at all for nine days running and he wrote nil pm every single evening anyway. He sat down at that counter nine nights in a row to write down that nothing had happened."

"That's the discipline of it."

"That's what I couldn't handle," said Priya. "I don't know what he was doing. But whatever it was, he did it on the ninth night."

Clara opened it.

It was a standard trade ledger, three hundred leaves, printed feint with a date column, a description column, and three money columns, bound in dark green cloth over boards with a red leather label. Edwin Pryor had ruled a fourth column by hand down the right hand side of every single page, in ink, with a straight edge, three hundred leaves of it, and headed it with nothing at all.

The entries began on the eleventh of January 2011 and ran to the fourth of March 2026, which was, Clara worked out later, the day before he died.

*11.i.11 . . 2 x paperback fict. . . 3.00 . . Mrs D. Hallam*

*11.i.11 . . county hist., 4 vols . . 15.00 . . the man from Yeovil*

*11.i.11 . . nil pm*

Every day. Fifteen years. A shop that took under a hundred pounds a week, recorded to the penny, in a hand that never once got tired.

And in the hand ruled column on the right, on perhaps one page in nine, a number.

Nothing else. Just a number, in pencil, set level with one of the day's lines. 114. 47. 302. 88.

Clara turned pages for a long time with her heart going.

"Priya."

"Mm."

"Come and look at this."

Priya came and stood behind her with a tea towel in her hands.

"What am I looking at?"

"The right hand column. He ruled it himself. There's a number against about one entry in nine, and it's in pencil and everything else is in ink." Clara went back four pages. "And here. Fourteenth of March 2013. The entry is *1 x Dorset churches, 2.50, G.A.* and the number in the margin is 53."

She reached into her bag and took out the printout of four hundred and two notes and turned to the top of it, and put her finger on the second line.

*The Parish Churches of North Dorset, Blandford 1971, page 53: 4.xi.96, 23.10, G.A.*

Priya sat down very slowly on the chair opposite.

"It's an index," Clara said. Her voice had gone strange. "It's not a shop book with a secret in it. It's a shop book *and* an index, and they're the same lines. He'd write a note in a book, and then that night after he closed he'd write the day's takings in here like a normal man, and against the line for the day he'd put the page number he'd used. And the book is whatever he happened to sell or handle or think about that day, which is in the *description* column, in ink, in a shop ledger, where nobody on earth would ever look twice."

"Why like that? Why not a list?"

"Because a list is one object." Clara was turning pages fast now. "Burn the list and it's gone. Steal the list and it's gone. He built it so that to destroy it you would have to destroy a shop ledger *and* six hundred books, and to read it you would have to have both at once and know that they went together. And he gave the ledger to a woman two doors down and left the books in a room everybody in this town knew he would not let them into." She looked up. "It's the cleverest thing I have ever seen anybody do with paper, and he did it alone, and it took him thirty years."

"So you can read it."

"Yes."

"Then read it," said Priya. "Now. Here. I'll make more coffee, I don't care what time it is."

And Clara found the first ruled entry in the book, on the twenty second of January 2011, and matched the number to her spreadsheet, and wrote the note out on a napkin, and then the second, and the third, and by the tenth she had stopped writing and was sitting with the ledger open in front of her and the printout beside it and her hand over her mouth.

"What?" said Priya. "What."

"The dates."

"What about the dates?"

"The entries in this book start in January 2011." Clara turned to the front board and back again as though the pages might have been hiding somewhere. "It's volume eleven. It says so. It's stamped on the label, XI, and I read it as a decoration for twenty minutes." She looked up, and the whole shape of the night had gone out from under her. "This is the eleventh ledger. He started the shop in 1994, and this one took him fifteen years to fill because the trade died. The first ten went in seventeen. That's what a busy shop looks like from the inside: ten fat books and then one thin life. There are ten more of these somewhere, and the one I want is number one.

"Priya, everything he wrote about that night, all of it, every note in that room from the first thirty pages of my spreadsheet, is indexed in a book I haven't got."
